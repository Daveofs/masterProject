\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Map-level, simulation-based inference from large-scale-structure surveys requires very large numbers of accurate lightcone simulations -- a demand that gravity solvers like \textsc{PkdGrav3}, which anchor the CosmoGridV1 reference suite, can only meet at a cost of many node-hours per realisation. Fast particle-mesh codes such as Disco-DJ produce the same large-scale clustering in minutes, but their mesh-limited force resolution progressively suppresses small-scale power -- precisely the scales that carry much of the non-Gaussian information such analyses exploit. This thesis builds and validates a pipeline that combines the speed of the particle-mesh solver with the small-scale accuracy of the tree code. For each CosmoGridV1 cosmology, identical backscaled initial conditions are reconstructed from the published linear perturbations and handed to both solvers, and a custom shell builder embedded in Disco-DJ's time integration reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition on CosmoGridV1's exact radial shell grid -- yielding paired full-sky lightcones that differ only through the force solver. On these pairs, three correction models are trained and compared on held-out cosmologies: a deterministic per-multipole transfer-function rescaling in harmonic space, a conditional flow-matching U-Net that transports each map patch from low to high fidelity, and an EDM-style conditional diffusion model that generates the missing small-scale residual from noise. All three restore the angular power spectrum of the corrected lightcones to the few-percent level on the scales they act on; the two generative models additionally recover the non-Gaussian one-point moments and the weak-lensing convergence statistics that the deterministic rescaling by construction cannot. The corrected fast lightcones thus approach the reference simulations at a small fraction of their cost.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

The large-scale structure (LSS) of the Universe -- the cosmic web of halos, filaments, sheets, and voids traced by galaxies and dark matter -- is one of the richest available probes of cosmology: its growth over cosmic time is sensitive to the matter density $\Omega_m$, the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$, and the dark-energy equation of state $w_0$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:cosmic_web}). Photometric surveys now measure this structure over large fractions of the sky, and the most powerful analyses no longer compress the data to two-point functions: they work at the \emph{map level}, extracting non-Gaussian information with deep networks trained on simulations \cite{fluri_full_2022, thomsen_dark_2026}. The price of this approach is simulation demand. Map-level inference is \emph{simulation-based}: every evaluation of the likelihood is implicit in a forward-simulated mock, so the analysis consumes entire suites of $N$-body lightcones spanning thousands of cosmologies -- the role played by CosmoGridV1 \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}, whose realisations are each expensive \textsc{PkdGrav3} runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}).

Fast approximate solvers offer a way out. Particle-mesh (PM) codes such as Disco-DJ \cite{list_disco-dj_2026} evolve the same initial conditions in minutes on a single GPU (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), but their force resolution is capped by the mesh: small-scale power is progressively suppressed, and with it exactly the non-Gaussian content that motivates map-level inference in the first place. The question this thesis addresses is therefore: \emph{can the small-scale content of an expensive reference simulation be restored onto a cheap particle-mesh lightcone, accurately enough for map-level statistics, at negligible additional cost?}

Answering it requires two ingredients that are as much infrastructure as machine learning. First, a controlled learning problem: the low- and high-fidelity maps must differ \emph{only} through the force solver, which demands byte-identical initial conditions (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}) and lightcones defined on identical radial shells -- the latter provided by a shell builder implemented inside Disco-DJ's time integration (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}), since the code's native lightcone support does not reach CosmoGridV1's depth. Second, the corrections themselves (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}): a deterministic transfer-function rescaling in spherical-harmonic space, and two generative, patch-based models -- conditional flow matching and EDM diffusion -- that draw genuinely stochastic small-scale structure where the fast solver carries none. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} evaluates all three on cosmologies held out of training, from per-shell power spectra down to weak-lensing convergence maps.

The remainder of this chapter provides the background these methods rest on: the cosmological framework and the simulation codes (Sec.~\ref{sec:theoretical-background}), followed by the machine-learning concepts -- U-Nets, flow matching, and diffusion -- that the correction pipelines instantiate (Sec.~\ref{sec:sr-methods}).

\section{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:theoretical-background}
\subsection{Cosmological Framework}
\subsubsection{The Cosmic Web}
\label{sec:cosmic_web}

On scales of a few to a few hundred megaparsecs, matter in the Universe is not smoothly distributed but arranged into an interconnected network of dense compact knots, elongated filaments, two-dimensional sheets, and large under-dense voids -- collectively known as the \emph{cosmic web} \cite{bond_how_1996, libeskind_tracing_2018}. The term originates with \citeauthor{bond_how_1996}, who showed that this filament-dominated pattern is already present in embryonic form in the tidal field of the initial density perturbations, with the nonlinear gravitational evolution sharpening rather than creating it \cite{bond_how_1996}. The same gravitational instability that seeds this pattern is responsible for its growth: initially small density fluctuations are amplified by their own self-gravity, so that overdense regions contract while voids expand and come to dominate the volume of the Universe \cite{bond_how_1996}. Large galaxy redshift surveys -- 2dFGRS, SDSS, and the 2MASS redshift survey (2MRS) among them -- as well as cosmological $N$-body simulations both recover this same web-like arrangement of galaxies and dark matter \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}.

Gravitational instability drives the emergence of anisotropic structure: because collapse along the direction of a region's strongest tidal compression proceeds fastest, an initially only mildly non-spherical overdensity first flattens into a sheet, then contracts further into a filament, and only finally collapses fully into a compact halo -- the sequence of anisotropic collapse first described by Zel'dovich \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. This picture can be made quantitative through the local tidal (or velocity-shear) field: following the tidal-tensor classification reviewed by \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}, one computes the Hessian of the (rescaled) gravitational potential $\phi$,
\begin{equation}
  T_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x_\alpha \partial x_\beta}, \qquad \nabla^2 \phi = \delta,
  \label{eq:tidal_tensor}
\end{equation}
where $\delta = (\rho - \bar\rho)/\bar\rho$ is the dimensionless matter overdensity and $\phi$ has been normalised by $4\pi G \bar\rho$ so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:tidal_tensor} takes the form of the Poisson equation \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. Diagonalising the (real, symmetric) tidal tensor at each point gives three eigenvalues $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \lambda_3$; counting how many of them exceed a chosen threshold $\lambda_{\rm th}$ classifies that point as part of a void (zero eigenvalues above threshold), a sheet (one), a filament (two), or a knot (three) \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. Voids, sheets, filaments and knots are thus not four independent morphologies but a nested hierarchy, each embedded within the next.

\subsubsection{Matter Power Spectrum and Transfer Function}
\label{sec:power_spectrum}

While the tidal-tensor classification above describes the cosmic web \emph{qualitatively}, its statistical properties are quantified through the matter power spectrum $P(k)$. Writing the density contrast in Fourier space as $\delta(\mathbf{k})$ and assuming statistical homogeneity and isotropy, the power spectrum is defined through
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle \delta(\mathbf{k})\, \delta^*(\mathbf{k}')\big\rangle = (2\pi)^3\, \delta_D(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}')\, P(k),
  \label{eq:power_spectrum_def}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_D$ is the Dirac delta and the angle brackets denote an ensemble average over realisations of the initial conditions \cite{dodelson_modern_2020, huterer_growth_2023}. In a simplified form it can be written as
\begin{equation}
    P(k) \propto k^{n_s}\,T^2(k),
\end{equation}
with $n_s$ the scalar spectral index \cite{eisenstein_baryonic_1998, huterer_growth_2023}. The transfer function $T(k)$ can in turn be approximated by
\begin{equation}
    T(k) = 1 - \frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}} + \dots, \qquad k_{\mathrm{eq}} = H_0\sqrt{\frac{2\Omega_m}{\Omega_r}},
\end{equation}
\cite{bardeen_statistics_1986, eisenstein_baryonic_1998}. The transfer function is therefore shaped by the energy content of the Universe at each epoch: the scale $k_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is set by matter--radiation equality -- the crossing of the radiation and matter curves in Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function} -- and massive neutrinos modify this budget in a scale-dependent way. As the red curves in Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function} show, each neutrino mass state behaves as radiation while relativistic and dilutes like matter after its non-relativistic transition; in between, neutrinos free-stream out of small-scale perturbations and suppress $T(k)$ below their free-streaming scale.

The dependence on the neutrino mass is not a side note here: it reappears directly when generating the initial conditions for the simulations in Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}, since matching the transfer function -- neutrinos included -- is a precondition for those initial conditions to be consistent with CosmoGridV1. More generally, obtaining $P(k)$ precisely requires solving the coupled linear Einstein--Boltzmann equations for the perturbed Friedmann--Lema\^itre--Robertson--Walker metric and the phase-space distributions of all matter and radiation species -- the system solved by the \textsc{class} code used in Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} and by the differentiable Einstein--Boltzmann module of the Disco-DJ framework \cite{hahn_disco-dj_2024}.

\subsubsection{Lightcones and Snapshots}
\label{sec:lightcones}

An $N$-body simulation naturally outputs \emph{snapshots}: the full particle phase-space (positions and velocities) within a periodic box of fixed comoving side length, at a fixed time. Reproducing how we actually observe the Universe, however, requires a \emph{lightcone}: an observer at a fixed spacetime point receives light emitted from progressively more distant (and hence earlier) shells as one looks to higher redshift, so a simulated lightcone must record particles at the redshift-dependent comoving distance from which they are being observed, not at a single global time.

Because the depth of the desired lightcone (out to some $z_{\max}$) is typically larger than a single simulation box, the box is periodically replicated around a centrally placed observer: replication exploits the fact that a simulation with periodic boundary conditions can, in principle, represent an arbitrarily large volume, at the cost of the same structures reappearing at multiple replica images along a given line of sight \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. The trade-off between box size, structure repetition, and the resulting bias or excess variance in projected lightcone statistics is discussed in detail by \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}, who additionally introduce a shell-permutation scheme that mitigates the repeated-structure problem by drawing different radial shells of a single lightcone from independent simulations of the same cosmology. The shell builder developed in this thesis (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}) implements the same replication idea directly inside the time integration, rather than as a post-processing step.

\subsection{Cosmological Simulations}

Cosmological $N$-body simulations have historically tracked, and in some respects driven, the development of high-performance computing: the number of particles that can be evolved in a fixed wall-clock time has grown by many orders of magnitude, both through raw hardware improvements and through continual gains in the efficiency of the gravity solvers themselves \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. The scientific payoff lies in modelling the large-scale structure (LSS) of the Universe to the non-linear precision that photometric surveys require. LSS observables are particularly sensitive to the present-day matter density $\Omega_m$ and the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$; because a lightcone simulation can be sliced into tomographic redshift bins, it further tracks the growth of structure over cosmic time, which is what makes such simulations sensitive to the dark-energy equation-of-state parameter $w_0$ and hence to the physical origin of cosmic acceleration \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. Two solvers embody opposite ends of the resulting accuracy-versus-cost trade-off, and both are used in this thesis: the tree-based \textsc{PkdGrav3} (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), which anchors the accurate CosmoGridV1 reference simulations, and the particle-mesh Disco-DJ (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), which is fast enough to generate the large volume of training data our approach requires.

\subsubsection{Disco-DJ}
\label{sec:discodj}

Disco-DJ (\emph{Differentiable Simulations for Cosmology -- Done with JAX}) is a particle-mesh (PM) code whose entire forward model is GPU-accelerated and differentiable, so that gradients of any output with respect to the input parameters -- or the initial conditions themselves -- are available through automatic differentiation \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. Like any $N$-body method it approximates the collisionless dynamics of dark matter, i.e.\ a solution of the cosmological Vlasov--Poisson system for the phase-space distribution $f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p},t)$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{p}}{a^2}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}} f = 0,
  \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^2\phi = \frac{3\,\Omega_m H_0^2}{2a}\,\delta,
  \label{eq:vlasov_poisson}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ are comoving coordinates, $\mathbf{p} = a^2\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ the conjugate momentum, $a(t)$ the scale factor, and $\phi$ the peculiar gravitational potential sourced by the density contrast $\delta$ through the Poisson equation \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.

What sets the PM scheme apart from the tree/FMM solver of Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav} is how the potential is obtained: rather than summing particle-by-particle, the particles are deposited onto a regular mesh to give $\delta$, the Poisson equation is solved in Fourier space with a single FFT (where $\nabla^2 \to -k^2$), and the force $-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi$ is interpolated back to each particle \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. A force evaluation is thus global and cheap, at the price of a spatial resolution limited by the mesh spacing -- the origin of the small-scale power deficit that the correction pipelines of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} are built to remove, and, as shown in Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}, also the origin of Disco-DJ's memory footprint on a cluster.

\subsubsection{PkdGrav3}
\label{sec:pkdgrav}

The gravity solver \textsc{PkdGrav3} takes the opposite approach: rather than a mesh, it computes the gravitational force with a binary-tree Fast Multipole Method (FMM). Rather than only approximating a distant tree cell's effect on individual particles (as in a Barnes--Hut tree code), it also expands the potential \emph{within} a receiving cell sourced by a distant cell, an interaction that lets the cost of a full force evaluation scale as $\mathcal{O}(N)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}.

This accuracy is not free: particles in different regions of a cosmological box span many orders of magnitude in local density, and hence in dynamical time, so a single global time step would be highly wasteful. \textsc{PkdGrav3} instead assigns each particle to one of several time-step ``rungs'', advancing only the particles active on a given rung at each sub-step, and additionally builds a second, smaller tree restricted to the small subset of particles on the shortest (``very active'') rungs so that this frequently-rebuilt tree stays cheap. The combination is summarised by the kick-drift-kick ``umbrella'' diagram over one base time step shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pkd_rungs}; it keeps the tree-construction cost from dominating the runtime even though the shortest rungs may be walked hundreds of times per base step \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}.

\subsubsection{CosmoGridV1}
\label{sec:cosmogrid}

CosmoGridV1 is a large, publicly released suite of lightcone $N$-body simulations built specifically for map-level (rather than two-point-function-only) cosmological inference from large-scale-structure probes such as weak lensing and galaxy clustering \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. It samples a six-dimensional flat $w$CDM parameter space ($\Omega_m$, $\sigma_8$, $w_0$, $H_0$, $n_s$, $\Omega_b$, with a fixed total neutrino mass) at 2500 points arranged on a Sobol sequence, running 7 independent realisations at every point, in addition to 200 realisations (with parameter-derivative pairs) at a fixed fiducial cosmology and a smaller set of higher-resolution benchmark runs used to validate the main configuration's choices of box size, particle count, and shell thickness \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. Every simulation is run with \textsc{PkdGrav3} in a periodic box of $900\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ with $832^3$ particles, and stores its lightcone as HEALPix particle-count maps at $N_{\rm side}=2048$ out to $z=3.5$, together with friends-of-friends halo catalogues at each output step \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. These raw particle-count shells are the basis from which observable maps of weak-lensing convergence, galaxy clustering, and intrinsic alignment are subsequently constructed, and the same dataset has previously been used, together with a shell-based baryon correction model, for a deep-learning cosmological parameter analysis of the KiDS-1000 weak-lensing survey \cite{fluri_full_2022}. It is this suite -- box size, particle count, cosmology, and all -- that the pipeline of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} takes as its accuracy target.

\section{Super-Resolution Methods}
\label{sec:sr-methods}

The resolution deficit between Disco-DJ's particle-mesh force solve and \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s tree-based one (Secs.~\ref{sec:discodj}, \ref{sec:pkdgrav}) is what the correction $f$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} is built to remove. Before turning to that correction directly, this section reviews the machine-learning background it draws on. A central design question for any model acting on the full-sky lightcone maps of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} is how it can process an input of $\sim 5\times10^7$ pixels per shell without exceeding the memory of a single GPU -- and the choice of architecture and training paradigm shapes how that question is answered.

\subsection{The Memory Problem on the Sphere}
\label{subsec:sr-problem}

Cosmological and astrophysical observables are frequently recorded as spherical sky maps: the Cosmic Microwave Background, neutral-hydrogen intensity maps, galaxy clustering, and gravitational lensing have all been analysed in this form. These maps are traditionally reduced to summary statistics such as the power spectrum or higher-order correlation functions, but convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have more recently been proposed as an alternative analysis tool in cosmology, since they can be trained to construct statistics that maximise the precision of parameter estimation while remaining robust to noise \cite{perraudin_deepsphere_2019}.

Applying a CNN directly on the sphere, however, is not a straightforward extension of the flat-image case, and raises two distinct challenges. First, a square pixel grid has discrete translation symmetries that make convolution -- and the shift of a filter by one pixel -- well defined; no grid on the sphere shares a perfectly symmetric structure of this kind, so moving a spherical filter requires some form of interpolation. Second, computational cost: because the rotation group $SO(3)$ is itself a three-dimensional manifold, a naive implementation of the $SO(3)$ correlation needed for a fully rotation-equivariant spherical CNN scales as $\mathcal{O}(n^6)$ \cite{cohen_spherical_2018}, which becomes prohibitive as the grid density grows.

Two classical tools mitigate the cost of working at full spherical resolution. The first is a change of basis: expanding a field in spherical harmonics rather than pixels -- the same change of basis that made constrained realisations of Gaussian random fields tractable when iterative pixel-space solvers became impractically slow \cite{hoffman_constrained_1991} -- is made fast in practice by the spherical-harmonic transform (SHT) of the HEALPix pixelisation \cite{gorski_healpix_2005}. Harmonic-space representations also have a long history inside deep learning itself, from the universal approximation theorem to Fourier-based network architectures \cite{li_fourier_2021}; the correction pipeline of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer} works entirely in this basis. The second tool is architectural: avoid processing the full map at once. The overlap-tile strategy of \citeauthor{ronneberger_u-net_2015} segments arbitrarily large images by splitting them into patches, so that GPU memory limits the patch size but not the image size \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}; the pipelines of Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow} and \ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} adopt exactly this strategy on gnomonic projections of the sphere.

\subsection{Architectures}
\label{subsec:architectures}

\subsubsection{U-Net}
\label{sssec:unet-arch}

The U-Net architecture was introduced for image segmentation in the regime of few annotated training samples \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}. Structurally, it consists of two complementary halves (Figure~\ref{fig:unet_generic}): a contracting path whose role is to capture \emph{context}, and a symmetric expanding path whose role is to recover precise, pixel-level \emph{localization} \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}.

The two paths achieve this division of labour through complementary operations. In the contracting path, each pooling step halves the spatial resolution while the number of feature channels is doubled: deeper layers operate on a coarser grid, but each unit there summarises an increasingly large region of the input. This builds up contextual, semantic information about \emph{what} is present in a region, at the cost of exactly \emph{where} within that region it lies. The expanding path recovers the lost spatial precision: each upsampling step doubles the resolution again and is paired with a concatenation of the corresponding feature map from the contracting path -- a \emph{skip connection} that lets fine, precisely localized detail flow directly across the network instead of having to survive the compression through the coarse bottleneck \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}. (In the original architecture the skipped feature maps are additionally cropped, only because its unpadded convolutions shrink the feature maps slightly; the padded convolutions used in this thesis make the crop unnecessary.)

\subsection{Generative Paradigms}
\label{subsec:generative-paradigms}

Beyond deterministic architectures, deep generative models -- algorithms that estimate and sample from an unknown data distribution -- offer a different way to frame the correction problem: instead of predicting \emph{the} high-resolution map (a conditional mean, which necessarily averages away small-scale variance), they draw a \emph{sample} that carries the full variance of the target field. This distinction is central to Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}, where restoring the small-scale \emph{power} -- not just the mean -- is the entire objective. Two such paradigms are used in this thesis.

\subsubsection{Flow Matching}
\label{sssec:flow-matching}

Flow Matching (FM) is a simulation-free objective for training continuous normalising flow (CNF) models that admits general probability paths -- rather than only diffusion processes -- as supervision \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. Let $x_1$ be distributed according to an unknown data distribution $q(x_1)$, of which only samples are available, and let $p_t$ be a probability path with $p_0$ a simple distribution (e.g.\ the standard normal) and $p_1 \approx q$. Given a target path $p_t(x)$ and the vector field $u_t(x)$ that generates it, the FM objective regresses a learned vector field $v_t$ with parameters $\theta$ onto $u_t$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,p_t(x)}\big\lVert v_t(x) - u_t(x) \big\rVert^2, \qquad t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1],\ x\sim p_t(x),
    \label{eq:flow_matching}
\end{equation}
so that a model reaching zero loss generates $p_t(x)$ by integrating the ODE $\dot{x} = v_t(x)$. Used naively this objective is intractable -- no closed form for a suitable $(p_t, u_t)$ pair is generally available -- but \citeauthor{lipman_flow_2023} construct both from probability paths and vector fields defined only \emph{per sample}, whose aggregation provably recovers the marginal $p_t$ and $u_t$ \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. The simplest such construction, used in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}, is the straight-line (linear) interpolant between a start point $x_0 \sim p_0$ and a data point $x_1$,
\begin{equation}
    x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1, \qquad u_t = x_1 - x_0,
    \label{eq:linear_interpolant}
\end{equation}
sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:flow_interp}: the target velocity is constant along each path, and the straighter the true transport between $p_0$ and $p_1$, the fewer ODE integration steps inference needs.

\subsubsection{Diffusion}
\label{sssec:diffusion-theory}

Diffusion-based generative models have emerged as a powerful framework for neural image synthesis, in both unconditional and conditional settings, in some cases matching or surpassing the image quality achieved by GANs \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Denoting the data distribution $p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)$ with standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}$, one considers the family of mollified distributions $p(x;\sigma)$ obtained by adding i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ to the data; for $\sigma_{\max}\gg\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}$, $p(x;\sigma_{\max})$ is practically indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise. A diffusion model samples a noise image $x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ and sequentially denoises it through decreasing noise levels $\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_0>\sigma_1>\dots>\sigma_N=0$, so that the endpoint is distributed according to the data \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. The process can equivalently be described by a probability-flow ODE that continuously raises or lowers the noise level as time runs forward or backward; \citeauthor{karras_elucidating_2022} disentangle the design choices this leaves open -- noise schedule, network preconditioning, loss weighting, and ODE solver -- into the modular ``EDM'' formulation whose concrete instantiation in this thesis is given in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Compared to the flow bridge above, a diffusion sampler always starts from pure noise, so its trajectory is genuinely curved and needs materially more solver steps -- the price of a fully generative process.

The architectures and generative paradigms of this section are the building blocks that Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} combines into the three concrete correction pipelines of this thesis.

\chapter{Methods and implementation}
\section{Pipeline Architecture}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The reference suite CosmoGridV1 evolves every realisation with \textsc{PkdGrav3} (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), whose FMM force solver and adaptive time-stepping resolve structure accurately down to small scales. That accuracy is expensive: each lightcone costs many node-hours, so the large number of realisations required for map-level, simulation-based inference dominates the total cost of the analysis \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023, potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. Disco-DJ offers the opposite trade-off: its particle-mesh solver (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}) reproduces the same large-scale clustering in minutes rather than hours, but the force resolution is capped by the PM grid, so power is progressively lost on small scales -- exactly the scales that carry much of the non-Gaussian information exploited by map-level analyses \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.

The aim of this work is therefore to keep Disco-DJ's speed while recovering the small-scale content that \textsc{PkdGrav3} would have produced. Formally, we seek a correction $f$ acting on the cheap lightcone maps such that
\begin{equation}
  f\!\left(\mathrm{Map}_{\textsc{disco}}\right) \;\approx\; \mathrm{Map}_{\textsc{CosmoGrid}},
  \label{eq:correction_goal}
\end{equation}
where both maps are generated from the \emph{same} initial conditions and cosmology, so that $f$ has only to model the resolution-dependent difference between the two solvers, not any change of realisation. This single requirement -- \emph{same initial conditions, same output format} -- dictates the entire architecture of the pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview}), which consists of three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Initial-condition generation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}). CosmoGridV1 publishes its cosmologies and shell tables, but not initial conditions that Disco-DJ could consume directly. For every cosmology we therefore rebuild the exact linear input -- the transfer function including massive neutrinos (Sec.~\ref{sec:power_spectrum}) -- with \textsc{class}, let \textsc{PkdGrav3} generate backscaled initial conditions from it, and hand the identical particle state to both solvers. Any residual difference between the two output maps is then attributable to the force solvers alone, which is precisely the difference $f$ must learn.
  \item \textbf{Shell building} (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}). CosmoGridV1 stores its lightcone as 69 HEALPix particle-count shells out to $z=3.5$ at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$ \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. Disco-DJ, however, ships only a restricted lightcone mode limited to the simulation box volume -- far short of the $z=3.5$ depth, which requires many periodic box replications (Sec.~\ref{sec:lightcones}). We therefore implemented a dedicated \emph{shell builder} inside Disco-DJ's time integration (\texttt{shell\_builder/}) that reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition and CosmoGridV1's shell binning exactly, so the low- and high-fidelity maps are defined shell-by-shell on identical radial bins.
  \item \textbf{Correction pipelines} (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}). On the resulting paired shells, three independent corrections $f$ are built and compared: a deterministic \emph{transfer-function} rescaling in harmonic space, a \emph{flow-matching U-Net} that transports each map patch from low to high fidelity, and an \emph{EDM diffusion} model that generates the missing small-scale residual from noise. All three are evaluated with the same shared statistics (per-shell and tomographic $C_\ell$, one-point moments, and weak-lensing convergence maps built from the corrected lightcone).
\end{enumerate}

Two structurally different routes to $f$ coexist in stage 3, and keeping both is deliberate. The harmonic-space route corrects the angular power spectrum $C_\ell$ mode-amplitude by mode-amplitude: it is cheap, exact on the sphere, and targets the deficit exactly where Eq.~\eqref{eq:cl} relates it to $P(k)$, but it corrects a summary statistic rather than the full field. The image-level route is expressive enough to restore \emph{structure}, not just power, but a full-sky HEALPix map at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$ ($\sim 5\times10^7$ pixels) does not fit through a CNN on a single GPU, so it must be tiled into patches and stitched back (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}) -- the memory problem anticipated in Sec.~\ref{subsec:sr-problem}. The remainder of this chapter follows the pipeline in data order: initial conditions, shell builder, correction pipelines, and finally the computational infrastructure everything runs on.

\label{sec:ic_generation}

This section describes how that common starting point is constructed for every CosmoGridV1 cosmology; the procedure is automated over all grid cosmologies by \texttt{pkdgrav/IC\_preparation.py}, which runs the per-cosmology Boltzmann solves concurrently (48 at a time) instead of serially. \subsection{Matching the CosmoGridV1 Linear Input}
\label{subsec:ic_linear}

Each CosmoGridV1 run ships the linear perturbations its own initial conditions were built from, as tabulated CDM+baryon density contrasts $\delta_{cb}(k, a)$. From the $a=1$ slice, the dimensional matter power spectrum is reconstructed as
\begin{equation}
  P_{cb}(k) \;=\; \frac{2\pi^2}{k^3}\, P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k)\, \delta_{cb}^2(k)\, h^3,
  \qquad P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k) = A_s \left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{pivot}}}\right)^{n_s - 1},
  \label{eq:pk_reconstruct}
\end{equation}
with $k_{\mathrm{pivot}} = 0.05\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ and $(A_s, n_s, h)$ read from the run's parameter file (\texttt{get\_transfer\_function.py}). The corresponding transfer function is exported in the \textsc{class} table convention $-\delta_m/k^2$ that \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s IC generator reads directly (\texttt{achTfFile}), so the particle load is drawn from \emph{exactly} the linear spectrum CosmoGridV1 used -- massive neutrinos included, as required by Sec.~\ref{sec:power_spectrum}.

Two derived quantities complete the parameter file. The clustering amplitude is obtained from the reconstructed spectrum through the standard top-hat integral
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_8^2 \;=\; \int \frac{k^2\,P_{cb}(k)}{2\pi^2}\, W^2(kR)\;\mathrm{d}k,
  \qquad W(x) = \frac{3\,(\sin x - x\cos x)}{x^{3}},\quad R = 8\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h,
  \label{eq:sigma8}
\end{equation}
and the radiation density $\Omega_r$ is computed by a \textsc{class} solve of the same cosmology with three degenerate massive neutrino states of $m_\nu = 0.02$~eV each, matching the fixed total neutrino mass of the CosmoGridV1 grid (Sec.~\ref{sec:cosmogrid}). \texttt{IC\_preparation.py} patches these values -- together with $\Omega_0 = \Omega_{cdm} + \Omega_\nu + \Omega_b$ and $w_0$ -- into each run's \texttt{cosmology.par}, idempotently, so a re-run only changes what is still inconsistent.

\subsection{Backscaled Initial Conditions and Hand-Off}
\label{subsec:ic_handoff}

From this patched configuration, \textsc{PkdGrav3} generates \emph{backscaled} initial conditions: the $a=1$ linear spectrum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pk_reconstruct} is scaled back to the starting redshift with the linear growth factor, and particles are displaced accordingly at the CosmoGridV1 resolution of $832^3$ particles in the $900\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ box. The resulting particle positions and velocities are exported in the \texttt{tipsy} format (\texttt{CosmoML.tipsy}) and used, \emph{unmodified}, as the initial state of the corresponding Disco-DJ run: Disco-DJ evolves this state forward to $a=1$ and accumulates the lightcone with the shell builder of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder} over the same redshift range ($z_{\max}=3.5$ down to $z=0$) at the same HEALPix resolution $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$. Both solvers thus share one particle realisation down to the byte level, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:correction_goal} isolates the solver difference by construction.

\subsection{Validation against CosmoGridV1}
\label{subsec:ic_validation}

Before this machinery is trusted for training data, it is validated end-to-end at the high-fidelity side: \textsc{PkdGrav3} is run with its \emph{native} lightcone module on the exact box, resolution, and cosmology used by CosmoGridV1, and the resulting matter angular power spectrum $C_\ell$ is compared shell-by-shell against the corresponding published CosmoGridV1 lightcone. Agreement here confirms that the reconstructed transfer function, the derived parameters of Sec.~\ref{subsec:ic_linear}, and the run configuration together reproduce the reference suite; only then is the same IC handed to Disco-DJ, whose shell output additionally validates the shell builder itself (\texttt{pkdgrav/validate\_shell\_builder.py}).

\section{Shell Builder}
\label{sec:shell_builder}

With identical initial conditions in place, the remaining prerequisite for Eq.~\eqref{eq:correction_goal} is that Disco-DJ emit its lightcone in exactly CosmoGridV1's format: particle-count maps on the same 69 radial shells. This section describes the shell builder (\texttt{shell\_builder/Shell\_Builder.py}) that adds this capability to Disco-DJ's time integration.

\subsection{Streaming Shell Accumulation}
\label{sec:shell_builder_core}

A lightcone output requires the particle distribution to be resampled at every integration step, since the observable past-lightcone shell changes continuously as the universe evolves. The most direct implementation -- store the full particle snapshot at each of the $n_{\mathrm{steps}}$ integration steps and decompose into shells afterwards -- is memory-prohibitive: retaining $n_{\mathrm{steps}}$ full-resolution snapshots simultaneously exceeds the available device memory for the particle counts considered here (cf.\ Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}). The shell decomposition is therefore \emph{interleaved} with the time integration, so that at any moment only the particle states immediately before and after the current step, plus the low-dimensional set of pixelised count maps, are held in memory (Figure~\ref{fig:shell_stream}).

\paragraph{Time discretization.} The radial shells are not defined by the integrator itself but supplied externally, through a CosmoGridV1-format metainfo table: each shell $s \in \{1,\dots,S\}$ is a redshift interval $[z_s^{\mathrm{lo}}, z_s^{\mathrm{hi}}]$ with corresponding comoving-distance interval $[r_s^{\mathrm{lo}}, r_s^{\mathrm{hi}}]$. To ensure that no shell boundary is crossed \emph{within} an integration step, the integration times are defined as the union of all shell edges,
\begin{equation}
  \{z_j\}_{j=0}^{S} = \mathrm{unique}\Big(\{z_s^{\mathrm{lo}}\}_{s=1}^{S} \,\cup\, \{z_s^{\mathrm{hi}}\}_{s=1}^{S}\Big), \qquad a_j = \frac{1}{1+z_j},
  \label{eq:step_grid}
\end{equation}
sorted in ascending order of $a_j$. By construction, every shell boundary coincides exactly with an integration-step boundary. The interval $[a_{\mathrm{ini}}, a_0]$ preceding the first shell is integrated separately with $n_{\mathrm{pre}}$ sub-steps and without shell accumulation, to bring the particle state into registry with the grid of Eq.~\eqref{eq:step_grid}; each of the remaining $S$ intervals $[a_j, a_{j+1}]$ is advanced with exactly one sub-step.

\paragraph{Accumulation.} At step $j$, the comoving positions of all particles are recorded before and after the sub-step, $\{\mathbf{x}_p^{(j)}\}$ and $\{\mathbf{x}_p^{(j+1)}\}$, and the step's scale-factor edges are converted to comoving lightcone radii through the cosmology-dependent map $\chi(a)$,
\begin{equation}
  R_j = \chi(a_j), \qquad R_{j+1} = \chi(a_{j+1}), \qquad R_j > R_{j+1}.
  \label{eq:step_radii}
\end{equation}
The shells traversed during the step are those whose radial bin overlaps the swept annulus,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}_j = \big\{\, s \;:\; r_s^{\mathrm{lo}} < R_j \ \wedge\ r_s^{\mathrm{hi}} > R_{j+1} \,\big\};
  \label{eq:active_shells}
\end{equation}
because the step grid is built from the shell edges themselves, $\mathcal{A}_j$ contains a single shell in the generic case (the general form covers degenerate or adjacent bins in the input table). For each active shell, the crossing test of Sec.~\ref{sec:crossing} converts the pre-/post-step states into a HEALPix count map $\mathbf{M}_s^{(j)}$, which is added to the shell's running total,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{S}_s \;\mathrel{+}=\; \mathbf{M}_s^{(j)}, \qquad s \in \mathcal{A}_j.
  \label{eq:shell_update}
\end{equation}
The pre-step state is then discarded and the post-step state becomes the next step's pre-step state. Once all steps are processed, the per-shell maps $\{\mathbf{S}_s\}_{s=1}^{S}$ are serialized together with the shell table. At no point are more than two full particle snapshots and $S$ integer maps of size $N_{\mathrm{pix}}$ resident -- this is what removes the memory bottleneck of the naive end-of-run decomposition.

\subsection{Crossing Condition}
\label{sec:crossing}

Which particles contribute to a shell, and where, is decided by the moving-lightcone formulation of \textsc{PkdGrav3} \cite{noauthor_examplepkdgrav3_lightcone_2022}, which the shell builder reproduces exactly (\texttt{Shell\_Builder.py} implements the same crossing equation as \texttt{lightcone.cxx}); Figure~\ref{fig:crossing} illustrates the two ingredients.

\paragraph{Periodic replicas.} The observer is placed at the box centre, $\mathbf{o} = (L/2, L/2, L/2)$, with $L$ the box side length. Since the requested lightcone depth $\chi_{\max} = \chi\big(1/(1+z_{\max})\big)$ exceeds $L$, the simulation volume is periodically replicated (Sec.~\ref{sec:lightcones}): the finite replica set
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D} = \Big\{\, \mathbf{d} = (n_1, n_2, n_3)\,L \;:\; \mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^3, \ \; \text{replicated cube intersects the ball of radius } \chi_{\max} \text{ about } \mathbf{o} \,\Big\}
  \label{eq:replica_set}
\end{equation}
is precomputed once from $(\chi_{\max}, L)$. For each $\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}$ the nearest and farthest possible distances of the replicated cube from the observer,
\begin{equation}
  r_{\min}(\mathbf{d}) = \big\lVert \max(0, |\mathbf{d}| - L/2) \big\rVert, \qquad
  r_{\max}(\mathbf{d}) = \big\lVert |\mathbf{d}| + L/2 \big\rVert,
  \label{eq:replica_bounds}
\end{equation}
are also precomputed (componentwise $\max$ and absolute value, then the Euclidean norm), so that a replica whose radial range cannot overlap a step's lightcone annulus is rejected without evaluating a single particle.

\paragraph{Linear crossing formula.} For step $j$ with boundaries $a_j < a_{j+1}$, write $r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} = \chi(a_j)$ and $r_j^{\mathrm{lo}} = \chi(a_{j+1})$. For a particle with pre-/post-step positions $\mathbf{x}_p(a_j), \mathbf{x}_p(a_{j+1})$ relative to the observer and a replica offset $\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}$, define
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{R}_0 = \mathbf{x}_p(a_j) + \mathbf{d}, \qquad \mathbf{R}_1 = \mathbf{x}_p(a_{j+1}) + \mathbf{d}, \qquad
  r_0 = \lVert \mathbf{R}_0 \rVert, \qquad r_1 = \lVert \mathbf{R}_1 \rVert.
  \label{eq:replica_radii}
\end{equation}
Following \textsc{PkdGrav3}, both the particle radius $r_p(v)$ and the inward-moving lightcone front $R(v)$ are interpolated linearly over the step,
\begin{equation}
  r_p(v) = r_0 + v\,(r_1 - r_0), \qquad R(v) = r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - v\,(r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_j^{\mathrm{lo}}), \qquad v\in[0,1],
  \label{eq:scalar_interp}
\end{equation}
and the crossing $r_p(v^\ast) = R(v^\ast)$ is solved in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  v^\ast = \frac{r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_0}{\big(r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_j^{\mathrm{lo}}\big) + (r_1 - r_0)}.
  \label{eq:vx_formula}
\end{equation}
A particle--replica pair is accepted only if $v^\ast \in [0,1)$. Since the half-open acceptance intervals of consecutive steps partition the time axis, every particle is caught in \emph{at most one} step per replica; evaluating the crossing test against the (generally narrower) metainfo shell bounds instead of the step bounds would break this partition and could double-count particles crossing two adjacent shells within one step. Numerically, pairs with $|r_1 - r_0| \le 10^{-20}$ are treated as non-moving and rejected, and the denominator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vx_formula} is floored in magnitude at $10^{-10}$.

\paragraph{Shell assignment and pixelization.} The crossing position is obtained by applying $v^\ast$ to the \emph{vector} positions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}} = (1-v^\ast)\,\mathbf{R}_0 + v^\ast\,\mathbf{R}_1, \qquad r_{\mathrm{cross}} = \lVert \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}} \rVert,
  \label{eq:cross_pos}
\end{equation}
and the pair is kept only if its crossing radius falls inside the shell bounds,
\begin{equation}
  r_s^{\mathrm{lo}} \le r_{\mathrm{cross}} < r_s^{\mathrm{hi}}.
  \label{eq:shell_filter}
\end{equation}
Surviving crossings are pixelised with the equal-area HEALPix \textsc{ring} scheme at resolution $N_{\mathrm{side}}$ from the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{cross}} = \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}}/r_{\mathrm{cross}}$, and the corresponding pixel of $\mathbf{M}_s^{(j)}$ is incremented. In the implementation, the loop over all active replicas runs inside a single JIT-compiled JAX kernel (\texttt{jax.lax.fori\_loop}), so the per-replica work is dispatched to the GPU as one program rather than from Python.

This procedure reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s box-replication lightcone \cite{noauthor_examplepkdgrav3_lightcone_2022} with two deliberate differences: the observer sits at the box centre rather than a corner, and the replica set $\mathcal{D}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_set} is sized adaptively from $(\chi_{\max}, L)$ -- with the per-step radial reject of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_bounds} -- rather than fixed a priori to $6^3 = 216$ images.

\section{Correction Pipelines}
\label{sec:correction-pipelines}

Three correction pipelines were implemented, each a self-contained module under \texttt{ml/} (\texttt{transfer/}, \texttt{unet/}, \texttt{diffusion/}) sharing only the neutral analysis library \texttt{ml/analysis/}. They span the two routes identified in Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} -- one deterministic harmonic-space rescaling and two generative, patch-based models -- and are deliberately evaluated through the same shared statistics and plotting code, so their outputs are comparable figure-by-figure. Table~\ref{tab:pipelines} summarises the contrast; Figure~\ref{fig:shared_problem} states the shared problem.

One measured property of the data drives every design choice that follows. The per-multipole cross-correlation \cite{feng_fastpm_2016}
\begin{equation}
  r(\ell, s) \;=\; \frac{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}\times\mathrm{high}}\big\rangle}{\sqrt{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}\big\rangle\,\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}\big\rangle}},
  \label{eq:phase_corr}
\end{equation}
computed across the training runs (\texttt{ml/transfer/transfer\_function.py}), shows that on the shells that carry signal, the low and high maps \emph{share phases} ($r \approx 0.9$--$1.0$) well into the small scales: Disco-DJ mostly gets the small-scale \emph{amplitude} wrong, not the positions of structures. This is why a pure amplitude rescaling (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer}) works at all. Only where $r$ decays -- on faint, shot-noise-dominated shells and at the highest multipoles (e.g.\ $r=0.56$ at $\ell=1000$ and $0.06$ at $\ell=2500$ on the faintest shells) -- do the maps genuinely decorrelate, and there the correct behaviour is not to amplify Disco-DJ's own noise but to \emph{generate} statistically fresh small-scale structure, which is precisely the role of the two generative pipelines (Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}, \ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion}).

Two further design decisions are shared by all learned components and are motivated by measured failures of their alternatives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Correct only the small scales; pin the large scales to Disco-DJ.} Disco-DJ's large scales are already correct ($T\approx 1$, $r\approx 1$), so both generative pipelines are formulated as \emph{high-pass residual} models: the learning target is $\mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{high}-\mathrm{low})$, where $\mathrm{HP}$ is a radial Fourier high-pass with a raised-cosine transition (cutoff and transition width both $0.1$ of the patch Nyquist frequency by default), and the corrected patch is composed as $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{sample})$. The composition re-applies the filter, so the output's large scales equal the low map's \emph{exactly}, regardless of integration error (\texttt{ml/unet/flow\_model.py}, \texttt{ml/diffusion/model.py}). This formulation is a direct response to two observed failures: a full-field diffusion model drew each tile's large scales from independent noise, so after stitching, the cross-tile phases averaged incoherently and destroyed the low-$\ell$ convergence spectrum; and even the low$\to$high flow drifted the already-correct large scales by a few percent per shell, which compounded coherently across the dozens of shells a convergence map integrates. The harmonic pipeline applies the same principle exactly in $\ell$-space, through a raised-cosine ramp on its correction factors.
  \item \textbf{Hold out whole cosmologies, not random patches.} The train/validation split is by cosmology (\texttt{dataset.split\_by\_cosmo}): patches from the same run share large-scale structure, so a random per-patch split would leak the validation set into training. All reported evaluations are on cosmologies the models never saw.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Patch Extraction and Full-Sky Reconstruction}
\label{subsec:pipe-patches}

The two patch-based pipelines share their data machinery. Training pairs are built by \texttt{make\_patch\_dataset.py}: for each patch, a random (cosmology, run), a random shell, a random centre pixel (uniform on the sphere), and a random orientation angle are drawn, and \emph{both} the low and the high shell map are projected at that same pointing with a gnomonic (flat-sky) projection at the map's native pixel resolution -- a like-for-like $256\times256$ crop with no resampling beyond the projection itself. Each patch stores the metadata needed to recreate it exactly (centre, orientation, shell redshift bounds, comoving distance) together with the run's varied cosmological parameters $(\Omega_m, \Omega_b, n_s, \sigma_8, w_0, h)$ from the CosmoGridV1 metainfo catalogue. From these, the conditioning vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c} = \big(h,\ \Omega_{cdm},\ \Omega_b,\ \Omega_m,\ n_s,\ \sigma_8,\ w_0,\ z_{\mathrm{shell}}\big) \in \mathbb{R}^8
  \label{eq:cosmo_z}
\end{equation}
is assembled in one place (\texttt{dataset.cosmo\_z\_vector}) and consumed identically at train and inference time, so the two can never drift apart.

At inference, a full-sky shell is corrected by the overlap-tile scheme of Figure~\ref{fig:tiling} (\texttt{ml/analysis/patch\_tiling.py}): the sphere is tiled with overlapping gnomonic patches centred on a coarse HEALPix grid (chosen so the patch field of view is at least four times the centre spacing), the model corrects each tile, and the tiles are blended back with a radial cosine taper $w(r) = \tfrac12(1+\cos \pi r)$ that falls to zero at the tile edge, hiding the seams. The tile-to-sphere pixel indices depend only on the geometry, so they are built once and reused for every shell and cosmology -- replacing a per-tile re-projection that would otherwise dominate the entire reconstruction cost.

Two subtleties matter only for the \emph{stochastic} pipeline (diffusion). First, each sky pixel is covered by many overlapping tiles and the blend averages them; if every tile drew independent randomness, the average would suppress the generated small-scale amplitude to $1/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ of its true value, with $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$ the effective number of contributing tiles (measured: only $\sim$41\% of the injected amplitude, $\sim$17\% of the power, survives at the default overlap). The fix is to draw one \emph{global} noise field on the sphere and hand each tile the crop matching its own pixels, so overlapping tiles agree by construction. Second, the taper is raised to a power $p$ before normalisation ($p=32$ by default in \texttt{apply\_diffusion.py}): large $p$ makes the nearest tile ``own'' each pixel (a soft Voronoi partition), in the spirit of the original overlap-tile strategy \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015} -- overlap provides context, but each output pixel comes from essentially one prediction. Deterministic per-tile models are unaffected ($p=1$ is kept for the flow), since their overlapping predictions already agree.

\subsection{Harmonic-Space Transfer-Function Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipe-transfer}

The first pipeline (\texttt{ml/transfer/}) contains no image-level network at all: it exploits the phase-sharing fact of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr} and corrects only mode \emph{amplitudes}, in the spherical-harmonic basis anticipated in Sec.~\ref{subsec:sr-problem}. Every shell is transformed to its harmonic coefficients $a_{\ell m}$ (up to $\ell_{\max}=3000$), and each mode is rescaled by a per-shell transfer function \cite{feng_fastpm_2016, garrison_improving_2016}
\begin{equation}
  a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{corr}} = a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{low}} \cdot T(\ell, s),
  \qquad
  T(\ell, s) = \sqrt{\frac{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}(s) \big\rangle}{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}(s) \big\rangle}},
  \label{eq:transfer_def}
\end{equation}
which by construction matches the target power spectrum while preserving the (correct) phases. Both spectra are smoothed in $\log_{10}$-space with a boxcar filter \emph{before} the ratio is taken: the physical $T(\ell)$ is smooth, whereas a single realisation's $C_\ell$ carries sample-variance and shot-noise scatter that would otherwise be fitted and amplified. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_flow} shows the apply chain.

\paragraph{Two estimators of $T$.} The \texttt{fit} mode averages the smoothed spectra over the training runs and treats $T$ as a property of the solver pair, independent of cosmology -- a robust deterministic baseline. The \texttt{emulate} mode instead trains a small MLP emulator of $T$ on features $\big(\log_{10}(\ell{+}1),\ z,\ H_0,\ \Omega_{cdm},\ \Omega_b,\ \Omega_m,\ n_s,\ \sigma_8,\ \log_{10} C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}\big)$, standardised per feature; this interpolates the transfer function to unseen cosmologies of the grid. The emulator is a plain feed-forward network (hidden layers $256$--$256$--$128$, ReLU, linear output) implemented in PyTorch and trained on GPU (Adam, learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch size $4096$, $L_2$ weight decay $\alpha = 10^{-4}$), with a 10\% held-out validation split, early stopping, and best-validation weights restored (\texttt{transfer\_function.py}). The GPU implementation matters for a reason worth recording: the training set is millions of $(\ell, s)$ rows with only 9 features each, a workload where a CPU implementation is bound by per-batch overhead rather than arithmetic -- a GPU handles exactly this trivial-per-sample, huge-throughput shape. Both estimators also store the phase correlation $R(\ell, s)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr}.

\paragraph{Where to correct.} The correction must not touch the already-correct large scales, and ``large'' is a \emph{physical} statement: the PM resolution deficit sets in at a fixed comoving scale (of order the PM grid cell), which maps to a different multipole on every shell. Using the flat-sky relation $\ell \simeq k\,\chi(z)$, a fixed scale $L$ ($5\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ in the production configuration, \texttt{run\_transfer.sh}) is converted per shell to
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\min}(s) = \left\lceil \frac{2\pi\,\chi(z_s)}{L} \right\rceil,
  \label{eq:ell_min}
\end{equation}
below which $T$ is forced to 1, with a raised-cosine ramp $w(\ell)$ handing over smoothly above it (a hard step in harmonic space rings in real space). This matches the measured behaviour: nearby shells visibly deviate from CosmoGridV1 from $\ell \sim 150$--$300$, while the most distant shells show no deviation out to $\ell = 3000$ -- and correctly receive no correction, because their $\ell_{\min}$ exceeds $\ell_{\max}$.

\paragraph{Negative pixels.} The corrected field inevitably contains negative pixels, and it is worth being precise about why. The truth is a non-negative, integer \emph{count} map, and on the distant, sparse shells the mean count per pixel lies far below unity, so most pixels are exactly zero. The correction, by contrast, is a band-limited real-space \emph{fluctuation} field: the residual $(T-1)\,a_{\ell m}$ reconstructed by the inverse SHT is a smooth, zero-mean oscillation with both signs. Adding such a field onto a map dominated by empty pixels necessarily drives every pixel where the negative half-wave exceeds the local count below zero -- negativity is a structural consequence of representing a boosted \emph{continuous} overdensity on top of a nearly empty \emph{discrete} map, not a numerical defect of the method. Every cheap pixel-wise repair was measured and rejected: flooring at zero deletes only the negative excursions, which raises the mean and injects spurious power exactly on the small scales the correction just restored, and re-balancing the mean afterwards or correcting the logarithm of the density instead merely redistributes the same bias. The corrected field is therefore deliberately \emph{not post-processed}: it is treated as a continuous overdensity field (\texttt{--no-clip}) and passed unchanged into every downstream statistic -- angular power spectra, moments, and the weak-lensing convergence maps of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} -- none of which requires per-pixel count validity.

\subsection{U-Net Flow-Matching Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}

The second pipeline (\texttt{ml/unet/}) is the first of the two generative ones: a conditional flow-matching model (Sec.~\ref{sssec:flow-matching}) acting on the gnomonic patches of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}. Its defining choice is the \emph{start point} of the flow: instead of Gaussian noise, the ODE starts at the low-fidelity patch itself,
\begin{equation}
  x_0 = \delta_{\mathrm{low}}, \qquad
  x_1 = x_0 + \mathrm{HP}\!\left(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}}\right), \qquad
  x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1,
  \label{eq:flow_bridge}
\end{equation}
so the model learns a \emph{bridge} from low to high rather than a full generative map from noise (\texttt{flow\_model.py}). Because $x_0$ already carries the correct large-scale structure, the target velocity $u = x_1 - x_0 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}})$ is a pure high-pass field -- integrating it can only ever \emph{add} small-scale content -- and the trajectory is nearly straight, so 8 Euler steps suffice at inference (Table~\ref{tab:pipelines}). The training loss is the flow-matching regression of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_matching} on this interpolant,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\;
  \frac{\big\lVert v_\theta(x_t, t, \mathbf{c}) - (x_1 - x_0) \big\rVert^2}{\max\!\big(\mathrm{Var}(x_1{-}x_0)\,/\,\overline{\mathrm{Var}},\ 0.1\big)},
  \label{eq:flow_loss}
\end{equation}
where the denominator is a bounded, batch-relative per-patch variance normalisation: a dense, well-resolved shell's true residual is naturally tiny, so plain MSE gives it far weaker gradients than a sparse shell's large residual, and the network never learns to calibrate the ``correct answer is nearly zero here'' case -- visible as a persistent few-percent bias on the densest shells. Normalising by each patch's own residual variance, relative to the batch mean and clamped to at most a $10\times$ upweighting, removes this imbalance without the gradient blow-ups an unbounded reweighting was observed to cause (\texttt{train\_flow.py}).

\paragraph{Architecture.} The velocity network \texttt{FlowUNet} instantiates the generic U-Net of Figure~\ref{fig:unet_generic} with four levels and base width 32: channels $1\to32\to64\to128\to256\to512$ at spatial resolutions $256^2 \to 16^2$, mirrored by the decoder with skip concatenations, and a $1\times1$ output head (Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}). Two conditioning paths are threaded through it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flow time $t$, everywhere.} A sinusoidal embedding of $t$ (dimension 128, expanded to 512 by a two-layer MLP) modulates \emph{every} convolution block via FiLM: each block is two $3\times3$ convolutions with GroupNorm(8) and SiLU, and after the first convolution the shared embedding is projected by a per-block linear layer to a channel-wise $(\mathrm{scale}, \mathrm{shift})$ pair applied as $h \mapsto h\,(1+\mathrm{scale}) + \mathrm{shift}$. One network thus represents the whole time-dependent velocity field $v_\theta(x, t)$.
  \item \textbf{Cosmology and redshift, once.} The 8-vector $\mathbf{c}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cosmo_z} passes through a two-layer MLP and is added, broadcast over space, into the $512$-channel bottleneck latent. The MLP's last layer is zero-initialised, so conditioning starts as an exact no-op and the conditioned model is architecturally identical to the unconditioned one at initialisation -- keeping training stable and the ablation clean.
\end{itemize}
The output head is likewise zero-initialised, so the untrained model predicts $v = 0$ (``stay at $x_0$'') -- the correct baseline from which to learn a correction. Training uses Adam (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, scaled linearly with the number of data-parallel ranks), cosine-annealed over the epochs, gradient clipping at norm 1, and PyTorch DDP across the four GPUs of a node (\texttt{train\_flow.py}). At inference, \texttt{apply\_flow.py} integrates $\dot x = v_\theta(x, t, \mathbf{c})$ with Euler steps, composes $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(x(1) - x_0)$ through the \emph{same} filter code used to build the training target, and reconstructs full skies via Figure~\ref{fig:tiling}.

\subsection{Diffusion Pipeline (EDM)}
\label{subsec:pipe-diffusion}

The third pipeline (\texttt{ml/diffusion/}) replaces the low$\to$high bridge with a genuinely generative process in the EDM formulation of \citeauthor{karras_elucidating_2022} (Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}): every sample starts from pure Gaussian noise, and the low map enters only as \emph{conditioning}. What is diffused is again not the full field but the high-pass residual $x_1 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}})$, for the reasons of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}; the conditioning, by contrast, is the \emph{full} low patch at all scales, channel-concatenated to the noisy input -- the denoiser needs the large-scale context to know which small-scale structure to synthesise, even though its output is band-limited.

\paragraph{Preconditioned denoiser.} The network is the two-channel variant of Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}, wrapped in the EDM preconditioning (\texttt{model.EDMPrecond}). Here $x$ (analogous to $x_t$ in flow matching) is the noisy high-pass residual whose clean target is $x_1 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{high}-\delta_{low})$, and $\delta_{low}$ is the low-resolution conditioning map (used at all scales) that steers the denoiser.
\begin{equation}
  D_\theta(x, \sigma, \mathrm{cond}) = c_{\mathrm{skip}}(\sigma)\,x
    + c_{\mathrm{out}}(\sigma)\, F_\theta\!\big(\,[\,c_{\mathrm{in}}(\sigma)\,x \,\oplus\, \delta_\mathrm{low}\,],\ c_{\mathrm{noise}}(\sigma),\ \mathbf{c}\big),
  \label{eq:edm_precond}
\end{equation}
with the standard coefficients
\begin{equation}
  c_{\mathrm{skip}} = \frac{\sigma_d^2}{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{\sigma\,\sigma_d}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{noise}} = \tfrac14 \ln \sigma,
  \label{eq:edm_coeffs}
\end{equation}
\cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Each coefficient has a distinct job, and together they let \emph{one} network operate across five orders of magnitude in noise level (Figure~\ref{fig:edm_sampling}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item $c_{\mathrm{in}}$ normalises the input: $x = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$ has variance $\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2$, so $c_{\mathrm{in}}\,x$ has unit variance at \emph{every} noise level -- the raw network $F_\theta$ never has to adapt its operating range to $\sigma$.
  \item $c_{\mathrm{skip}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}}$ blend an identity path with the network output. At small $\sigma$, $c_{\mathrm{skip}} \to 1$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}} \to 0$: the noisy input is already almost the answer, and the network only supplies a small correction. At large $\sigma$, $c_{\mathrm{skip}} \to 0$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}} \to \sigma_d$: the input is essentially pure noise, and the network must synthesise the answer outright. The denoiser thus interpolates \emph{continuously} between ``pass through'' and ``generate'', instead of one set of weights having to learn both regimes unaided.
  \item $c_{\mathrm{noise}}$ log-compresses $\sigma \in [0.002, 80]$ into an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ scalar, which is what the sinusoidal embedding of Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}(a) receives -- the diffusion counterpart of the flow time $t$.
\end{itemize}
The conditioning channel ($\delta_{low}$) is neither noised nor rescaled by $c_{\mathrm{in}}$ -- it is not part of the diffusion process, it only steers it. One EDM default is deliberately \emph{not} adopted: the data scale $\sigma_d$, tuned to $0.5$ for 8-bit natural images in \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}, is instead \emph{measured} as the standard deviation of the actual training target -- the high-pass residual, whose variance differs materially from a natural image's -- over a few real batches at start-up (\texttt{estimate\_sigma\_data}); using the wrong $\sigma_d$ would mis-scale all four coefficients of Eq.~\eqref{eq:edm_coeffs} at once.

\paragraph{Training.} Per example, a noise level is drawn as $\ln\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(-1.2, 1.2^2)$, the target is noised to $x_t = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$, and the denoising error is weighted per noise level and normalised per patch:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, \lambda(\sigma)\,
      \frac{\big\lVert D_\theta(x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon,\ \sigma,\ \delta_{\mathrm{low}}) - x_1 \big\rVert^2}
           {\max\!\big(\mathrm{Var}(x_1)/\overline{\mathrm{Var}},\ 0.1\big)} \right],
  \qquad
  \lambda(\sigma) = \frac{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}{(\sigma\,\sigma_d)^2},
  \label{eq:edm_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda(\sigma)$ is EDM's weighting that equalises gradient magnitude across noise levels \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}, and the bounded per-patch normalisation is the same device -- and the same measured motivation -- as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_loss}. The bound is essential here: an unbounded $1/\mathrm{Var}$ divisor compounds multiplicatively with $\lambda(\sigma)$'s own dynamic range, and the combination was observed to produce loss spikes that prevented convergence entirely. The optimiser setup mirrors the flow pipeline's.

\paragraph{Sampling.} Inference follows EDM's deterministic Algorithm 1 (\texttt{model.sample\_heun}): the Karras noise schedule
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_i = \Big(\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho} + \tfrac{i}{N-1}\big(\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho} - \sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho}\big)\Big)^{\rho},
  \qquad \rho = 7,\ \ \sigma \in [0.002,\ 80],
  \label{eq:karras_schedule}
\end{equation}
integrated with Heun's second-order method over $N = 32$ steps from $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:edm_sampling}). The step count is not a tuning failure but the point of this pipeline: a trajectory from pure noise is genuinely curved (Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}), and with up to two denoiser calls per Heun step, this is the most expensive of the three corrections per shell. The corrected patch is composed exactly as in the flow pipeline, $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{sample})$, and full skies are reconstructed with the shared global noise field and $p=32$ blending of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches} -- the sampler is deterministic given (noise, conditioning), which is precisely what makes overlapping tiles consistent.

In summary, the three pipelines answer the same question at three levels of ambition: \emph{transfer} restores the power spectrum by construction but cannot restore structure; \emph{unet-flow} transports each patch along a short, nearly straight bridge and restores stochastic small-scale power at moderate cost; \emph{diffusion} generates the missing band from noise through a full multi-step sampler, at the highest cost. All three inherit the same guarantees from the shared formulation -- large scales pinned to Disco-DJ, corrections confined to the scales where the deficit lives, and evaluation on held-out cosmologies. Running them at production scale is the subject of the next section.

\subsection{Local Development}
\label{sec:local_dev}

Both codes were first set up on a local machine to establish a minimal working configuration with an interactive debugging loop: a bug in the shell-builder logic of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder} or the IC hand-off of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} is caught in seconds, rather than after a queued cluster job has run to completion. Tests at $16^3 = 4096$ particles complete in under a minute on a single core; such a run is not physically meaningful, it exercises the full pipeline -- initial conditions, integration, lightcone accumulation -- end to end before committing to a production configuration.

The binding constraint at production size is memory. Storing only the raw phase-space state -- six single-precision components per particle -- for a CosmoGridV1-sized run gives
\begin{equation}
    N_p = 832^3 \approx 5.8\times10^{8}, \qquad
    N_p \times 24\ \text{bytes} \approx 14\ \text{GB},
    \label{eq:phasespace_mem}
\end{equation}
already beyond a typical workstation's RAM once any working headroom is included -- and this floor is exceeded differently by the two codes. \textsc{PkdGrav3} carries, on top of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phasespace_mem}, its per-particle tree, per-particle rung state, and domain-decomposition buffers (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), so its footprint grows with tree depth and rung occupancy. Disco-DJ is instead bounded by its force mesh: the Poisson solve is one global FFT (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), so the whole PM grid -- density, its Fourier transform, and force components -- must be resident simultaneously, and the demand scales with the chosen mesh resolution rather than the particle count. Production runs of either code therefore require a cluster.

\subsection{Euler (ETH Zurich)}

On Euler, each code gets its own virtual environment: Disco-DJ is built on JAX and needs a matched GPU software stack, while \textsc{PkdGrav3} is a compiled tree code with its own toolchain -- a per-code split that avoids the version conflicts a shared environment invites. Euler provides the memory and GPU partitions for the full $832^3$ resolution; the GPU acceleration specifically benefits Disco-DJ, whose entire force solve is GPU-native, whereas the \textsc{PkdGrav3} build used here is CPU-bound. Long-running jobs are distributed across multiple nodes where the code supports it.

\subsection{CSCS (Alps)}

The largest runs use the CSCS Alps system, whose NVIDIA GH200 nodes provide 120~GB of on-package memory each. This directly addresses Disco-DJ's binding constraint: since its memory demand tracks the PM mesh (Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}), a larger memory pool translates into a finer force mesh -- and hence better small-scale force resolution -- per node. Production runs use \texttt{RES\_PM} $= 1664 = 2\times832$ mesh cells per dimension at the CosmoGridV1 box and particle count (Appendix~\ref{app:slurm}). The same nodes also host the correction-pipeline training of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}, where the memory allows large per-GPU batches of $256^2$ patches, and DDP training spans the four GPUs of a node.

\section{Software Engineering}
\subsection{Code and Data Layout}

Executables and virtual environments live in the cluster's backed-up but quota-limited \texttt{HOME}; simulation snapshots, shell maps, per-job logs, and diagnostic plots -- bulk, regenerable output -- go to \texttt{SCRATCH}, which trades persistence for effectively unbounded (periodically purged) capacity. Keeping every submission's full log proved valuable for debugging and cross-run comparison alike: a run's log is often the only remaining record of exactly which configuration produced a given output. The CosmoGridV1 reference dataset ($\sim110$~TB) was transferred to CSCS with a managed Globus transfer job, which runs unattended and recovers from dropped connections -- an interactive single-stream copy at this scale would be both too slow and too fragile.

\subsection{Job Scheduling and Submission}

Every Python driver is paired with a Slurm batch script: the driver runs unchanged from the local tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev} up to a production allocation, while only the surrounding batch script changes. Jobs are launched interactively (\texttt{bash}) for fast-turnaround debugging or queued (\texttt{sbatch}) for long unattended runs. Each batch script follows the same two-part layout (Appendix~\ref{app:slurm}): a header of \texttt{\#SBATCH} directives declaring \emph{resources} (nodes, GPUs, task layout, exclusivity, wall-clock limit), \emph{accounting} (account, partition), and \emph{bookkeeping} (job name, working directory, \texttt{\%j}-tagged log paths so concurrent runs never overwrite each other) -- followed by a short execution body that activates the code-specific environment, fixes input paths (e.g.\ the \texttt{CosmoML.tipsy} IC of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and sets the physical parameters as shell variables (\texttt{RES=832}, \texttt{RES\_PM=1664}, \texttt{BOXSIZE=900.0}).

The resource header also fixes the job's cost against the allocation,
\begin{equation}
    C = n_{\mathrm{nodes}} \times t \quad [\text{node-hours}],
    \label{eq:node_hours}
\end{equation}
billed regardless of how much of $t$ is spent computing -- which is exactly why short single-node interactive sessions are used for debugging and exclusive multi-node allocations are reserved for production. One detail keeps the scripts scalable: the header reserves one task per node, and the execution body computes the real degree of parallelism as one process per GPU, \texttt{NTASKS=\$((SLURM\_NNODES * 4))}, passed explicitly to \texttt{srun}. Scaling a job up therefore means editing a single \texttt{--nodes} line; the process-per-GPU launch follows automatically.

\subsection{Version Control}

The whole project -- the analysis and ML pipelines together with the modified Disco-DJ containing the shell builder -- is kept in a dedicated Git repository. Beyond protection against machine failure, this gives a reliable way back to a known-good state when an experimental change breaks the pipeline, and keeps the many configuration variants organised.

\paragraph{Statistics and resolutions.} Every pipeline is scored with the same shared diagnostics (\texttt{ml/analysis/}): per-shell full-sky angular power spectra pooled into three redshift bins, example patches with per-patch 2D power ratios, one-point moments (variance, skewness, excess kurtosis) as a function of shell depth, and weak-lensing convergence maps. The $\kappa$ maps are built with the \textsc{UFalcon} lightcone integration \cite{sgier_fast_2018} (\texttt{ml/analysis/weak\_lensing.py}): all corrected shells within a source bin's redshift range are combined into one convergence map per cosmology at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=1024$ ($\ell \le 2048$), using the DES-Y3 \textsc{metacal} source redshift distributions of tomographic bins 1 ($z \lesssim 1.05$) and 4 ($z \lesssim 1.85$) -- the lowest- and highest-redshift bins, which weight the nearby (hardest) and distant (easiest) shells respectively. One resolution asymmetry must be kept in mind throughout: the transfer pipeline operates on the native $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$ shells up to $\ell_{\max}=3000$, whereas the two learned pipelines were trained and applied at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$ (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}), so their shell-level statistics extend only to $\ell \sim 1500$ and their $\kappa$ maps lack input power beyond the corresponding band limit.

\paragraph{Percentile bands.} The shaded bands in the $C_\ell$-ratio figures are \emph{percentile bands across the held-out evaluation set}, not error bars on a mean. For the shell-level grids (e.g.\ Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_cl}), five shells are sampled from each redshift bin for each of the ten cosmologies, and the ratio $C_\ell/C_\ell^{\mathrm{true}}$ is computed per (cosmology, shell) pair -- fifty samples per bin. The solid line is their median; the band spans the 16th to 84th percentile, i.e.\ the central 68\% of the actual cosmology-to-cosmology and shell-to-shell scatter. A narrow band therefore means the correction is \emph{uniformly} good across cosmologies and depths, not merely good on average; for the $\kappa$ figures the band is taken across the ten cosmologies at fixed $\ell$.

\section{Transfer-Function Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-transfer}

Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_loss} shows its loss: training and validation losses coincide throughout -- with only nine features and millions of rows, the emulator cannot memorise -- and converge within the first $\sim 10^4$ gradient steps to a best validation loss of $\tfrac12\mathrm{MSE} = 9.9\times10^{-5}$, corresponding to an RMS error on the predicted transfer function of $\sqrt{2 \times 9.9\times10^{-5}} \approx 1.4\%$ per $(\ell, s)$ sample.

\section{U-Net Flow-Matching Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-flow}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The flow was trained for 200 epochs on the $10^5$-patch dataset (Table~\ref{tab:training}), distributed over 16 DDP ranks (4 Alps nodes $\times$ 4 GH200), with the base learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$ scaled linearly to $4.8\times10^{-4}$ and cosine-annealed; the high-pass residual formulation used cutoff $0.05$ and transition $0.20$ of the patch Nyquist frequency. Figure~\ref{fig:flow_loss} shows the variance-normalised flow-matching loss of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_loss}: validation improves until epoch $\approx 110$ (best $0.166$ at epoch 109 -- the checkpoint used for everything below) and then drifts slowly upward while the training loss continues to fall, i.e.\ mild but genuine overfitting past that point. The residual plateau is expected rather than alarming: at intermediate $t$ the target velocity is not uniquely determined by the network's input, so the regression loss is bounded below by this irreducible conditional variance.

\section{Diffusion Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-diffusion}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The diffusion model shares the flow's data, architecture width, schedule length, and high-pass formulation (Table~\ref{tab:training}); it differs in the objective (Eq.~\ref{eq:edm_loss}, with the measured $\sigma_d = 1.006$), the unscaled learning rate of $3\times10^{-5}$, and the 32-step Heun sampler with shared sphere noise and $p=32$ blending at inference. Its loss curve (Figure~\ref{fig:diff_loss}) looks qualitatively different from the flow's: the EDM-weighted denoising loss collapses from $\approx 5.9$ to $\approx 0.9$ within the first $\sim 15$ epochs and is then almost flat, with training and validation coinciding to the end (best validation $0.80$ at epoch 165) -- no overfitting is observed. This is characteristic of the objective: every example is presented at a freshly drawn noise level and noise realisation, an implicit augmentation that makes memorisation far harder than for the flow's fixed low$\to$high pairs.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:res-synthesis}

Three consistent messages emerge. First, \emph{every} pipeline repairs the two-point statistics on the scales it acts on: the transfer function holds shell and convergence spectra within a few percent out to the native $\ell = 3000$ resolution at almost no inference cost, and the two learned pipelines do the same within their $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$ evaluation band. Second, the statistics beyond two-point cleanly separate the deterministic from the generative route: skewness and kurtosis -- at shell level and $\kappa$ level alike -- are untouched by the amplitude rescale but restored by both generative models, with the flow essentially exact and the diffusion slightly conservative. Third, the two generative models trade differently at the convergence level: the flow gives the most precise moments and the tightest bands, the diffusion the flattest high-$\ell$ spectra. The comparison between transfer and the learned pipelines at the highest multipoles is not yet apples-to-apples -- the learned corrections were evaluated at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, and extending them to the native resolution is the most direct next step (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}).

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis set out to answer a practical question at the heart of map-level, simulation-based cosmological inference: can the small-scale accuracy of an expensive reference $N$-body lightcone be restored onto a cheap particle-mesh lightcone, well enough for the statistics such analyses actually consume? The answer, on held-out cosmologies spanning the full CosmoGridV1 $w$CDM grid, is yes -- with the qualification that \emph{which} correction suffices depends on \emph{which} statistic must be right.

The foundation was infrastructural. By reconstructing each CosmoGridV1 cosmology's exact linear input -- transfer function, $\sigma_8$, and radiation density, massive neutrinos included -- and letting \textsc{PkdGrav3} generate one backscaled initial condition consumed byte-identically by both solvers (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and by embedding a shell builder into Disco-DJ's time integration that reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition on CosmoGridV1's radial shell grid within constant memory (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}), the learning problem was reduced to its irreducible core: paired full-sky lightcones differing only through the force solver. Every result in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} rests on this controlled setup.

On these pairs, three corrections were built (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}), sharing one measured insight -- Disco-DJ's small-scale deficit is primarily one of \emph{amplitude}, its phases remaining correlated with the truth deep into the nonlinear regime -- and one shared guarantee, the high-pass residual formulation that pins the already-correct large scales to the fast simulation exactly. The deterministic transfer-function pipeline holds shell and weak-lensing convergence spectra within a few percent of the reference out to the native $\ell = 3000$, at essentially zero inference cost; it is the method of choice wherever two-point statistics suffice. What it cannot do -- by construction, not by failure -- is generate the one-point non-Gaussianity of the missing structure. The two generative pipelines close exactly that gap: both the flow-matching U-Net and the EDM diffusion model restore skewness and excess kurtosis at shell and convergence level on cosmologies they never saw, the flow essentially exactly and with the tightest cosmology-to-cosmology scatter, the diffusion slightly conservatively but with the flattest high-multipole convergence spectra. That a 32-step stochastic sampler, evaluated on thousands of overlapping tiles sharing a single global noise field, reassembles into consistent full-sky maps also validates the overlap-tile machinery itself as a general route for applying patch-based generative models to the sphere.

Several limitations bound these conclusions and set the agenda. The learned pipelines were trained and evaluated at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, so their corrections currently stop short of the native $\ell = 3000$ band where the transfer function was scored -- retraining on native-resolution patches is the most direct extension, and the patch machinery already supports it. The corrected fields are continuous overdensities, not integer count maps, which downstream weak-lensing statistics tolerate but a consumer requiring per-pixel count validity would not. The corrections were validated on matter shells and derived convergence maps at fixed total neutrino mass, without baryonic feedback -- which operates on precisely the scales corrected here and would need its own treatment \cite{schneider_matter_2015, fluri_full_2022}. And each cosmology entered with a single realisation, so realisation-to-realisation generalisation at fixed cosmology remains to be quantified.

The direction, however, is clear. A Disco-DJ lightcone costs minutes where the reference costs node-hours, and the corrections add almost nothing to that -- so corrected fast lightcones can populate the simulation demand of deep-learning analyses of current surveys \cite{thomsen_dark_2026, fluri_full_2022} at a small fraction of the reference suite's cost, reserving full $N$-body runs for validation rather than production. The longer-term prospect is tighter still: Disco-DJ is differentiable end-to-end \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}, and a differentiable corrected forward model -- from cosmological parameters through the corrected lightcone to the map-level statistic -- would put gradient-based inference within reach of the same pipeline.
