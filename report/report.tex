\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Map-level, simulation-based inference is becoming the standard way to extract cosmological information from large-scale-structure surveys. Working with the field itself rather than with compressed summary statistics retains the non-Gaussian information that gravitational collapse imprints on small scales, allows systematic effects to be modelled in the forward direction where they physically act, and lets different probes be described self-consistently within a single simulated universe \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024, gatti_dark_2024, lemos_simbig_2023}. The cost is borne entirely by simulation: the likelihood is never written down, so every analysis consumes a suite of forward-modelled mocks spanning the parameter space. Gravity-only lightcone suites built for exactly this purpose exist -- CosmoGridV1 \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023} among them -- but each is a \emph{fixed} grid of cosmologies, and each of its realisations is an \mbox{$N$}-body run costing many node-hours. A recent DES-Y3 analysis built on that suite required of order one million map-level mocks \cite{thomsen_dark_2026}; reproducing such coverage at the volume Stage-IV surveys demand is prohibitive.

Fast approximate solvers are the natural alternative. Particle-mesh codes such as Disco-DJ \cite{list_disco-dj_2026} evolve the same initial conditions in minutes rather than hours, but their force resolution is capped by the mesh, so they progressively lose small-scale power -- precisely the regime that motivated working at the map level. This thesis asks whether that deficit can be repaired after the fact: whether a learned correction can restore the small-scale content of an expensive reference simulation onto a cheap one, accurately enough for the statistics such analyses actually consume.

Answering it required first constructing a controlled comparison. CosmoGridV1 does not
distribute initial conditions, so for every cosmology the linear input is reconstructed
from the published perturbations and handed to \textsc{PkdGrav3}, which generates a
backscaled particle load; the Disco-DJ run is started from that load, while the reference
remains CosmoGridV1's own lightcone. A shell builder embedded in Disco-DJ's time
integration reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition on
CosmoGridV1's exact radial shell grid, so the two are defined shell by shell on identical
radial bins. The result is paired full-sky lightcones that differ only through the force solver. On these pairs three corrections are trained and compared on held-out cosmologies: a deterministic per-multipole transfer-function rescaling in harmonic space, a conditional flow-matching U-Net that transports each map patch from low to high fidelity, and a conditional diffusion model in the Elucidated Diffusion Model (EDM) formulation, which generates the missing small-scale residual from noise.

Evaluated on a common footing, all three restore the angular power spectrum to within a few percent on the scales they act on. The two generative models additionally recover most of the non-Gaussian one-point and weak-lensing convergence signal that a deterministic rescaling by construction cannot: the flow lifts the convergence excess kurtosis from \mbox{$0.54$} to \mbox{$0.98$} of its reference value, where the transfer function reaches only \mbox{$0.64$}. Corrected fast lightcones thus approach the reference simulations at a small fraction of their cost, which is what would allow full \mbox{$N$}-body runs to be reserved for validation rather than production.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Modern cosmology rests on a remarkably economical model. Six parameters suffice for \mbox{$\Lambda$}CDM: the densities of cold dark matter and baryons, the Hubble constant, the amplitude and tilt of the primordial fluctuation spectrum, and the optical depth to reionisation. With these it accounts for observations spanning the age of the Universe, from the temperature and polarisation anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background and the abundance of the light elements to the clustering of galaxies, the growth of structure, and the accelerating expansion inferred by distant supernovae \cite{dodelson_modern_2020}. Two of its ingredients have no explanation in the Standard Model of particle physics: the cold dark matter that dominates the matter budget, and the cosmological constant that drives the acceleration. The sharpest tests of both come from how structure grows \cite{huterer_growth_2023}.

The large-scale structure (LSS) of the Universe is the cosmic web of halos, filaments, sheets and voids traced by galaxies and dark matter. It has been mapped observationally since the first redshift slices revealed that the galaxy distribution is not random \cite{de_lapparent_slice_1986}, and is understood theoretically as the anisotropic collapse of Gaussian initial fluctuations \cite{bond_how_1996, libeskind_tracing_2018}. It is also one of the richest probes of cosmology available, because its growth over cosmic time is sensitive to the matter density \mbox{$\Omega_m$}, the clustering amplitude \mbox{$\sigma_8$} and the dark-energy equation of state \mbox{$w_0$} (Sec.~\ref{sec:cosmic_web}) \cite{dodelson_modern_2020, huterer_growth_2023}. Current and upcoming cosmological surveys -- the Dark Energy Survey \cite{gatti_dark_2024}, DESI \cite{collaboration_desi_2016}, Euclid \cite{collaboration_euclid_2025} and the Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time \cite{ivezic_lsst_2019} -- now measure this structure over large fractions of the sky, and the most powerful analyses no longer compress the data to two-point functions, or to other summary statistics such as weak-lensing peaks and the baryon-acoustic-oscillation scale \cite{eisenstein_baryonic_1998}; they work at the \emph{map level}, extracting non-Gaussian information with deep networks trained on simulations \cite{fluri_full_2022, jeffrey_dark_2024, gatti_dark_2024, thomsen_dark_2026}.

Three recent analyses show what this buys and what it costs. \citeauthor{jeffrey_dark_2024} compress DES-Y3 weak-lensing mass maps with a neural network trained on simulations and infer \mbox{$w$}CDM parameters without ever writing down a likelihood, the compression itself being learned rather than chosen \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024}. \citeauthor{gatti_dark_2024} instead compare families of hand-built non-Gaussian statistics on the same maps -- wavelet harmonics, scattering transforms and map moments -- and find that combining them tightens \mbox{$S_8$} well beyond what the Gaussian two-point information supports \cite{gatti_dark_2024}. \textsc{SimBIG} carries the same idea to galaxy clustering, performing inference directly at the field level from a forward model built on \mbox{$N$}-body simulations with a halo-occupation prescription \cite{lemos_simbig_2023}. What unites them is the cost structure: the statistic is cheap, and the simulation suite that calibrates it is not. The DES-Y3 analysis closest to this thesis makes the scale explicit -- it builds its forward model on CosmoGridV1 and requires of order one million map-level mock realisations \cite{thomsen_dark_2026}.

The term \emph{simulation-based} covers two distinct things, and it is worth separating them. The first is the absence of a prediction from first principles. For the linear power spectrum one exists: an Einstein--Boltzmann solver integrates the perturbation equations directly and returns \mbox{$P(k)$} without simulating anything \cite{seljak_line_1996}. At the map level there is no such shortcut. The statistics of an evolved, non-linear density field on the sky can only be obtained by evolving it end to end: a many-body system under gravity, which is what CosmoGridV1 does \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023, potter_pkdgrav3_2016}, or, where baryonic physics must be modelled explicitly rather than absorbed into a correction, full hydrodynamics -- for which the suites reaching the volume and resolution a cosmological analysis needs currently number of order ten realisations, far too few to serve as a theory prediction across a parameter space.

The second is how the likelihood is then treated, which is a separate choice. One can write down an explicit form for it and evaluate it at the field level, or decline to write it at all and let a neural network learn the likelihood or the posterior from simulated pairs -- neural likelihood and neural posterior estimation, the route \citeauthor{jeffrey_dark_2024} take for DES-Y3 \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024}. Both consume the same resource: entire suites of forward-simulated lightcones spanning the parameter space, each realisation an expensive \textsc{PkdGrav3} run (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}) \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. It is this demand, not the inference machinery, that sets the cost of a map-level analysis.

Fast approximate solvers are a promising alternative. Two established examples show the range of approach. COLA hands the large-scale motion to Lagrangian perturbation theory, which knows it analytically, and asks the integrator only for what perturbation theory cannot supply, so that a handful of time steps suffices \cite{tassev_solving_2013}. FastPM instead constrains a particle-mesh solver so that the linear growth comes out right however few steps it is given \cite{feng_fastpm_2016}. Disco-DJ belongs to the same family but is the most modern of them, written for GPUs and differentiable end to end \cite{hahn_disco-dj_2024, list_disco-dj_2026}, which is why it is the solver used here. Such particle-mesh (PM) codes evolve the same initial conditions in minutes on a single GPU (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), but their force resolution is capped by the mesh: small-scale power is progressively suppressed \cite{schneider_matter_2015}, and with it exactly the non-Gaussian content that motivates map-level inference in the first place. The question this thesis addresses is therefore: can the small-scale content of an expensive reference simulation be restored onto a cheap particle-mesh lightcone, accurately enough for map-level statistics, at negligible additional cost?

Answering it requires two ingredients that are as much infrastructure as machine learning. First, a controlled learning problem: the low- and high-fidelity maps must differ \emph{only} through the force solver, which demands byte-identical initial conditions (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}) and lightcones defined on identical radial shells -- the latter provided by a shell builder implemented inside Disco-DJ's time integration (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}), since the code's native lightcone support does not reach CosmoGridV1's depth.

The second ingredient is the corrections themselves (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}). Three are built and compared here, and they were not chosen arbitrarily: they span a deliberate range, from an emulation technique the community already trusts to two generative approaches that are new to this particular problem.

The first is the conservative baseline. Rescaling mode amplitudes so that a cheap simulation reproduces the power spectrum of an expensive one is standard practice: it is how fast solvers are calibrated against full \mbox{$N$}-body runs \cite{feng_fastpm_2016}, how initial-condition transients are removed \cite{garrison_improving_2016}, and how a single simulation is rescaled to other cosmologies \cite{angulo_one_2009}. Its appeal here is directness. The quantity being learned is the transfer function between the high- and low-fidelity runs -- a per-multipole, per-shell gain -- and that gain is fitted against the angular power spectrum itself, which is the statistic the corrected lightcones are ultimately scored on. The method therefore optimises exactly what is measured, with no proxy objective in between. A small network predicts the gain from the cosmological parameters, which is what allows it to be applied to cosmologies never run at high fidelity.

Its limitation is equally clear, and is the reason for the other two. A per-mode gain multiplies amplitudes and leaves phases untouched, so it can restore how much power sits at each scale but not the spatial arrangement that carries it. Where the fast solver has placed structure correctly this is exactly right, and Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} shows that it usually has. Where the two simulations have genuinely decorrelated, however, rescaling amplifies the fast solver's own noise rather than recovering the missing structure, and no amount of tuning changes that: the one-point statistics a map-level analysis consumes are precisely the ones a phase-preserving operation cannot manufacture.

Generative models are the natural answer, because they sample rather than predict. A regression trained on paired maps converges to the conditional mean, which is smooth by construction and under-disperses exactly the small-scale variance we are trying to restore; a generative model instead draws a field with the right statistics, whose small-scale structure is fresh rather than copied. The idea has a short but encouraging history in cosmology. Deep networks have been trained to super-resolve cosmological fields \cite{ramanah_super-resolution_2020, li_ai-assisted_2020, ni_ai-assisted_2021} and to emulate \mbox{$N$}-body displacements directly \cite{he_learning_2018, jamieson_field_2022}; diffusion models have more recently joined them \cite{mudur_can_2022, rouhiainen_super-resolution_2023}, and have been extended to galaxy surveys at the field level \cite{cuesta-lazaro_point_2024}.

Two such models are used here rather than one, because they differ in a way that matters. Conditional flow matching \cite{lipman_flow_2023, liu_flow_2022} learns a velocity field transporting one distribution into another, and can therefore be started from the low-fidelity map itself: the model has only to travel the short distance between the two fidelities. A diffusion model in the Elucidated Diffusion Model (EDM) formulation \cite{karras_elucidating_2022} starts instead from pure noise and generates the missing small-scale component outright, conditioned on the low-fidelity map. The first is cheaper and inherits the fast solver's structure by construction; the second is more expensive but is a genuinely generative process, and is the better bet on the shells where the two simulations have decorrelated most. Whether that theoretical advantage survives contact with the data is one of the questions Chapter~\ref{ch:results} answers.

The remainder of this chapter provides the background these methods rest on: the cosmological framework and the simulation codes (Sec.~\ref{sec:theoretical-background}), followed by the machine-learning concepts -- U-Nets, flow matching, and diffusion -- that the correction pipelines instantiate (Sec.~\ref{sec:sr-methods}).


\section{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:theoretical-background}
\subsection{Cosmological Framework}
\subsubsection{The Cosmic Web}
\label{sec:cosmic_web}

On scales of a few to a few hundred megaparsecs, matter in the Universe is not smoothly distributed but arranged into an interconnected network of dense compact knots, elongated filaments, two-dimensional sheets, and large under-dense voids, collectively known as the \emph{cosmic web} \cite{bond_how_1996, libeskind_tracing_2018}. The name is due to \citeauthor{bond_how_1996}, and their argument was more than descriptive. The filament-dominated pattern can already be read out of the tidal field of the initial, still-linear perturbations, which means gravitational evolution does not invent the web at all. It sharpens an outline that was present from the beginning \cite{bond_how_1996}. The same gravitational instability that seeds this pattern is responsible for its growth: initially small density fluctuations are amplified by their own self-gravity, so that overdense regions contract while voids expand and come to dominate the volume of the Universe \cite{bond_how_1996}. The pattern was first seen directly in the CfA redshift survey \cite{de_lapparent_slice_1986}, and large galaxy surveys -- 2dFGRS \cite{colless_2df_2001}, SDSS \cite{york_sloan_2000}, and the 2MASS \cite{huchra_2mass_2012} redshift survey among them -- as well as cosmological $N$-body simulations both recover this same web-like arrangement of galaxies and dark matter \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}.

Gravitational instability drives the emergence of anisotropic structure: because collapse along the direction of a region's strongest tidal compression proceeds fastest, an initially only mildly non-spherical overdensity first flattens into a sheet, then contracts further into a filament, and only finally collapses fully into a compact halo. The sequence of anisotropic collapse was first described by Zel'dovich \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. This picture can be made quantitative through the local tidal (or velocity-shear) field: following the tidal-tensor classification reviewed by \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}, one computes the Hessian of the (rescaled) gravitational potential $\phi$,
\begin{equation}
  T_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x_\alpha \partial x_\beta}, \qquad \nabla^2 \phi = \delta,
  \label{eq:tidal_tensor}
\end{equation}
where $\delta = (\rho - \bar\rho)/\bar\rho$ is the dimensionless matter overdensity and $\phi$ has been normalised by $4\pi G \bar\rho$ so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:tidal_tensor} takes the form of the Poisson equation \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. Diagonalising the (real, symmetric) tidal tensor at each point gives three eigenvalues $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \lambda_3$; counting how many of them exceed a chosen threshold $\lambda_{\rm th}$ classifies that point as part of a void (zero eigenvalues above threshold), a sheet (one), a filament (two), or a knot (three) \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. Voids, sheets, filaments and knots are thus not four independent morphologies but a nested hierarchy, each embedded within the next.

\subsubsection{Matter Power Spectrum and Transfer Function}
\label{sec:power_spectrum}

While the tidal-tensor classification above describes the cosmic web \emph{qualitatively}, its statistical properties are better quantified through the matter power spectrum $P(k)$, which for a Gaussian field is a complete description, as the small scales are not Gaussian, $P(k)$ does not fully characterise the late-time matter field, and the higher-order statistics of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are precisely what the generative corrections are built to restore. Writing the density contrast in Fourier space at a fixed time as $\delta(\mathbf{k})$
and assuming statistical homogeneity and isotropy, the power spectrum at that time is defined through
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle \delta(\mathbf{k})\, \delta^*(\mathbf{k}')\big\rangle
  = (2\pi)^3\, \delta_D(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}')\, P(k),
  \label{eq:power_spectrum_def}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_D$ is the Dirac delta and the angle brackets denote an ensemble average over realisations of the initial conditions \cite{dodelson_modern_2020, huterer_growth_2023}. In a simplified form it can be written as
\begin{equation}
    P(k) \propto k^{n_s}\,T^2(k),
\end{equation}
with $n_s$ the scalar spectral index \cite{eisenstein_baryonic_1998, huterer_growth_2023}.
Expanding the fitting formula of \cite{bardeen_statistics_1986} on large scales,
$k \ll k_{\mathrm{eq}}$, gives
\begin{equation}
    T(k) = 1 - \alpha_1 \left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)
             + \alpha_2 \left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)^{2}
             + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}}\right),
    \qquad
    k_{\mathrm{eq}} = H_0\,\sqrt{\frac{2\Omega_m}{\Omega_r}},
    \label{eq:transfer_expansion}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$ are fixed numerical coefficients set by the shape of the fitting formula, and $k_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is the wavenumber of the mode that enters the horizon at matter--radiation equality.

The transfer function is therefore shaped by the energy content of the Universe at each epoch: the scale $k_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is set by matter--radiation equality -- the crossing of the radiation and matter curves in Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function} -- and massive neutrinos modify this budget in a scale-dependent way. The red curves of Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function} show each mass state tracking the photons while it is still relativistic and turning over to a matter-like dilution once it is not. What matters for \mbox{$T(k)$} happens in between: a neutrino that is fast but no longer relativistic streams freely out of any perturbation smaller than the distance it can cross, so it never falls into small-scale potential wells and contributes nothing to their growth. Below that free-streaming scale the transfer function is suppressed accordingly \cite{lesgourgues_neutrino_2012}.


The suppression propagates from $P(k)$ into every projected statistic, in particular the angular power spectrum $C_\ell$, which is (schematically) a line-of-sight projection of the three-dimensional power,
\begin{equation}
    C_\ell \approx \int k^2\, P(k)\, \mathrm{d}k,
    \label{eq:cl}
\end{equation}
\cite{seljak_line_1996}, and $C_\ell$ is the statistic on which the lightcone maps of this thesis are ultimately scored (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}).

The dependence on the neutrino mass is not a side note here: it reappears directly when generating the initial conditions for the simulations in Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}, since matching the transfer function, neutrinos included, is a precondition for those initial conditions to be consistent with CosmoGridV1. More generally, obtaining \mbox{$P(k)$} precisely requires solving the coupled linear Einstein--Boltzmann equations for the perturbed Friedmann--Lema\^itre--Robertson--Walker metric and the phase-space distributions of all matter and radiation species. The system solved by the \textsc{class} code used in Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} and by the differentiable Einstein--Boltzmann module of the Disco-DJ framework \cite{hahn_disco-dj_2024}.

\subsection{Cosmological Simulations}

Cosmological $N$-body simulations have historically tracked, and in some respects driven, the development of high-performance computing: the number of particles that can be evolved in a fixed wall-clock time has grown by many orders of magnitude, both through raw hardware improvements and through continual gains in the efficiency of the gravity solvers themselves \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. The scientific payoff lies in modelling the LSS of the Universe to the non-linear precision that the cosmological surveys require. LSS observables are particularly sensitive to the present-day matter density $\Omega_m$ and the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$; because a lightcone simulation can be sliced into tomographic redshift bins, it further tracks the growth of structure over cosmic time, which is what makes such simulations sensitive to the dark-energy equation-of-state parameter $w_0$ and hence to the physical origin of cosmic acceleration \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}.

The method itself is simple to state. The continuous matter distribution is discretised into a finite set of massive \emph{superparticles}, each standing for a large parcel of the underlying phase-space distribution, and those particles are advanced under their mutual gravity \cite{dehnen_n-body_2011}. Richer physics can be layered on -- gas dynamics, radiative cooling, star formation, feedback -- and the hydrodynamical suites that result are what galaxy-formation studies rest on \cite{vogelsberger_cosmological_2019}, at rapidly increasing cost. For the weak-lensing and clustering statistics considered here, a gravity-only treatment is an adequate approximation, and gravity-only (\mbox{$N$}-body) simulation is the central tool of this work.

Two choices then fix the run: the initial conditions, drawn from a linear Einstein--Boltzmann solution (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and the algorithm that evaluates the gravitational force at each step. The force solver is where the codes differ from one another, and the established approaches trade resolution against cost in different ways \cite{dehnen_n-body_2011}. A particle mesh solves the Poisson equation on a grid with a single FFT: fast, but with the force resolution capped at the cell size; P$^3$M (particle--particle/particle-mesh) schemes add a direct short-range summation on top to recover part of it, refining the mesh where clustering is strongest \cite{couchman_mesh-refined_1991}. The particle mesh is the scheme behind the fast solvers named at the start of this chapter -- COLA \cite{tassev_solving_2013}, FastPM \cite{feng_fastpm_2016} and Disco-DJ \cite{list_disco-dj_2026} -- which is both what makes them fast and what caps their resolution. Hierarchical trees and fast multipole expansions instead group distant particles rather than gridding them, at \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$} and \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N)$} cost respectively \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}: \textsc{Gadget} pairs a tree with a particle mesh \cite{springel_simulating_2021}, and \textsc{PkdGrav3}, which produced the reference suite used here, uses a fast multipole method (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}) \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. Adaptive-mesh refinement takes a third route, refining the grid where the density is high, as in \textsc{Ramses} \cite{teyssier_cosmological_2002}.

Species other than cold dark matter and baryons complicate the picture: photons, massive neutrinos and dark energy contribute to the expansion history and, on large scales, to the gravitational potential, but they are not represented by the particle load. Gravity-only codes therefore either fold them into the background expansion alone, or carry them as separate \emph{linear} fields evolved alongside the particles \cite{dakin_cosmological_2022}. Which of the two routes a code takes turns out to matter a great deal for this thesis, and is taken up in Sec.~\ref{subsec:ic_linear}.

This thesis works between the two ends of that design space, and both solvers are described in turn below: the particle-mesh Disco-DJ (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), fast enough to generate the large volume of training data the approach requires, and the tree-based \textsc{PkdGrav3} (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), which anchors the accurate CosmoGridV1 reference simulations. 

\subsubsection{Lightcones and Snapshots}
\label{sec:lightcones}

An $N$-body simulation naturally outputs \emph{snapshots}: the full particle phase-space (positions and velocities) within a periodic box of fixed comoving side length, at a fixed time. Reproducing how we actually observe the Universe, however, requires a \emph{lightcone}: an observer at a fixed spacetime point receives light emitted from progressively more distant (and hence earlier) regions as one looks to higher redshift, so a simulated lightcone must record particles at the redshift-dependent comoving distance from which they are being observed, not at a single global time. Because the lightcone is organised by radius in this way, it is naturally stored as a set of \emph{shells}: concentric radial slices centred on the observer, each spanning a narrow range of comoving distance and therefore of redshift, and each recorded as a full-sky map of the particles that crossed the lightcone within it \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}.

Because the depth of the desired lightcone (out to some $z_{\max}$) is typically larger than a single simulation box, the box is periodically replicated around a centrally placed observer: replication exploits the fact that a simulation with periodic boundary conditions can, in principle, represent an arbitrarily large volume, at the cost of the same structures reappearing at multiple replica images along a given line of sight \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. The trade-off between box size, structure repetition, and the resulting bias or excess variance in projected lightcone statistics is discussed in more detail by \citeauthor{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023} \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}, who additionally introduce a shell-permutation scheme that mitigates the repeated-structure problem by drawing different radial shells of a single lightcone from independent simulations of the same cosmology. The shell builder developed in this thesis (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}) implements the same replication idea directly inside the time integration, rather than as a post-processing step.

\subsubsection{Disco-DJ}
\label{sec:discodj}

Disco-DJ (\emph{Differentiable Simulations for Cosmology, Done with JAX}) is a particle-mesh (PM) code written so that its forward model runs on the GPU from end to end and stays differentiable the whole way. The consequence is that automatic differentiation returns the gradient of any output with respect to whatever was fed in: the cosmological parameters, or the initial conditions themselves \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. It also introduces the \textsc{bullfrog} integrator, which reaches a given accuracy in markedly fewer time steps than a standard symplectic scheme \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. Like any $N$-body method it approximates the collisionless dynamics of dark matter, i.e.\ a solution of the cosmological Vlasov--Poisson system for the phase-space distribution $f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p},t)$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{p}}{a^2}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}} f = 0,
  \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^2\phi = \frac{3\,\Omega_m H_0^2}{2a}\,\delta,
  \label{eq:vlasov_poisson}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ are comoving coordinates, $\mathbf{p} = a^2\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ the conjugate momentum, $a(t)$ the scale factor, and $\phi$ the peculiar gravitational potential sourced by the density contrast $\delta$ through the Poisson equation \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.

What sets the PM scheme apart from the tree/FMM solver of Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav} is how the potential is obtained: rather than summing particle-by-particle, the particles are deposited onto a regular mesh to give $\delta$, the Poisson equation is solved in Fourier space with a single FFT (where $\nabla^2 \to -k^2$), and the force $-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi$ is interpolated back to each particle \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. A force evaluation is thus global and cheap, at the price of a spatial resolution limited by the mesh spacing. The origin of the small-scale power deficit that the correction pipelines of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} are built to remove, and, as shown in Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}, also the origin of Disco-DJ's memory footprint on a cluster.

\subsubsection{PkdGrav3}
\label{sec:pkdgrav}

The gravity solver \textsc{PkdGrav3} takes the opposite approach: rather than a fixed mesh, it computes the gravitational force with a binary-tree Fast Multipole Method (FMM). A Barnes--Hut tree expands at one end only: a distant cell is replaced by a multipole approximation, and the field that approximation produces is then evaluated separately at every particle receiving it \cite{barnes_hierarchical_1986}. FMM expands at both ends. It describes how the distant cell's field varies \emph{across} the receiving cell as a whole, so a single cell-to-cell interaction serves every particle inside the receiver instead of one particle at a time. Removing that per-particle bookkeeping is what brings a full force evaluation from \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$} down to \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N)$} \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}.

This accuracy is not free: particles in different regions of a cosmological box span many orders of magnitude in local density, and hence in dynamical time, so a single global time step would be highly wasteful. \textsc{PkdGrav3} instead assigns each particle to one of several time-step ``rungs'', advancing only the particles active on a given rung at each sub-step, and additionally builds a second, smaller tree restricted to the small subset of particles on the shortest (``very active'') rungs so that this frequently-rebuilt tree stays cheap. The combination is summarised by the kick-drift-kick ``umbrella'' diagram over one base time step shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pkd_rungs}; it keeps the tree-construction cost from dominating the runtime even though the shortest rungs may be walked hundreds of times per base step \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}.

It is precisely this combination of a per-particle tree, per-particle rung state, and domain-decomposition buffers that gives \textsc{PkdGrav3} the larger, and rather different, memory footprint discussed quantitatively in Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}.

\textsc{PkdGrav3} is among the best-performing and most scalable \mbox{$N$}-body codes currently available, and the largest runs to date were produced with it. The Euclid Flagship simulation evolves \mbox{$16000^3 \approx 4\times10^{12}$} particles in a \mbox{$3600\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} box, the largest \mbox{$N$}-body simulation performed, on the same CSCS machines used in this thesis \cite{collaboration_euclid_2025}, and both CosmoGridV1 \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023} and the analyses built on it \cite{fluri_full_2022, thomsen_dark_2026} rest on \textsc{PkdGrav3} runs. Its small-scale accuracy is exactly what makes it slower than a particle-mesh code on fixed hardware, which is the trade-off this thesis sets out to exploit.

\subsubsection{CosmoGridV1}
\label{sec:cosmogrid}

CosmoGridV1 is a large, publicly released suite of lightcone $N$-body simulations built specifically for map-level (rather than two-point-function-only) cosmological inference from large-scale-structure probes such as weak lensing and galaxy clustering, targeting Stage-III survey requirements such as those of DES-Y3 and KiDS-1000 \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023, thomsen_dark_2026}. Its coverage of parameter space is a Sobol sequence of 2500 points in six flat-\mbox{$w$}CDM dimensions -- \mbox{$\Omega_m$}, \mbox{$\sigma_8$}, \mbox{$w_0$}, \mbox{$H_0$}, \mbox{$n_s$} and \mbox{$\Omega_b$}, with the total neutrino mass held fixed -- and each of those points carries 7 independent realisations. Two further sets sit beside the grid. One is 200 realisations at a single fiducial cosmology, paired so that parameter derivatives can be taken; the other is a smaller, higher-resolution benchmark set whose function is to confirm that the production runs' box size, particle count and shell thickness were chosen finely enough to be trusted \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. Every simulation is run with \textsc{PkdGrav3} in a periodic box of $900\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ with $832^3$ particles, and stores its lightcone as HEALPix particle-count maps at $N_{\rm side}=2048$ out to $z=3.5$, together with friends-of-friends halo catalogues at each output step \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. These raw particle-count shells are the basis from which observable maps of weak-lensing convergence, galaxy clustering, and intrinsic alignment are subsequently constructed, and the same dataset has previously been used, together with a shell-based baryon correction model, for a deep-learning cosmological parameter analysis of the KiDS-1000 weak-lensing survey \cite{fluri_full_2022}, and for a simulation-based inference analysis of DES-Y3 \cite{thomsen_dark_2026}. It is this suite that the pipeline of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} takes as its accuracy target.

\section{Super-Resolution Methods}
\label{sec:sr-methods}

The resolution deficit between Disco-DJ's particle-mesh force solve and \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s tree-based one is what the correction $f$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} is built to remove. Before turning to that correction directly, this section reviews the machine-learning background it draws on. A central design question for any model acting on the full-sky lightcone maps of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} is how it can process an input of \mbox{$\sim 5\times10^7$} pixels per shell without exceeding the memory of a single GPU, and the choice of architecture and training paradigm shapes how that question is answered.

\subsection{The Memory Problem on the Sphere}
\label{subsec:sr-problem}

Cosmological and astrophysical observables are frequently recorded as spherical sky maps: the Cosmic Microwave Background, neutral-hydrogen intensity maps, galaxy clustering, and gravitational lensing have all been analysed in this form. The conventional route is to compress such a map into a summary statistic -- a power spectrum, a higher-order correlation function \cite{gatti_dark_2024} -- and to analyse that instead of the map. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) offer a different bargain, and it is the one this thesis ultimately depends on: rather than fixing the statistic in advance and hoping it is sufficient, one lets the network discover whatever compression of the map carries the most information about the parameters \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024}, learning it from simulations that already contain the noise it will have to tolerate \cite{fluri_full_2022, perraudin_deepsphere_2019}.

Applying a CNN directly on the sphere, however, is not a straightforward extension of the flat-image case, and raises two distinct challenges. First, a square pixel grid has discrete translation symmetries that make convolution, and the shift of a filter by one pixel, well defined; no grid on the sphere shares a perfectly symmetric structure of this kind, so moving a spherical filter requires some form of interpolation. Second, cost. Equivariance under rotation is the property one would want such a network to have, but the rotation group \mbox{$SO(3)$} is three-dimensional, and a correlation computed over it therefore carries three angular integrals rather than the two of a flat convolution.

Two classical tools mitigate the cost of working at full spherical resolution. The first is a change of basis: expanding a field in spherical harmonics rather than pixels, made fast in practice by the spherical-harmonic transform (SHT) of the HEALPix pixelisation \cite{gorski_healpix_2005}.

The saving is not in the raw parameter count. CosmoGridV1 stores each shell as a HEALPix map at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$}, that is \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{pix}}=$\mbox{$12\cdot N_{\mathrm{side}}^2$}$= 5.0\times10^{7}$} pixels, which is already a heavy compression of the \mbox{$832^3 \approx 5.8\times10^{8}$} particles the simulation evolves \cite{gorski_healpix_2005}.  Expanding one such map in spherical harmonics to the band limit the pixelisation supports, \mbox{$\ell_{\max} = 3N_{\mathrm{side}}-1 = 6143$} , gives \mbox{$(\ell_{\max}+1)^2 = 3.8\times10^{7}$} independent real coefficients -- essentially the same count as the pixels it came from, so changing basis buys nothing on its own \cite{sullivan_methods_2024}. What it buys is the form the covariance takes in the new basis. For a statistically isotropic field, invariance under rotations fixes the second-order statistics to
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle a_{\ell m}\, a^{*}_{\ell' m'} \big\rangle = C_\ell\, \delta_{\ell \ell'}\, \delta_{m m'},
  \label{eq:alm_cov}
\end{equation}
that is, \mbox{$m$} drops out \cite{challinor_lecture_2009}: different modes are uncorrelated, and the variance of each depends only on \mbox{$\ell$}. A multiplicative correction in pixel space carries one free number per pixel, \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{pix}})$}, whereas the transfer function \mbox{$T(\ell)$} of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer} carries one per multipole, \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(\ell_{\max})$}: 6\,144 numbers against 50 million at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$}, a factor of some 8\,000. The band limit \mbox{$\ell \le \ell_{\max}$} is a separate and additional saving, truncating the representation further and making the transform itself cheaper.

Harmonic-space representations also have a history inside deep learning itself. A Fourier neural operator parametrises the kernel of an integral operator directly in Fourier space and keeps only a fixed number of low modes \cite{li_fourier_2021}. Because that budget is fixed in advance, the network's size and cost no longer depend on how finely the input happens to be sampled: one trained at low resolution can be evaluated at high resolution without retraining. The correction pipeline of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer} shares part of that property. Its transfer function is indexed by multipole, which labels an angular scale on the sky rather than a position on a grid, so the same \mbox{$T(\ell)$} applies to a map at any \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}$} -- though only within the band it was fitted on, so extending it to smaller scales means refitting rather than simply evaluating. The two patch-based pipelines have no such freedom at all: their features are learned at a fixed pixel scale, which is why all three must be scored at a common resolution (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}).

The second tool is architectural: rather than making the transform cheaper, avoid ever presenting the whole map to the network at once.
\citeauthor{ronneberger_u-net_2015} face the same wall and step around it by cutting the image into overlapping tiles and segmenting each in turn, which makes the memory budget a constraint on the tile rather than on the image: an arbitrarily large input becomes tractable because the network never sees all of it at once \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}. Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow} and~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} borrow that idea wholesale, tiling gnomonic projections of the sphere instead of a plane. The alternative is to build the sphere into the architecture itself, as \textsc{DeepSphere} does by treating the HEALPix grid as a graph \cite{perraudin_deepsphere_2019, defferrard_deepsphere_2020}; the tiling route was taken here because it lets a standard planar U-Net be reused unchanged, at the cost of having to stitch the sphere back together (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}).

\subsection{Architectures}
\label{subsec:architectures}

\subsubsection{U-Net}
\label{sssec:unet-arch}

The U-Net was designed for segmenting biomedical images, a setting in which labelled examples are scarce enough that the architecture has to be economical with them \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}. Its shape gives it both -- its name and its behaviour (Figure~\ref{fig:unet_generic}). The network first descends a contracting path (encoder), trading spatial precision for an ever wider view of the image, and then climbs back out along a mirrored expanding path (decoder) that restores the resolution surrendered on the way down. The two halves answer different questions -- what is here, and exactly where is it -- and the architecture is built so that neither answer has to be sacrificed for the other.

In the encoder, each pooling step halves the grid and doubles the number of channels, and the effect is the one you get by stepping back from a picture: the deeper a unit sits, the wider the region of the input it draws on, and the better it can say \emph{what} is in that region -- but the coarser the grid it sits on, so the less precisely it can say \emph{where}. Depth buys context at the cost of location. The decoder undoes the resolution loss, doubling the grid at each step, and at every level it is handed the encoder's feature map of matching size to place alongside its own. This is the \emph{skip connection}, and it is what makes the architecture work: the upsampled path knows what is present but has lost track of exactly where, while the encoder map at that same resolution still knows. Combining the two gives the decoder both, and the positional detail never has to pass through the coarse bottleneck at all \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}.

\subsection{Generative Paradigms}
\label{subsec:generative-paradigms}

Beyond deterministic architectures, deep generative models -- algorithms that estimate and sample from an unknown data distribution -- offer a different way to frame the correction problem. The dividing line is not whether a model returns one output or many, but what it is trained to minimise. A pixel-wise squared error against the target map has the conditional mean as its optimum, and that mean averages over every small-scale realisation consistent with the input, smoothing away exactly the variance the correction exists to restore \cite{ledig_photo-realistic_2017}. A map corrected that way would carry the right large-scale structure over a measurably under-dispersed small-scale field.

Both pipelines of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} optimise instead an objective whose solution is the target \emph{distribution}: flow matching by regressing a velocity field that transports one distribution onto the other \cite{lipman_flow_2023, liu_flow_2022}, diffusion by learning to denoise at every noise level \cite{ho_denoising_2020, karras_elucidating_2022}. That the flow achieves this with a fully deterministic sampler -- it starts from the low-fidelity map rather than from noise, and returns one answer per patch -- and still restores the small-scale power is the clearest evidence that it is the objective, not the stochasticity, that does the work.

Determinism does matter later, though, at a point where averaging returns for a quite different reason. Full-sky maps are assembled from overlapping patches, so most sky pixels are predicted several times over and the predictions must be combined. For the flow this is harmless: being deterministic, every tile covering a pixel returns the same answer, and averaging identical predictions changes nothing. For the diffusion model it is destructive, because each tile draws its own independent realisation of the small-scale field, and averaging independent draws suppresses their amplitude \cite{wiegand_kish_1968} -- the same smoothing as before, arriving through the blend rather than the loss. Binding overlapping generations to a shared global solution is a known requirement for diffusion models applied tile-wise \cite{bar-tal_multidiffusion_2023}; Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches} gives the size of the effect here and how it is avoided.

Restoring the small-scale \emph{power}, and not merely its mean, is the entire objective of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}. Two such paradigms are used in this thesis.


\subsubsection{Flow Matching}
\label{sssec:flow-matching}

Flow matching trains a continuous normalising flow (CNF) by plain regression rather than by simulation: the network is fitted to a velocity field that is available in closed form, so at no point during training does an ordinary differential equation (ODE) have to be integrated, which is what earlier CNF training required, and what made it expensive. A second restriction also falls away. The probability path supplying the supervision need not be a diffusion; any path whose velocity can be written down will serve \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. The same idea arrived independently as rectified flow \cite{liu_flow_2022} and as stochastic interpolants \cite{albergo_building_2023}. Let $x_1$ be distributed according to an unknown data distribution $q(x_1)$, of which only samples are available, and let $p_t$ be a probability path with $p_0$ a simple distribution (e.g.\ the standard normal) and $p_1 \approx q$. Given a target path $p_t$ and the vector field $u_t$ that generates it, the FM objective regresses a learned vector field $v_\theta$ onto $u_t$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
      = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_t}\big\lVert v_\theta(x_t, t) - u_t(x_t) \big\rVert^2,
      \qquad t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1],\quad x_t \sim p_t,
    \label{eq:flow_matching}
\end{equation}
so that a model reaching zero loss generates $p_t(x)$ by integrating the ordinary differential equation (ODE) $\dot{x} = v_t(x)$. Used naively this objective is intractable -- no closed form for a suitable $(p_t, u_t)$ pair is generally available -- but \citeauthor{lipman_flow_2023} construct both from probability paths and vector fields defined only \emph{per sample}, whose aggregation provably recovers the marginal $p_t$ and $u_t$ \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. The simplest such construction, used in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}, is the straight-line (linear) interpolant between a start point $x_0 \sim p_0$ and a data point $x_1$,
\begin{equation}
    x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1, \qquad u_t = x_1 - x_0,
    \label{eq:linear_interpolant}
\end{equation}
sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:flow_interp}: the target velocity is constant along each path, and the straighter the true transport between $p_0$ and $p_1$, the fewer ODE integration steps inference needs.

\subsubsection{Diffusion}
\label{sssec:diffusion-theory}

Diffusion models \cite{ho_denoising_2020, song_score-based_2021} approach generation from the opposite end: an image is progressively destroyed by noise, and the network is trained to undo that destruction one step at a time. The construction works as readily with conditioning as without it, and on image quality it has drawn level with generative adversarial networks (GANs) and in places moved past them \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Denoting the data distribution $p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)$ with standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}$, one considers the family of mollified distributions $p(x;\sigma)$ obtained by adding i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ to the data; for $\sigma_{\max}\gg\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}$, $p(x;\sigma_{\max})$ is practically indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise. A diffusion model samples a noise image $x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ and sequentially denoises it through decreasing noise levels $\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_0>\sigma_1>\dots>\sigma_N=0$, so that the endpoint is distributed according to the data \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. The process can equivalently be described by a probability-flow ODE (Ordinary Differential Equation) that continuously raises or lowers the noise level as time runs forward or backward; \cite{karras_elucidating_2022} disentangle the design choices this leaves open -- noise schedule, network preconditioning, loss weighting, and ODE solver -- into the modular ``EDM'' (Elucidated Diffusion Model)formulation whose concrete instantiation in this thesis is given in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Compared to the flow above, which starts from the low-fidelity map rather than from noise and therefore travels a short, nearly straight path, a diffusion sampler always starts from pure noise, so its trajectory is genuinely curved and needs materially more solver steps, the price of a fully generative process.

The architectures and generative paradigms of this section are the building blocks that Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} combines into the three concrete correction pipelines of this thesis.


\chapter{Methods and implementation}
\section{Pipeline Architecture}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The reference suite CosmoGridV1 evolves every realisation with \textsc{PkdGrav3} (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), whose FMM force solver and adaptive time-stepping resolve structure accurately down to small scales. That accuracy is expensive: each lightcone costs many node-hours, so the large number of realisations required for map-level, simulation-based inference dominates the total cost of the analysis \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023, potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. Disco-DJ offers the opposite trade-off: its particle-mesh solver (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}) reproduces the same large-scale clustering in minutes rather than hours, but the force resolution is capped by the PM grid, so power is progressively lost on small scales, exactly the scales that carry much of the non-Gaussian information exploited by map-level analyses \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.

The aim of this work is therefore to keep Disco-DJ's speed while recovering the small-scale content that \textsc{PkdGrav3} would have produced. Formally, we seek a correction $f$ acting on the cheap lightcone maps such that
\begin{equation}
  f\!\left(\mathrm{Map}_{\textsc{disco}}\right) \;\approx\; \mathrm{Map}_{\textsc{CosmoGridV1}},
  \label{eq:correction_goal}
\end{equation}
where both maps are generated from the \emph{same} initial conditions and cosmology, so that $f$ has only to model the resolution-dependent difference between the two solvers, not any change of realisation. This single requirement -- \emph{same initial conditions, same output format} -- dictates the entire architecture of the pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview}), which consists of three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Initial-condition generation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}). CosmoGridV1 publishes its cosmologies and shell tables, but not initial conditions that Disco-DJ could consume directly. For every cosmology we therefore rebuild the exact linear input, the transfer function including massive neutrinos (Sec.~\ref{sec:power_spectrum}), with \textsc{class}, let \textsc{PkdGrav3} generate backscaled initial conditions from it, and hand the identical particle state to both solvers. Any residual difference between the two output maps is then attributable to the force solvers alone, which is precisely the difference $f$ must learn.
  \item \textbf{Shell building} (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}). CosmoGridV1 stores its lightcone as 69 HEALPix particle-count shells out to $z=3.5$ at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$ \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. Disco-DJ, however, ships only a restricted lightcone mode limited to the simulation box volume, far short of the \mbox{$z=3.5$} depth, which requires many periodic box replications (Sec.~\ref{sec:lightcones}). We therefore implemented a dedicated \emph{shell builder} inside Disco-DJ's time integration (\texttt{shell\_builder/}) that reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition and CosmoGridV1's shell binning exactly, so the low- and high-fidelity maps are defined shell-by-shell on identical radial bins.
  \item \textbf{Correction pipelines} (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}). On the resulting paired shells, three independent corrections $f$ are built and compared: a deterministic \emph{transfer-function} rescaling in harmonic space, a \emph{flow-matching U-Net} that transports each map patch from low to high fidelity, and an \emph{EDM diffusion} model that generates the missing small-scale residual from noise. All three are evaluated with the same shared statistics (per-shell and tomographic $C_\ell$, one-point moments, and weak-lensing convergence maps built from the corrected lightcone).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reconstructing the Linear Input and Handing It Over}
\label{subsec:ic_linear}
\label{subsec:ic_why_tk}
\label{subsec:ic_neutrinos}
\label{subsec:ic_handoff}

CosmoGridV1 does not distribute initial conditions, and the way its own runs were
started cannot be reused here. Recovering a starting point that both solvers can share
therefore takes three steps: establishing what has to be rebuilt, rebuilding it, and
checking that the two codes really do start from the same thing.

\paragraph{Why a tabulated transfer function is needed at all.} The published
configuration drives \textsc{PkdGrav3} in its \texttt{bClass=1} mode, in which the
cosmology is not specified through parameters but read wholesale from an HDF5 file
produced by \textsc{concept} \cite{dakin_cosmological_2022}, which calls \textsc{class}
internally and tabulates the full linear perturbation history of every species. In that
mode \textsc{PkdGrav3} does more than draw a particle load: it carries photons, the
massive-neutrino species, the dark-energy fluid and the metric perturbations as
\emph{linear} fields on their own $208^3$ grid
(\texttt{achLinSpecies = "g+ncdm[0]+fld+metric"}, \texttt{nGridLin = 208}) and adds
their gravitational effect to the $832^3$ nonlinear particles at every step. The linear
cosmology is an active ingredient throughout the run, not a boundary condition at the
start.

Disco-DJ has no counterpart to that machinery: it is a pure particle-mesh code
(Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}) and evolves particles under the Poisson equation alone. Were
\textsc{PkdGrav3} run with its linear species active while Disco-DJ was not, the two
lightcones would differ through the treatment of photons, neutrinos and dark energy
\emph{in addition} to the force solver, and the correction of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:correction_goal} would be learning that difference too, defeating the
controlled comparison the whole thesis rests on. Both runs must therefore start from
one plain particle load with no linear species carried afterwards, which forces
\textsc{PkdGrav3} into \texttt{bClass=0} and replaces the HDF5 reference by
\texttt{achTfFile}, a static tabulated transfer function. CosmoGridV1 ships no such
table, so it has to be reconstructed.

\paragraph{Rebuilding it.} Each CosmoGridV1 run ships the linear perturbations its own
initial conditions were built from, as tabulated CDM+baryon density contrasts
$\delta_{cb}(k, a)$. From the $a=1$ slice the dimensional power spectrum is
reconstructed as
\begin{equation}
  P_{cb}(k) \;=\; \frac{2\pi^2}{k^3}\, P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k)\, \delta_{cb}^2(k)\, h^3,
  \qquad P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k) = A_s \left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{pivot}}}\right)^{n_s - 1},
  \label{eq:pk_reconstruct}
\end{equation}
with $k_{\mathrm{pivot}} = 0.05\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ and $(A_s, n_s, h)$ read from the
run's parameter file (\texttt{get\_transfer\_function.py}), and exported in the
\textsc{CAMB} table convention that \textsc{PkdGrav3} reads directly. Two
derived quantities complete the configuration file. The clustering amplitude follows
from the same spectrum through the standard top-hat integral
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{8,cb}^2 \;=\; \int \frac{k^2\,P_{cb}(k)}{2\pi^2}\, W^2(kR)\;\mathrm{d}k,
  \qquad W(x) = \frac{3\,(\sin x - x\cos x)}{x^{3}},\quad R = 8\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h,
  \label{eq:sigma8}
\end{equation}
and the radiation density $\Omega_r$ from a \textsc{class} solve of the same cosmology,
with the neutrino sector declared as \textsc{concept} declares it: one species of three
degenerate states at the grid's fixed mass, at the temperature that reproduces
$N_{\rm eff} = 3.046$, with the cold dark matter density reduced accordingly so that
$\Omega_m$ is preserved. \texttt{IC\_preparation.py} writes these values, together
with $\Omega_0$ and $w_0$, into each run's \texttt{cosmology.par}, idempotently, so a
re-run only changes what is still inconsistent.

\paragraph{Getting the two codes to agree.} None of this was got right first time, and
the route to it is the most useful part of the story. Figure~\ref{fig:ic_error_budget}
is that history: four simulations of the same cosmology, each divided by the
\textsc{PkdGrav3} run started from the same initial conditions. Every curve is a run
that was actually performed, and each stage fixes one ingredient.

Two features of the figure matter before the stages themselves. The offsets are essentially \emph{flat} across the largest scales the box represents: a wrong ingredient displaces the whole ratio bodily rather than bending it, which is the fingerprint of a mis-set growth normalisation rather than of physics at any particular scale, and it is why these were so easily mistaken for a genuine difference between the two force solvers. And the improvement is not monotonic in the obvious way, because several of the errors partly cancelled one another.

Stage~(1) is Disco-DJ with no radiation density in its background at all: early
versions offered no input for it, even though \textsc{PkdGrav3} was being handed its
own. Radiation is negligible today but not at the starting redshift, where it
contributes at the percent level to the expansion rate and hence to the growth factor
that carries the initial conditions forward, and the run sits \mbox{$5.7\%$} high. Supplying
it, stage~(2), removes most of that but leaves $3.1\%$. Stage~(3) is the decisive one:
Disco-DJ is no longer started from \textsc{concept}'s linear input at all, but from the
particle load \textsc{PkdGrav3} itself produces by backscaling. Backscaling carries a
\mbox{$z=0$} spectrum to the starting redshift with one growth factor that is the same at
every scale, and with massive neutrinos the growth of the CDM+baryon field is not
scale-independent. The neutrinos are exactly what the procedure cannot transport, so
handing both codes the same \emph{theory} is not the same as handing them the same
\emph{initial conditions}. That brings the offset to $-0.8\%$. Stage~(4) closes the
last of it: the table, and the $\sigma_8$ it is normalised to, must describe the
CDM+baryon field rather than the total matter field, because in both codes the
particles carry cold dark matter and baryons only and neutrinos never become particles.
A table built from the total matter field imprints the neutrinos' free-streaming
suppression on a field containing no neutrinos, and \textsc{PkdGrav3} normalises the
load to a $\sigma_8$ in the same file that must refer to the same field.
\texttt{get\_transfer\_function.py} writes out both variants, so the pairing is a real
choice; making it consistently leaves $-0.2\%$.

\paragraph{The hand-off.} With the configuration settled, \textsc{PkdGrav3} generates the
backscaled initial conditions: the $a=1$ spectrum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pk_reconstruct} is
scaled back to the starting redshift with the linear growth factor, and particles are
displaced accordingly at CosmoGridV1's resolution of $832^3$ particles in the
$900\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ box. The resulting positions and velocities are exported in the
\texttt{tipsy} format (\texttt{CosmoML.tipsy}) and used \emph{unmodified} as the initial
state of the corresponding Disco-DJ run, which evolves them to $a=1$ and accumulates
the lightcone with the shell builder of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder} over the same
redshift range and at the same HEALPix resolution. Where \textsc{PkdGrav3} is re-run here for validation (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation}) it is handed the same file, so that pair is byte-identical; the production reference remains CosmoGridV1's own lightcone, which shares its phases with this load but was evolved from a different linear input.

This load is deliberately \emph{not} a reproduction of CosmoGridV1's own native initial conditions, which came from \textsc{concept}'s forward evolution rather than from backscaling a static table. The two share phases and are normalised to agree on the large-scale power spectrum, but a single scale-independent growth factor cannot reproduce a scale-dependent forward evolution at every redshift at once: the construction is anchored where the table is taken, at $z=0$, and drifts slowly away from it towards higher redshift. That drift is not a hidden approximation. It is
measurable, and Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation} quantifies it shell by shell as a sub-percent, monotonically redshift-dependent offset in the large-scale amplitude of the resulting lightcone.

first result, separating only at high \mbox{$\ell$}, and only on the nearer shells. Here \mbox{$s$} indexes the radial shell and \mbox{$\ell$} the multipole, so \mbox{$R(\ell, s)$} is one ratio curve per shell. The ratio resolves what that
comparison cannot, and three distinct effects emerge from it.

\paragraph{Lightcone geometry.} The first of the two figures mentioned below validates the shell builder itself. An
error in the geometry -- a mis-solved crossing condition (Eq.~\ref{eq:vx_formula}),
a missing periodic replica (Eq.~\ref{eq:replica_set}), a wrong shell assignment
(Eq.~\ref{eq:shell_filter}), or a HEALPix ordering mismatch -- would misplace or
double-count particles on the sphere and corrupt the spectrum at every
multipole, most conspicuously at the largest scales. No such corruption is
present: for $\ell \lesssim 100$ all four shells scatter about unity with no
systematic offset. The scatter is not uniform across that range, and its structure
is itself the expected one. It grows towards low \mbox{$\ell$}, reaching a few percent
below \mbox{$\ell \approx 20$} on the two most distant shells, where only a handful of
independent modes contribute to each multipole and the ratio of two finite
realisations is correspondingly noisy. The nearer shells, which carry many more
modes per multipole, stay within a few tenths of a percent over the same range.

\paragraph{The backscaling trade-off.} The second figure a small residual in the
large-scale amplitude, and it follows directly from how the initial
conditions are built. The linear spectrum is extracted at $a = 1$ and scaled back
to the starting redshift with the linear growth factor
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:ic_handoff}), which anchors the construction at $z = 0$: there
the reconstructed field matches its target by design. That anchoring holds exactly
at one epoch only. Growth is not strictly scale-independent once massive neutrinos
and radiation contribute to the expansion (Sec.~\ref{sec:power_spectrum}), so a
single backscaling cannot hold at every redshift at once, and the agreement drifts
away from the anchor. The measured large-scale plateau follows exactly that
pattern, shifting monotonically with the depth of the shell:

deficit the corrections must repair, but systematic rather than noise. It is the price of backscaled initial conditions: the closer a shell lies to $z = 0$, the better its overall amplitude is reproduced.

\paragraph{The resolution deficit.} The third effect is the one
Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} exists to remove, and
Figure~\ref{fig:cl_ratio_summary} exposes its defining property: it is a function
of comoving scale, not of multipole. Disco-DJ's force resolution is capped
by its particle-mesh cell, $L_{\mathrm{box}}/N_{\mathrm{pm}} = 900/1664 =
0.54\,\mathrm{cMpc}/h$, a fixed physical length; through the flat-sky correspondence \mbox{$\ell \simeq 2\pi\chi/L$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:limber}, with \mbox{$\chi$} the comoving distance to the shell and \mbox{$L$} the comoving length of the structure in question that one length maps to a different multipole
on every shell, since $\chi$ grows with redshift. The dashed markers show where one fixed comoving scale of \mbox{$17\,\mathrm{cMpc}/h$} -- the measured onset of the deficit, and the scale at which all three corrections are switched on (Eq.~\ref{eq:ell_min}) -- lands on each shell, and they track the onset of the departure of the particle-mesh spectrum from the high-fidelity one shell by shell: the nearest
shell departs from unity around $\ell \sim 70$ and falls to $R \approx 0.24$ near
$\ell \approx 2000$; shell~20 departs at $\ell \sim 300$ and reaches $R \approx
0.56$ at $\ell = 3000$; shell~40 departs at $\ell \sim 600$ and reaches only
$R \approx 0.92$; and shell~60 shows no departure at all within $\ell \le 3000$.
The onset is monotonic in \mbox{$\chi$} and consistent with a single fixed comoving scale across all four shells, and the dashed markers make the agreement quantitative: at \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{cMpc}/h$} they fall at \mbox{$\ell = 79$}, \mbox{$326$} and \mbox{$754$} on shells~5, 20 and~40, against measured onsets of \mbox{$\ell \sim 70$}, \mbox{$300$} and \mbox{$600$}. This is what justifies the per-shell correction onset \mbox{$\ell_{\min}(s)$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min}: no single global \mbox{$\ell_{\min}$} describes these curves, whereas one fixed length in \mbox{$\mathrm{cMpc}/h$} describes them all at once.

Shell~60 is the instructive exception. Its marker sits at \mbox{$\ell = 1395$}, comfortably inside the measured range, yet no departure is visible there at all. The onset scale says where a deficit would begin if there were one; it says nothing about how large it is. By \mbox{$z \approx 2$} the field on these scales is still close to linear, so the particle-mesh solver has little non-linear structure to miss and the deficit never rises above the sample-variance scatter. The two statements are consistent: the correction is switched on for such shells and simply finds almost nothing to do, which is exactly what Sec.~\ref{sec:res-transfer} measures. The most distant redshift bin sits at \mbox{$0.998$}--\mbox{$1.004$} both before and after correction. Finally, one feature should not be mistaken for a recovery. On shells~5 and~60 the ratio turns back upwards at the highest multipoles, on shell~60 even crossing above unity. Both runs contain the same number of particles; once shot noise dominates $C_\ell$ the ratio is driven towards unity regardless of how much clustering power Disco-DJ is missing. The apparent improvement of shell~5 beyond $\ell \approx 2000$ is therefore an artefact of shot noise, not restored structure.


\section{Shell Builder}
\label{sec:shell_builder}

With identical initial conditions in place, the remaining prerequisite for Eq.~\eqref{eq:correction_goal} is that Disco-DJ emit its lightcone in exactly CosmoGridV1's format: particle-count maps on the same 69 radial shells. This section describes the shell builder (\texttt{shell\_builder/Shell\_Builder.py}) that adds this capability to Disco-DJ's time integration.

\subsection{Streaming Shell Accumulation}
\label{sec:shell_builder_core}

A lightcone output requires the particle distribution to be resampled at every integration step, since the observable past-lightcone shell changes continuously as the universe evolves. The most direct implementation, store the full particle snapshot at each of the \mbox{$n_{\mathrm{steps}}$} integration steps and decompose into shells afterwards, is very impractical: retaining \mbox{$n_{\mathrm{steps}}$} full-resolution snapshots simultaneously exceeds the available device memory for the particle counts considered here  (cf.\ Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}).
The shell decomposition is therefore \emph{interleaved} with the time integration, so that at any moment only the particle states immediately before and after the current step, plus the low-dimensional set of pixelised count maps, are held in memory (Figure~\ref{fig:shell_stream}).

\paragraph{Time discretization.} The radial shells are not defined by the integrator but
supplied externally, through a CosmoGridV1-format metainfo table: each shell
$s \in \{1,\dots,S\}$ is a redshift interval $[z_s^{\mathrm{lo}}, z_s^{\mathrm{hi}}]$ with a
corresponding comoving-distance interval $[r_s^{\mathrm{lo}}, r_s^{\mathrm{hi}}]$. Since the
shells are contiguous, their edges form a single ordered sequence of boundaries
$z_0 > z_1 > \dots > z_S$, which we take directly as the integration nodes, so that each shell
coincides with exactly one integration interval:
\begin{equation}
  a_0 < a_1 < \dots < a_S, \qquad a_j = \frac{1}{1+z_j}, \qquad
  \text{shell } s = [\,a_{s-1},\ a_s\,], \quad s = 1,\dots,S.
  \label{eq:step_grid}
\end{equation}
No shell edge is therefore ever crossed within a step. The interval
$[a_{\mathrm{ini}}, a_0]$ preceding the first shell is integrated separately with
$n_{\mathrm{pre}}$ sub-steps and without shell accumulation, to bring the particle state into
registry with the grid of Eq.~\eqref{eq:step_grid}; each of the remaining $S$ intervals
$[a_j, a_{j+1}]$, $j = 0,\dots,S-1$, is then advanced with a single sub-step. The practical consequence is that the time steps are not chosen for numerical convenience but inherited: the integration nodes are CosmoGridV1's shell edges. This is why both the shell binning and the step grid are supplied externally rather than set inside the integrator, and it is what allows the two lightcones to be compared shell by shell on identical radial bins, with no rebinning after the fact.


\paragraph{Accumulation.} At step $j$, the comoving positions of all particles are recorded before and after the sub-step, $\{\mathbf{x}_p^{(j)}\}$ and $\{\mathbf{x}_p^{(j+1)}\}$, and the step's scale-factor edges are converted to comoving lightcone radii through the cosmology-dependent map $\chi(a)$,
\begin{equation}
  R_j = \chi(a_j), \qquad R_{j+1} = \chi(a_{j+1}), \qquad R_j > R_{j+1},
  \label{eq:step_radii}
\end{equation}
so the step sweeps the annulus between the near radius \mbox{$R_{j+1}$} and the far radius \mbox{$R_j$}. Because the integration nodes are the shell edges (Eq.~\eqref{eq:step_grid}), that annulus is a shell: step \mbox{$j$} sweeps shell \mbox{$s = j+1$} and no other, so there is no set of affected shells to determine and no shell is touched by more than one step. The crossing test of Sec.~\ref{sec:crossing} is applied to the pre- and post-step states once for each periodic replica \mbox{$\mathbf{d}$} (Eq. \eqref{eq:replica_set}), and every accepted crossing is pixelised into a HEALPix count map \mbox{$\mathbf{M}_s(\mathbf{d})$}. Accumulation therefore runs over replicas within the step rather than over steps,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{S}_s \;=\; \sum_{\mathbf{d}\,\in\,\mathcal{D}} \mathbf{M}_s(\mathbf{d}), \qquad s = j+1,
  \label{eq:shell_update}
\end{equation}
and the shell is complete once its own step has been processed. The pre-step state is then discarded and the post-step state becomes the next step's pre-step state. Once all steps are processed, the per-shell maps $\{\mathbf{S}_s\}_{s=1}^{S}$ are serialized together with the shell table. At no point are more than two full particle snapshots and \mbox{$S$} integer maps of size \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{pix}}$} resident. This is what removes the memory bottleneck of the naive end-of-run decomposition.

\subsection{Crossing Condition}
\label{sec:crossing}

Which particles contribute to a shell, and where, is decided by the moving-lightcone formulation of \textsc{PkdGrav3} \cite{noauthor_examplepkdgrav3_lightcone_2022}, which the shell builder reproduces exactly (\texttt{Shell\_Builder.py} implements the same crossing equation as \texttt{lightcone.cxx}); Figure~\ref{fig:crossing} illustrates the two ingredients.

\paragraph{Periodic replicas.} 
The observer is placed at the box centre, \mbox{$\mathbf{o} = (L/2, L/2, L/2)$}, with \mbox{$L$} the box side length. These coordinates are dictated by Disco-DJ, which expects particle positions in \mbox{$[0, L)$}: the tipsy load therefore applies the canonical \mbox{$(x + 0.5)\,L$} transform (\texttt{disco/read\_tipsy\_file.py}), and that transform carries \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s origin -- where its own observer sits, in its native \mbox{$[-L/2, L/2)$} convention -- onto \mbox{$(L/2)^3$}. Both lightcones are therefore built about the same physical point, expressed in different conventions. Since the requested lightcone depth $\chi_{\max} = \chi\big(1/(1+z_{\max})\big)$ exceeds $L$, the simulation volume is periodically replicated (Sec.~\ref{sec:lightcones}): the finite replica set
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D} = \Big\{\, \mathbf{d} = (n_1, n_2, n_3)\,L \;:\; \mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^3, \ \; \text{replicated cube intersects the ball of radius } \chi_{\max} \text{ about } \mathbf{o} \,\Big\}
  \label{eq:replica_set}
\end{equation}
is precomputed once from $(\chi_{\max}, L)$. For each $\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}$ the nearest and farthest possible distances of the replicated cube from the observer,
\begin{equation}
  r_{\min}(\mathbf{d}) = \big\lVert \max(0, |\mathbf{d}| - L/2) \big\rVert, \qquad
  r_{\max}(\mathbf{d}) = \big\lVert |\mathbf{d}| + L/2 \big\rVert,
  \label{eq:replica_bounds}
\end{equation}
are also precomputed (componentwise $\max$ and absolute value, then the Euclidean norm), so that a replica whose radial range cannot overlap a step's lightcone annulus is rejected without evaluating a single particle.


\paragraph{Linear crossing formula.} For step $j$ with boundaries $a_j < a_{j+1}$, write $r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} = \chi(a_j)$ and $r_j^{\mathrm{lo}} = \chi(a_{j+1})$. For a particle with pre-/post-step positions $\mathbf{x}_p(a_j), \mathbf{x}_p(a_{j+1})$ relative to the observer and a replica offset $\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}$, define
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{R}_0 = \mathbf{x}_p(a_j) + \mathbf{d}, \qquad \mathbf{R}_1 = \mathbf{x}_p(a_{j+1}) + \mathbf{d}, \qquad
  r_0 = \lVert \mathbf{R}_0 \rVert, \qquad r_1 = \lVert \mathbf{R}_1 \rVert.
  \label{eq:replica_radii}
\end{equation}
Following \textsc{PkdGrav3}, both the particle radius $r_p(v)$ and the inward-moving lightcone front $R(v)$ are interpolated linearly over the step,
\begin{equation}
  r_p(v) = r_0 + v\,(r_1 - r_0), \qquad R(v) = r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - v\,(r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_j^{\mathrm{lo}}), \qquad v\in[0,1],
  \label{eq:scalar_interp}
\end{equation}
and the crossing $r_p(v^\ast) = R(v^\ast)$ is solved in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  v^\ast = \frac{r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_0}{\big(r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_j^{\mathrm{lo}}\big) + (r_1 - r_0)}.
  \label{eq:vx_formula}
\end{equation}
A particle--replica pair is accepted only if $v^\ast \in [0,1)$. Since the half-open acceptance intervals of consecutive steps partition the time axis, every particle is caught in at most one step per replica; evaluating the crossing test against the (generally narrower) metainfo shell bounds instead of the step bounds would break this partition and could double-count particles crossing two adjacent shells within one step.

\paragraph{Shell assignment and pixelization.} The crossing position is obtained by applying $v^\ast$ to the vector positions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}} = (1-v^\ast)\,\mathbf{R}_0 + v^\ast\,\mathbf{R}_1, \qquad r_{\mathrm{cross}} = \lVert \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}} \rVert,
  \label{eq:cross_pos}
\end{equation}
and the pair is kept only if its crossing radius falls inside the shell bounds,
\begin{equation}
  r_s^{\mathrm{lo}} \le r_{\mathrm{cross}} < r_s^{\mathrm{hi}}.
  \label{eq:shell_filter}
\end{equation}
Surviving crossings are pixelised with the equal-area HEALPix \textsc{ring} scheme at resolution $N_{\mathrm{side}}$ from the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{cross}} = \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}}/r_{\mathrm{cross}}$, and the corresponding pixel of $\mathbf{M}_s^{(j)}$ is incremented. In the implementation, the loop over all active replicas runs inside a single JIT-compiled JAX kernel (\texttt{jax.lax.fori\_loop}), so the per-replica work is dispatched to the GPU as one program rather than from Python making it more efficient.

This procedure reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s box-replication lightcone \cite{noauthor_examplepkdgrav3_lightcone_2022}, with one deliberate difference: the replica set \mbox{$\mathcal{D}$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_set} is sized adaptively from \mbox{$(\chi_{\max}, L)$}, with the per-step radial reject of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_bounds}, rather than fixing the number of replicas a priori (\texttt{shell\_builder/utils/compute\_replica\_offsets}). The observer sits at the same physical point in both: \textsc{PkdGrav3} works in the \mbox{$[-L/2, L/2)$} convention with the observer at the origin, while the shell builder reads positions into \mbox{$[0, L)$} and places it at \mbox{$(L/2, L/2, L/2)$} -- the box centre either way.


Together, Secs.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} and \ref{sec:shell_builder} deliver what the learning problem needs: for every cosmology, two lightcones on identical shells that differ only through the force solver. The next section builds the corrections that close that difference.

\section{Correction Pipelines}
\label{sec:correction-pipelines}

Three correction pipelines were implemented, each a self-contained module under \texttt{ml/} (\texttt{transfer/}, \texttt{unet/}, \texttt{diffusion/}) sharing only the neutral analysis library \texttt{ml/analysis/}. They span the two paths identified in Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} -- one deterministic harmonic-space rescaling and two generative, patch-based models -- and are deliberately evaluated through the same shared statistics and plotting code, so their outputs are comparable figure-by-figure. Table~\ref{tab:pipelines} summarises the contrast; Figure~\ref{fig:shared_problem} states the shared problem.

One measured property of the data drives every design choice that follows. The per-multipole cross-correlation \cite{feng_fastpm_2016}
\begin{equation}
  r(\ell, s) \;=\; \frac{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}\times\mathrm{high}}\big\rangle}{\sqrt{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}\big\rangle\,\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}\big\rangle}},
  \label{eq:phase_corr}
\end{equation}
computed across the training runs (\texttt{ml/transfer/transfer\_function.py}), shows that on the dense, high-$z$ shells that carry signal, the low and high maps share phases ($r \approx 0.9$--$1.0$) well into the small scales: Disco-DJ mostly gets the small-scale amplitude wrong, not the positions of structures. This is why a pure amplitude rescaling (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer}) works at all. Only where $r$ decays -- on faint (less-dense), shot-noise-dominated shells and at the highest multipoles (e.g.\ $r=0.56$ at $\ell=1000$ and $0.06$ at $\ell=2500$ on the faintest shells) -- do the maps genuinely decorrelate, and there the correct behaviour is not to amplify Disco-DJ's own noise but to generate statistically fresh small-scale structure, which is precisely the role of the two generative pipelines (Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}, \ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion}).

Two further design decisions are shared by all learned components and are motivated by measured failures of their alternatives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Correct only the small scales; pin the large scales to Disco-DJ.} On large scales Disco-DJ is already correct: the low and high maps agree both in phase ($r\approx 1$) and in amplitude, the transfer function $T\approx 1$ (defined below in Eq. \ref{eq:transfer_def}), so both generative pipelines are formulated as high-pass residual models: the learning target is $\mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{high}-\mathrm{low})$, where $\mathrm{HP}$ is a radial Fourier high-pass whose cutoff is set \emph{per shell} by a fixed comoving scale $L$, exactly as for the harmonic pipeline (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min}): the deficit begins at a fixed physical length, so its onset multipole $2\pi\chi(s)/L$ moves with the shell, and a single angular cutoff could be right for at most one of them.Both generative pipelines act on patches rather than on whole shells: a \mbox{$256\times256$} flat cut-out of the sphere, obtained by gnomonic projection at the map's own pixel scale. How the sphere is tiled into such patches and reassembled afterwards is the subject of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}. The corrected patch is composed as $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{sample})$. The composition re-applies the filter, so the output's large scales equal the low map's exactly, regardless of integration error (\texttt{ml/unet/flow\_model.py}, \texttt{ml/diffusion/model.py}). This formulation is a direct response to two observed failures: a full-field diffusion model drew each tile's large scales from independent noise, so after stitching, the cross-tile phases averaged incoherently and destroyed the low-$\ell$ convergence spectrum; and even the low$\to$high flow drifted the already-correct large scales by a few percent per shell, which compounded coherently across the dozens of shells a convergence map integrates. The harmonic pipeline applies the same principle exactly in $\ell$-space, forcing its correction factors to unity below $\ell_{\min}(s)$.


  \item \textbf{Hold out whole cosmologies, not random patches.} The train/validation split is by cosmology (\texttt{dataset.split\_by\_cosmo}): patches from the same run share large-scale structure, so a random per-patch split would leak the validation set into training. All reported evaluations are on cosmologies the models never saw.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Patch Extraction and Full-Sky Reconstruction}
\label{subsec:pipe-patches}

Training pairs are built by \texttt{make\_patch\_dataset.py}: for each patch a random (cosmology, run) and a random shell are drawn, together with a pointing and an orientation -- a centre pixel drawn uniformly on the sphere, fixing \emph{where} the patch sits, and a rotation angle about that centre, fixing \emph{how} the square is laid over the sky. Both the low and the high shell map are then projected at that same pointing and orientation, with a gnomonic (flat-sky) projection at the map's native pixel resolution: a like-for-like \mbox{$256\times256$} crop with no resampling beyond the projection itself. Each patch stores the metadata needed to recreate it exactly (centre, orientation, shell redshift bounds, comoving distance) together with the run's varied cosmological parameters $(\Omega_m, \Omega_b, n_s, \sigma_8, w_0, h)$ from the CosmoGridV1 metainfo catalogue. From these, the conditioning vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c} = \big(h,\ \Omega_{cdm},\ \Omega_b,\ \Omega_m,\ n_s,\ \sigma_8,\ w_0,\ z_{\mathrm{shell}}\big) \in \mathbb{R}^8
  \label{eq:cosmo_z}
\end{equation}
is assembled in one place (\texttt{dataset.cosmo\_z\_vector}) and consumed identically at train and inference time, so the two can never drift apart.

At inference, a full-sky shell is corrected by the overlap-tile scheme of Figure~\ref{fig:tiling} (\texttt{ml/analysis/patch\_tiling.py}): the sphere is tiled with overlapping gnomonic patches centred on a coarse HEALPix grid (chosen so the patch field of view is at least four times the centre spacing), the model corrects each tile, and the tiles are blended back with a radial cosine taper $w(r) = \tfrac12(1+\cos \pi r)$ that falls to zero at the tile edge, hiding the seams. The tile-to-sphere pixel indices depend only on the geometry, so they are built once and reused for every shell and cosmology, replacing a per-tile re-projection that would otherwise dominate the entire reconstruction cost.

Two subtleties matter only for the stochastic pipeline (diffusion). First, each sky pixel is covered by many overlapping tiles and the blend averages them; if every tile drew independent randomness, the average would suppress the generated small-scale amplitude to $1/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ of its true value, with $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$ the effective number of contributing tiles. \cite{wiegand_kish_1968} The fix is to draw one global noise field on the sphere and hand each tile the crop matching its own pixels, so overlapping tiles agree by construction. Second, the taper is raised to a power \mbox{$p$} before normalisation (\mbox{$p=32$} by default in \texttt{apply\_diffusion.py}): large \mbox{$p$} makes the nearest tile ``own'' each pixel (a soft Voronoi partition), in the spirit of the original overlap-tile strategy \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}, overlap provides context, but each output pixel comes from essentially one prediction. Deterministic per-tile models are unaffected ($p=1$ is kept for the flow), since their overlapping predictions already agree. The same tension, fusing many overlapping diffusion samples into one globally consistent image, is what \cite{bar-tal_multidiffusion_2023} address by binding the per-tile denoising to a shared global solution; the shared-noise construction here plays that role on the sphere.

\subsection{Harmonic-Space Transfer-Function Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipe-transfer}

The first pipeline (\texttt{ml/transfer/}) contains no image-level network at all: it exploits the phase-sharing fact of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr} and corrects only mode amplitudes, in the spherical-harmonic basis anticipated in Sec.~\ref{subsec:sr-problem}. Every shell is transformed to its harmonic coefficients $a_{\ell m}$ (up to $\ell_{\max}=3000$), and each mode is rescaled by a per-shell transfer function \cite{feng_fastpm_2016, garrison_improving_2016}
\begin{equation}
  a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{corr}} = a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{low}} \cdot T(\ell, s),
  \qquad
  T(\ell, s) = \sqrt{\frac{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}(s) \big\rangle}{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}(s) \big\rangle}},
  \label{eq:transfer_def}
\end{equation}
which by construction matches the target power spectrum while preserving the (correct) phases. Both spectra are smoothed in $\log_{10}$-space with a boxcar filter before the ratio is taken: the physical $T(\ell)$ is smooth, whereas a single realisation's $C_\ell$ carries sample-variance and shot-noise scatter that would otherwise be fitted and amplified. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_flow} shows the apply chain.

\paragraph{Two estimators of $T$.} The \texttt{fit} mode averages the smoothed spectra over the training runs and treats $T$ as a property of the solver pair, independent of cosmology, a robust deterministic baseline. The \texttt{emulate} mode instead trains a small MLP emulator of $T$ on features $\big(\log_{10}(\ell{+}1),\ z,\ H_0,\ \Omega_{cdm},\ \Omega_b,\ \Omega_m,\ n_s,\ \sigma_8,\ \log_{10} C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}\big)$, standardised per feature; this interpolates the transfer function to unseen cosmologies of the grid. The emulator is a plain feed-forward network (hidden layers $256$--$256$--$128$, ReLU, linear output) implemented in PyTorch and trained on GPU (Adam, learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch size $4096$, $L_2$ weight decay $\alpha = 10^{-4}$), with a 15\% held-out validation split, early stopping, and best-validation weights restored (\texttt{transfer\_function.py}). The GPU implementation matters for a reason worth recording: the training set is millions of \mbox{$(\ell, s)$} rows with only 9 features each, a workload where a CPU implementation is bound by per-batch overhead rather than arithmetic. A GPU handles exactly this trivial-per-sample, huge-throughput shape. Both estimators also store the phase correlation $R(\ell, s)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr}.

\paragraph{What the emulator learns.} Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function_learned}
shows the two objects the pipeline actually runs on, as predicted for one
held-out cosmology. Both behave exactly as the construction of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_def} and the phase argument of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr}
require, and both do so as a function of shell depth. The gain $T(\ell, s)$ sits
at unity across the large scales on every shell -- the emulator recovers "no
correction needed here" on its own, without being told, since the ramp of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min} is imposed only later at apply time -- and rises only once
the multipole reaches the scale where the particle-mesh solver has begun to lose
power. Where that happens tracks the shell's comoving distance, in the same way
the validation of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation} showed the deficit itself
tracking it: the nearest shell shown ($z \approx 0.09$) needs a gain of $T = 1.12$
at $\ell = 500$, $1.46$ at $\ell = 1000$ and $1.75$ at $\ell = 1500$ -- close to a
factor $3$ in $C_\ell$, since the correction enters as $T^2$ -- the intermediate
shells progressively less and later ($T = 1.15$, $1.05$ and $1.02$ at $\ell = 1500$
for $z \approx 0.26$, $0.49$ and $0.77$), and the most distant shell
($z \approx 2.2$) stays within half a percent of unity across the entire measured
range. The correction the emulator
applies to a shell is thus set almost entirely by how far away that shell is.

The phase correlation $r(\ell, s)$ in the right-hand panel is what licenses this
whole approach, and it also marks its limit. On the shells that carry most of the
signal it stays at unity far into the nonlinear regime, \mbox{$r = 0.996$} at
\mbox{$\ell = 1500$} on the shell at \mbox{$z \approx 0.77$}, and \mbox{$0.999$} at \mbox{$z \approx 2.2$} --
meaning Disco-DJ has placed the
structure correctly and only mis-weighted it, which is precisely the regime in
which rescaling mode amplitudes and keeping their phases is the right operation.
On the nearest, sparsest shells it decays instead ($r = 0.79$ at $\ell = 1000$ and
$0.51$ at $\ell = 1500$ for $z \approx 0.09$), and there
the two maps no longer describe the same structures at all: multiplying by
$T$ still restores the correct power, but the small-scale pattern it
produces is amplified Disco-DJ noise rather than recovered structure. That is the
gap the generative pipelines of Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}
and~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} are built to close.


\paragraph{Where to correct.} The correction must not touch the already-correct large
scales, and ``large'' is a physical statement: the PM resolution deficit sets in at a fixed
comoving scale (of order the PM grid cell), which maps to a different multipole on every
shell. Using the flat-sky (Limber) relation
\begin{equation}
  \ell + \tfrac{1}{2} = k\,\chi(z), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{L},
  \label{eq:limber}
\end{equation}
of \cite{loverde_extended_2008}, a fixed scale $L$ ($17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ in the production
configuration, \texttt{run\_transfer.sh}; see Sec.~\ref{sec:res-transfer} for how it was
chosen) with wavenumber \mbox{$k = 2\pi/L$} is converted per shell to
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\min}(s) = \left\lceil \frac{2\pi\,\chi(z_s)}{L} - \frac{1}{2} \right\rceil
  \;\approx\; \left\lceil \frac{2\pi\,\chi(z_s)}{L} \right\rceil,
  \label{eq:ell_min}
\end{equation}
where the $\tfrac{1}{2}$, retained by \citeauthor{loverde_extended_2008} because dropping it degrades the Limber approximation at $\mathcal{O}(\ell^{-1})$, is negligible for the threshold multipoles $\ell_{\min}\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{2}\text{--}10^{3})$ relevant here, and is dropped in the implementation (\texttt{ell\_min\_from\_mpc\_h}). Below \mbox{$\ell_{\min}$}, \mbox{$T$} is forced to $1$; above it the correction is applied in full, with no transition band. This matches the measured behaviour: nearby shells visibly deviate from CosmoGridV1 from \mbox{$\ell \sim 150$}--\mbox{$300$}, while the most distant shells show no deviation out to
\mbox{$\ell = 3000$}, and correctly receive no correction, because their \mbox{$\ell_{\min}$} exceeds \mbox{$\ell_{\max}$}.


\paragraph{Negative pixels.} The corrected field inevitably contains negative pixels, and it is worth being precise about why. The truth is a non-negative, integer count map, and on the distant, sparse shells the mean count per pixel lies far below unity, so most pixels are exactly zero. The correction, by contrast, is a band-limited real-space fluctuation field: the residual $(T-1)\,a_{\ell m}$ reconstructed by the inverse SHT is a smooth, zero-mean oscillation with both signs. Adding such a field onto a map dominated by empty pixels necessarily drives every pixel where the negative half-wave exceeds the local count below zero, negativity is a structural consequence of representing a boosted continuous overdensity on top of a nearly empty discrete map, not a numerical defect of the method. Every cheap pixel-wise repair was measured and rejected: flooring at zero deletes only the negative excursions, which raises the mean and injects spurious power exactly on the small scales the correction just restored, and re-balancing the mean afterwards or correcting the logarithm of the density instead merely redistributes the same bias. The corrected field is therefore deliberately not post-processed: it is treated as a continuous overdensity field (\texttt{--no-clip}) and passed unchanged into every downstream statistic -- angular power spectra, moments, and the weak-lensing convergence maps of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} -- none of which requires per-pixel count validity.

\subsection{U-Net Flow-Matching Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}

The second pipeline (\texttt{ml/unet/}) is the first of the two generative ones: a conditional flow-matching model (Sec.~\ref{sssec:flow-matching}) acting on the gnomonic patches of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}. Its defining choice is the start point of the flow: instead of Gaussian noise, the ODE starts at the low-fidelity patch itself,
\begin{equation}
  x_0 = \delta_{\mathrm{low}}, \qquad
  x_1 = x_0 + \mathrm{HP}\!\left(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}}\right), \qquad
  x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1,
  \label{eq:flow_bridge}
\end{equation}
so the model learns a bridge from low to high rather than a full generative map from noise (\texttt{flow\_model.py}). Because \mbox{$x_0$} already carries the correct large-scale structure, the target velocity \mbox{$u = x_1 - x_0 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}})$} is a pure high-pass field, integrating it can only ever add small-scale content, and the trajectory is nearly straight, so 8 Euler steps suffice at inference (Table~\ref{tab:pipelines}). The training loss is the flow-matching regression of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_matching} on this interpolant,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\;
  \frac{\big\lVert v_\theta(x_t, t, \mathbf{c}) - (x_1 - x_0) \big\rVert^2}{\max\!\big(\mathrm{Var}(x_1{-}x_0)\,/\,\overline{\mathrm{Var}},\ 0.1\big)},
  \label{eq:flow_loss}
\end{equation}
where the denominator is a bounded, batch-relative per-patch variance normalisation: a dense, well-resolved shell's true residual is naturally tiny, so plain MSE gives it far weaker gradients than a sparse shell's large residual, and the network never learns to calibrate the ``correct answer is nearly zero here'' case, visible as a persistent few-percent bias on the densest shells. Normalising by each patch's own residual variance, relative to the batch mean and clamped to at most a $10\times$ upweighting, removes this imbalance without the gradient blow-ups an unbounded reweighting was observed to cause (\texttt{train\_flow.py}).

\paragraph{Architecture.} The velocity network \texttt{FlowUNet} instantiates the generic U-Net of Figure~\ref{fig:unet_generic} with four levels and base width 32: channels $1\to32\to64\to128\to256\to512$ at spatial resolutions $256^2 \to 16^2$, mirrored by the decoder with skip concatenations, and a $1\times1$ output head (Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}). Two conditioning paths are threaded through it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flow time $t$, everywhere.} Time is embedded sinusoidally (dimension 128, widened to 512 by a two-layer MLP) and then allowed to reach into every convolution block through FiLM \cite{perez_film_2017}. A block is two \mbox{$3\times3$} convolutions with GroupNorm(8) and SiLU; between them, a linear layer belonging to that block alone turns the shared embedding into one scale and one shift per channel, applied as \mbox{$h \mapsto h\,(1+\mathrm{scale}) + \mathrm{shift}$}. Conditioning thus enters as a per-channel affine transform rather than as an extra input, which is what lets a single set of weights represent the whole time-dependent velocity field. One network thus represents the whole time-dependent velocity field $v_\theta(x, t)$.
  \item \textbf{Cosmology and redshift, once.} The 8-vector $\mathbf{c}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cosmo_z} passes through a two-layer MLP and is added, broadcast over space, into the $512$-channel bottleneck latent.
\end{itemize}
The output head is likewise zero-initialised, so the untrained model predicts $v = 0$ (``stay at $x_0$''), the correct baseline from which to learn a correction. Training uses Adam (learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$, scaled linearly with the number of data-parallel ranks), cosine-annealed over the epochs, gradient clipping at norm 1, and PyTorch DDP (Distributed Data Parallel) across the four GPUs of a node (\texttt{train\_flow.py}). At inference, \texttt{apply\_flow.py} integrates $\dot x = v_\theta(x, t, \mathbf{c})$ with Euler steps, composes $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(x(1) - x_0)$ through the same filter code used to build the training target, and reconstructs full skies via Figure~\ref{fig:tiling}.

\subsection{Diffusion Pipeline (EDM)}
\label{subsec:pipe-diffusion}

The third pipeline (\texttt{ml/diffusion/}) replaces the low$\to$high bridge with a genuinely generative process in the EDM formulation of \citeauthor{karras_elucidating_2022} (Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}): every sample starts from pure Gaussian noise, and the low map enters only as conditioning. What is diffused is again not the full field but the high-pass residual \mbox{$x_1 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}})$}, for the reasons of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}; the conditioning, by contrast, is the \emph{full} low patch at all scales, channel-concatenated to the noisy input. The denoiser needs the large-scale context to know which small-scale structure to synthesise, even though its output is band-limited.

\paragraph{Preconditioned denoiser.} The network is the two-channel variant of Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}, wrapped in the EDM preconditioning (\texttt{model.EDMPrecond}). Here $x$ (analogous to $x_t$ in flow matching) is the noisy high-pass residual whose clean target is $x_1 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{high}-\delta_{low})$, and $\delta_{low}$ is the low-resolution conditioning map (used at all scales) that steers the denoiser.
\begin{equation}
  D_\theta(x, \sigma, \mathrm{cond}) = c_{\mathrm{skip}}(\sigma)\,x
    + c_{\mathrm{out}}(\sigma)\, F_\theta\!\big(\,[\,c_{\mathrm{in}}(\sigma)\,x \,\oplus\, \delta_\mathrm{low}\,],\ c_{\mathrm{noise}}(\sigma),\ \mathbf{c}\big),
  \label{eq:edm_precond}
\end{equation}
with the standard coefficients
\begin{equation}
  c_{\mathrm{skip}} = \frac{\sigma_d^2}{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{\sigma\,\sigma_d}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{noise}} = \tfrac14 \ln \sigma,
  \label{eq:edm_coeffs}
\end{equation}
\cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Each coefficient has a distinct job, and together they let \emph{one} network operate across five orders of magnitude in noise level (Figure~\ref{fig:edm_sampling}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \mbox{$c_{\mathrm{in}}$} normalises the input: \mbox{$x = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$} has variance \mbox{$\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2$}, so \mbox{$c_{\mathrm{in}}\,x$} has unit variance at \emph{every} noise level. The raw network \mbox{$F_\theta$} never has to adapt its operating range to \mbox{$\sigma$}.
  \item $c_{\mathrm{skip}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}}$ blend an identity path with the network output. At small $\sigma$, $c_{\mathrm{skip}} \to 1$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}} \to 0$: the noisy input is already almost the answer, and the network only supplies a small correction. At large $\sigma$, $c_{\mathrm{skip}} \to 0$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}} \to \sigma_d$: the input is essentially pure noise, and the network must synthesise the answer outright. The denoiser thus interpolates continuously between ``pass through'' and ``generate'', instead of one set of weights having to learn both regimes unaided.
  \item $c_{\mathrm{noise}}$ log-compresses $\sigma \in [0.002, 80]$ into an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ scalar, which is what the sinusoidal embedding of Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}(a) receives, the diffusion counterpart of the flow time $t$.
\end{itemize}
The conditioning channel ($\delta_{low}$) is neither noised nor rescaled by $c_{\mathrm{in}}$. It is not part of the diffusion process, it only steers it. One EDM default is deliberately not adopted: the data scale $\sigma_d$, tuned to $0.5$ for natural images in \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}, is instead measured as the standard deviation of the actual training target -- the high-pass residual, whose variance differs materially from a natural image's -- over a few real batches at start-up (\texttt{estimate\_sigma\_data}); using the wrong $\sigma_d$ would mis-scale all four coefficients of Eq.~\eqref{eq:edm_coeffs} at once.

\paragraph{Training.} Per example, a noise level is drawn as $\ln\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(-1.2, 1.2^2)$, the target is noised to $x_t = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$, and the denoising error is weighted per noise level and normalised per patch:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, \lambda(\sigma)\,
      \frac{\big\lVert D_\theta(x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon,\ \sigma,\ \delta_{\mathrm{low}}) - x_1 \big\rVert^2}
           {\max\!\big(\mathrm{Var}(x_1)/\overline{\mathrm{Var}},\ 0.1\big)} \right],
  \qquad
  \lambda(\sigma) = \frac{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}{(\sigma\,\sigma_d)^2},
  \label{eq:edm_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda(\sigma)$ is EDM's weighting that equalises gradient magnitude across noise levels \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}, and the bounded per-patch normalisation is the same device, and the same measured motivation, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_loss}. The bound is essential here: an unbounded $1/\mathrm{Var}$ divisor compounds multiplicatively with $\lambda(\sigma)$'s own dynamic range, and the combination was observed to produce loss spikes that prevented convergence entirely. The optimiser setup mirrors the flow pipeline's.

\paragraph{Sampling.} Inference follows EDM's deterministic Algorithm 1 (\texttt{model.sample\_heun}): the Karras noise schedule
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_i = \Big(\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho} + \tfrac{i}{N-1}\big(\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho} - \sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho}\big)\Big)^{\rho},
  \qquad \rho = 7,\ \ \sigma \in [0.002,\ 80],
  \label{eq:karras_schedule}
\end{equation}
integrated with Heun's second-order method over $N = 32$ steps from $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:edm_sampling}). The step count is not a tuning failure but the point of this pipeline: a trajectory from pure noise is genuinely curved (Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}), and with up to two denoiser calls per Heun step, this is the most expensive of the three corrections per shell. The corrected patch is composed exactly as in the flow pipeline, \mbox{$\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{sample})$}, and full skies are reconstructed with the shared global noise field and \mbox{$p=32$} blending of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}. The sampler is deterministic given (noise, conditioning), which is precisely what makes overlapping tiles consistent.

In summary, the three pipelines answer the same question at three levels of ambition: transfer restores the power spectrum by construction but cannot restore structure; unet-flow transports each patch along a short, nearly straight bridge and restores stochastic small-scale power at moderate cost; diffusion generates the missing band from noise through a full multi-step sampler, at the highest cost. All three inherit the same guarantees from the shared formulation, large scales pinned to Disco-DJ, corrections confined to the scales where the deficit lives, and evaluation on held-out cosmologies. Running them at production scale is the subject of the next section.

\subsection{Local Development}
\label{sec:local_dev}

Both codes were first set up on a local machine to establish a minimal working configuration with an interactive debugging loop: a bug in the shell-builder logic of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder} or the IC hand-off of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} is caught in seconds, rather than after a queued cluster job has run to completion. Tests at $16^3 = 4096$ particles complete in under a minute on a single core; such a run is not physically meaningful, it exercises the full pipeline -- initial conditions, integration, lightcone accumulation -- end to end before committing to a production configuration.

The binding constraint at production size is memory. Storing only the raw phase-space state -- six single-precision components per particle -- for a CosmoGridV1-sized run gives
\begin{equation}
    N_p = 832^3 \approx 5.8\times10^{8}, \qquad
    N_p \times 24\ \text{bytes} \approx 14\ \text{GB},
    \label{eq:phasespace_mem}
\end{equation}
already beyond a typical workstation's RAM once any working headroom is included -- and this floor is exceeded differently by the two codes. \textsc{PkdGrav3} carries, on top of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phasespace_mem}, its per-particle tree, per-particle rung state, and domain-decomposition buffers (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), so its footprint grows with tree depth and rung occupancy. Disco-DJ is instead bounded by its force mesh: the Poisson solve is one global FFT (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), so the whole PM grid -- density, its Fourier transform, and force components -- must be resident simultaneously, and the demand scales with the chosen mesh resolution rather than the particle count. Production runs of either code therefore require a cluster.

\subsection{Euler (ETH Zurich)}

On Euler, each code gets its own virtual environment: Disco-DJ is built on JAX and needs a matched GPU software stack, while \textsc{PkdGrav3} is a compiled tree code with its own toolchain. A per-code split that avoids the version conflicts a shared environment invites. Euler provides the memory and GPU partitions for the full $832^3$ resolution; the GPU acceleration specifically benefits Disco-DJ, whose entire force solve is GPU-native, whereas the \textsc{PkdGrav3} build used here is CPU-bound. Long-running jobs are distributed across multiple nodes where the code supports it.

\subsection{CSCS (Alps)}

The largest runs use the CSCS Alps system, whose NVIDIA GH200 nodes provide 120~GB of on-package memory each. This directly addresses Disco-DJ's binding constraint: since its memory demand tracks the PM mesh (Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}), a larger memory pool translates into a finer force mesh, and hence better small-scale force resolution, per node. Production runs use \texttt{RES\_PM} $= 1664 = 2\times832$ mesh cells per dimension at the CosmoGridV1 box and particle count (Appendix~\ref{app:slurm}). The same nodes also host the correction-pipeline training of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}, where the memory allows large per-GPU batches of $256^2$ patches, and DDP training spans the four GPUs of a node.

\section{Software Engineering}
\subsection{Code and Data Layout}

Executables and virtual environments live in the cluster's backed-up but quota-limited \texttt{HOME}; simulation snapshots, shell maps, per-job logs, and diagnostic plots -- bulk, regenerable output -- go to \texttt{SCRATCH}, which trades persistence for effectively unbounded (periodically purged) capacity. Keeping every submission's full log proved valuable for debugging and cross-run comparison alike: a run's log is often the only remaining record of exactly which configuration produced a given output. The CosmoGridV1 reference dataset (\mbox{$\sim110$}~TB) was transferred to CSCS with a managed Globus transfer job, which runs unattended and recovers from dropped connections. An interactive single-stream copy at this scale would be both too slow and too fragile.

\subsection{Job Scheduling and Submission}

Every Python driver is paired with a Slurm batch script: the driver runs unchanged from the local tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev} up to a production allocation, while only the surrounding batch script changes. Jobs are launched interactively (\texttt{bash}) for fast-turnaround debugging or queued (\texttt{sbatch}) for long unattended runs. Each batch script follows the same two-part layout (Appendix~\ref{app:slurm}): a header of \texttt{\#SBATCH} directives declaring \emph{resources} (nodes, GPUs, task layout, exclusivity, wall-clock limit), \emph{accounting} (account, partition), and \emph{bookkeeping} (job name, working directory, \texttt{\%j}-tagged log paths so concurrent runs never overwrite each other), followed by a short execution body that activates the code-specific environment, fixes input paths (e.g.\ the \texttt{CosmoML.tipsy} IC of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and sets the physical parameters as shell variables (\texttt{RES=832}, \texttt{RES\_PM=1664}, \texttt{BOXSIZE=900.0}).

The resource header also fixes the job's cost against the allocation,
\begin{equation}
    C = n_{\mathrm{nodes}} \times t \quad [\text{node-hours}],
    \label{eq:node_hours}
\end{equation}
billed regardless of how much of \mbox{$t$} is spent computing, which is exactly why short single-node interactive sessions are used for debugging and exclusive multi-node allocations are reserved for production. One detail keeps the scripts scalable: the header reserves one task per node, and the execution body computes the real degree of parallelism as one process per GPU, \texttt{NTASKS=\$((SLURM\_NNODES * 4))}, passed explicitly to \texttt{srun}. Scaling a job up therefore means editing a single \texttt{--nodes} line; the process-per-GPU launch follows automatically.

\subsection{Version Control}

The whole project, the analysis and ML pipelines together with the modified Disco-DJ containing the shell builder, is kept in a dedicated Git repository. Beyond protection against machine failure, this gives a reliable way back to a known-good state when an experimental change breaks the pipeline, and keeps the many configuration variants organised.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:res-protocol}

\paragraph{Held-out cosmologies.} All pipelines share one split convention (\texttt{transfer\_function.split\_val\_cosmos}, \texttt{dataset.split\_by\_cosmo}): of the 198 grid cosmologies with both a Disco-DJ and a CosmoGridV1 lightcone available, whole cosmologies are shuffled with a fixed seed and 30 ($15\%$) are withheld from every fit and training run. Figure~\ref{fig:heldout_params} shows where they sit in parameter space; Table~\ref{tab:heldout} lists their parameters. All 30 are used for every diagnostic, including the compute-heavy full-sky and weak-lensing ones, and the same 30 for every pipeline, so all figures of this chapter are directly comparable.

\paragraph{Statistics and resolutions.} Every pipeline is scored with the same shared diagnostics (\texttt{ml/analysis/}): per-shell full-sky angular power spectra pooled into three redshift bins, example patches with per-patch 2D power ratios, one-point moments (variance, skewness, excess kurtosis) as a function of shell depth, and weak-lensing convergence maps. The $\kappa$ maps are built with the \textsc{UFalcon} lightcone integration \cite{sgier_fast_2018} (\texttt{ml/analysis/weak\_lensing.py}): all corrected shells within a source bin's redshift range are combined into one convergence map per cosmology at the same $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, $\ell \le 1500$ as the shells they integrate -- building $\kappa$ at a finer resolution than its input shells would only upsample, producing spectra above the shells' own band limit that are pixel-window artefacts rather than signal -- using the DES-Y3 \textsc{metacal} source redshift distributions of tomographic bins 1 ($z \lesssim 1.05$) and 4 ($z \lesssim 1.85$) -- the lowest- and highest-redshift bins, which weight the nearby (hardest) and distant (easiest) shells respectively. 

\paragraph{A common evaluation footing.} All three pipelines are scored on the same maps. The flow and diffusion models work on flat \mbox{$256\times256$} patches cut from shells at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$}; the transfer function works on those same shells in harmonic space, transformed to \mbox{$\ell_{\max}=1500$}. That is simply the band such a map contains -- a HEALPix map at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$} carries no information above \mbox{$\ell \approx 1535$} -- so the two representations cover the same scales. All three corrections then switch on at the same place, the fixed comoving scale \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min}, and so act on the same range of every shell, differing only in what they do there.

\paragraph{Percentile bands.} The shaded bands in the $C_\ell$-ratio figures are \emph{percentile bands across the held-out evaluation set}, not error bars on a mean. For the shell-level grids (e.g.\ Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_cl}), five shells are sampled from each redshift bin for each of the 30 cosmologies, and the ratio \mbox{$C_\ell/C_\ell^{\mathrm{true}}$} is computed per (cosmology, shell) pair, 150 samples per bin. The solid line is their median; the band spans the 16th to 84th percentile, i.e.\ the central 68\% of the actual cosmology-to-cosmology and shell-to-shell scatter. A narrow band therefore means the correction is \emph{uniformly} good across cosmologies and depths, not merely good on average; for the $\kappa$ figures the band is taken across the 30 cosmologies at fixed $\ell$.

\section{Transfer-Function Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-transfer}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The emulator was trained on the 168 training cosmologies with $\ell_{\max}=1500$, the band limit of the common $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$ footing (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}), spectra smoothed with a 21-point boxcar (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer}), and the per-shell correction onset fixed at $17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ with a raised-cosine transition of width $0.10\,\ell_{\max}$; the MLP ($256$--$256$--$128$, Adam, learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch $4096$, $L_2$ decay $10^{-4}$) is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:training}. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_loss} shows its loss: training and validation track each other throughout, separated by a constant \mbox{$\approx 9\%$} that never widens, with only nine features and millions of rows there is far too little capacity per sample to memorise, and they converge within the first \mbox{$\sim 10^4$} gradient steps to a best validation loss of \mbox{$\tfrac12\mathrm{MSE} = 1.26\times10^{-4}$}, corresponding to an RMS error on the predicted transfer function of \mbox{$\sqrt{2 \times 1.26\times10^{-4}} \approx 1.6\%$} per \mbox{$(\ell, s)$} sample.

\paragraph{Full-sky \mbox{$C_\ell$}.} With that onset, Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_cl} shows the correction working across the lightcone rather than in one bin. In the nearest bin it lifts the ratio from \mbox{$0.953$} to \mbox{$1.003$} at \mbox{$\ell \in [400,900]$}, from \mbox{$0.854$} to \mbox{$1.001$} at \mbox{$[900,1300]$}, and from \mbox{$0.768$} to \mbox{$1.003$} at \mbox{$[1300,1500]$} -- a repair worth up to \mbox{$23$} percentage points, landing within \mbox{$0.3\%$} of the truth. The middle bin, whose uncorrected deficit reaches \mbox{$2.7\%$} at the band edge, is likewise brought to \mbox{$1.003$}. The most distant bin is all but untouched: the corrected and uncorrected curves are indistinguishable below \mbox{$\ell \approx 800$} and drift apart by only \mbox{$0.1\%$} at \mbox{$\ell = 1000$}.

\paragraph{Patches.} The example patches of Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_patches} make the mechanism visible: because the correction rescales amplitudes but keeps Disco-DJ's phases, the corrected patch shows the same structures as the input, with the missing small-scale graininess restored on top. The per-patch power-ratio panels come with a caveat: they measure a spherical-harmonic-calibrated correction through flat-sky 2D FFTs of individual gnomonic patches, and on several shells this diagnostic shows band-limited overshoots that the full-sky $C_\ell$ of Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_cl} -- the statistic the method is calibrated on, and the one used downstream -- does not exhibit. The discrepancy is a property of the patch diagnostic (projection and pixelisation of a full-sky correction), and its residual cause has not been fully isolated.


\paragraph{Moments.} Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_moments} exposes the pipeline's designed-in limitation. The full-sky variance of the corrected shells lands nearly on the truth, variance is an integral of \mbox{$C_\ell$}, which the method matches by construction. Skewness and excess kurtosis, however, remain at the level of the uncorrected maps, far below the reference on the sparse shells: a deterministic, phase-preserving amplitude rescale cannot manufacture the one-point non-Gaussianity that the missing small-scale structure carries. This is precisely the gap the generative pipelines exist to close.

\section{U-Net Flow-Matching Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-flow}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The flow was trained for 200 epochs on the $10^5$-patch dataset (Table~\ref{tab:training}), distributed over 16 DDP ranks (4 Alps nodes $\times$ 4 GH200), with the base learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$ scaled linearly to $4.8\times10^{-4}$ and cosine-annealed; the high-pass residual used the same per-shell onset \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} as the harmonic pipeline, so the model is asked to correct exactly the band that is actually deficient on each shell. Figure~\ref{fig:flow_loss} shows the variance-normalised flow-matching loss of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_loss}: validation improves until epoch \mbox{$\approx 109$} (best \mbox{$0.145$} at epoch 109 -- the checkpoint used for everything below) and then drifts slowly upward while the training loss continues to fall, i.e.\ mild but genuine overfitting past that point. The residual plateau is expected rather than alarming: at intermediate $t$ the target velocity is not uniquely determined by the network's input, so the regression loss is bounded below by this irreducible conditional variance.

\section{Diffusion Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-diffusion}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The diffusion model shares the flow's data, architecture width, schedule length, and high-pass formulation (Table~\ref{tab:training}); it differs in the objective the unscaled learning rate of \mbox{$3\times10^{-5}$}, and the 32-step Heun sampler with shared sphere noise and \mbox{$p=32$} blending at inference. Its loss curve (Figure~\ref{fig:diff_loss}) looks qualitatively different from the flow's: the EDM-weighted denoising loss collapses from $\approx 5.9$ to $\approx 0.6$ within the first $\sim 20$ epochs and is then almost flat, with training and validation coinciding to the end (best validation \mbox{$0.512$} at epoch 165) -- no overfitting is observed. This is characteristic of the objective: every example is presented at a freshly drawn noise level and noise realisation, an implicit augmentation that makes memorisation far harder than for the flow's fixed low$\to$high pairs.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{plots/results/diffusion/loss_curve.png}
    \caption{Training and validation loss of the EDM diffusion model (Eq.~\ref{eq:edm_loss}; 9-epoch rolling mean). The loss plateaus early and train/validation coincide throughout. The per-example noise draws act as strong implicit augmentation.}
    \label{fig:diff_loss}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Full-sky $C_\ell$.} The reconstructed shell spectra (Figure~\ref{fig:diff_cl}) match the flow's picture closely over most of the corrected range. In the nearest bin the two agree exactly where the correction opens -- both reach \mbox{$1.007$} over \mbox{$\ell \in [400,900]$}, against an uncorrected \mbox{$0.953$} -- and separate progressively thereafter, the diffusion falling to \mbox{$0.987$} over \mbox{$[900,1300]$} and \mbox{$0.948$} over \mbox{$[1300,1500]$} where the flow holds \mbox{$1.000$} and \mbox{$0.970$}. In the middle bin the two are indistinguishable, both landing within three parts in a thousand of unity across the whole band. The summary is that the two generative pipelines reproduce the shell spectra equally well wherever the deficit is moderate, and the flow is the better of the two on the nearest shells, where the correction has the most work to do. That a 32-step stochastic sampler, stitched from thousands of tiles sharing one global noise field, reproduces full-sky spectra at this level is the central validation of the tiling scheme of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:res-synthesis}

Figure~\ref{fig:method_comparison} reduces all three pipelines to a single view: for each held-out cosmology the ratio of a $\kappa$-map moment to its reference value, averaged over the 30 cosmologies, with the cosmology-to-cosmology scatter as an errorbar. Because every method is scored at the same $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, $\ell_{\max}=1500$ footing, the vertical ordering is a statement about the corrections themselves rather than about the resolutions they were given.

    \centering

The uncorrected Disco-DJ lightcone is shown on the same axis. The distance from it to unity is the gap each method is trying to close. All three are evaluated at the common $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, $\ell_{\max}=1500$ footing, which handicaps the transfer function relative to its native-resolution capability (see the common-footing discussion in Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}).}

Three consistent messages emerge. First, every pipeline repairs the two-point statistics on the scales it acts on, and at a small fraction of the \mbox{$N$}-body cost: the uncorrected bin~1 variance ratio of \mbox{$0.803 \pm 0.018$} is lifted to \mbox{$0.973 \pm 0.008$} by the flow, \mbox{$0.967 \pm 0.006$} by the transfer function and \mbox{$0.948 \pm 0.010$} by the diffusion model. The three are within three percentage points of one another, which is the substantive point: on a two-point statistic the cheapest correction is competitive with the most expensive, and the transfer function achieves its result at essentially zero inference cost and with the tightest scatter.

Second, the higher moments separate the deterministic from the generative route cleanly, and the separation is structural rather than a matter of tuning. A pure amplitude rescale preserves phases, so it cannot manufacture the phase correlations that skew a distribution; the transfer function accordingly moves skewness only from \mbox{$0.733 \pm 0.020$} to \mbox{$0.793 \pm 0.027$} and excess kurtosis from \mbox{$0.537 \pm 0.042$} to \mbox{$0.638 \pm 0.058$}. The generative models go far beyond it: the flow reaches \mbox{$0.989 \pm 0.011$} in skewness and \mbox{$0.979 \pm 0.037$} in excess kurtosis, the diffusion \mbox{$0.896 \pm 0.013$} and \mbox{$0.772 \pm 0.036$}.

Third, the flow is the strongest correction overall. It leads on every one of the three moments in both source bins, and it does so with the whole non-Gaussian signal essentially recovered rather than merely improved -- an excess kurtosis within \mbox{$2\%$} of the reference where the uncorrected lightcone retains barely half of it. The residual across all methods remains one-sided -- every correction undershoots, none overshoots -- so what is left is a small systematic deficit rather than added noise.

The same picture holds in bin~4 with uniformly smaller gaps (flow \mbox{$0.992/0.992/0.983$}, diffusion \mbox{$0.986/0.963/0.906$}, transfer \mbox{$0.979/0.892/0.784$}), as expected: the distant shells are the ones Disco-DJ already gets nearly right, so less has to be repaired. Taken together the results suggest a division of labour rather than an outright winner. Where an analysis consumes two-point statistics, the transfer function delivers them to within a few percent for free, and is the pragmatic choice. Where it consumes the field -- which is the entire premise of map-level inference -- only a generative correction supplies what is missing, and on this evidence the flow-matching model supplies almost all of it.


\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis set out to answer a practical question at the heart of map-level, simulation-based cosmological inference: can the small-scale accuracy of an expensive reference $N$-body lightcone be restored onto a cheap particle-mesh lightcone, well enough for the statistics such analyses actually consume? The answer, on held-out cosmologies spanning the full CosmoGridV1 $w$CDM grid, is yes, with the qualification that which correction suffices depends on which statistic must be right.

The foundation was infrastructural. By reconstructing each CosmoGridV1 cosmology's exact linear input -- transfer function, $\sigma_8$, and radiation density, massive neutrinos included -- and letting \textsc{PkdGrav3} generate one backscaled initial condition consumed byte-identically by both solvers (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and by embedding a shell builder into Disco-DJ's time integration that reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition on CosmoGridV1's radial shell grid within constant memory (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}), the learning problem was reduced to its irreducible core: paired full-sky lightcones differing only through the force solver. Every result in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} rests on this controlled setup.

On these pairs, three corrections were built (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}), sharing one measured insight -- Disco-DJ's small-scale deficit is primarily one of \emph{amplitude}, its phases remaining correlated with the truth deep into the nonlinear regime -- and one shared guarantee, the high-pass residual formulation that pins the already-correct large scales to the fast simulation exactly. The deterministic transfer-function pipeline holds shell and weak-lensing convergence spectra to within half a percent of the reference across the band it acts on, at essentially zero inference cost and with the tightest cosmology-to-cosmology scatter of the three; where an analysis consumes two-point statistics it remains the pragmatic choice, being competitive on convergence variance (\mbox{$0.967$} against the flow's \mbox{$0.973$}) at a small fraction of the cost. What it cannot do -- by construction, not by failure -- is generate the one-point non-Gaussianity of the missing structure. The two generative pipelines close exactly that gap: both the flow-matching U-Net and the EDM diffusion model restore skewness and excess kurtosis at shell and convergence level on cosmologies they never saw, the flow most completely -- recovering the convergence excess kurtosis to \mbox{$0.979 \pm 0.037$} of the reference, against \mbox{$0.537$} uncorrected -- and with the tightest cosmology-to-cosmology scatter, the diffusion consistently further short on every moment. Their convergence spectra differ over most of the band, the flow leading by three to five percentage points through the intermediate multipoles, the two converging near \mbox{$0.90$} only at the \mbox{$\ell = 1500$} band limit where both are resolution-bound rather than correction-bound. That a 32-step stochastic sampler, evaluated on thousands of overlapping tiles sharing a single global noise field, reassembles into consistent full-sky maps also validates the overlap-tile machinery itself as a general route for applying patch-based generative models to the sphere.

Several limitations bound these conclusions and set the agenda.
All three pipelines were evaluated at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, with the band capped at $\ell_{\max}=1500$ -- a common footing chosen so that the comparison measures the corrections themselves rather than the resolutions they were given (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}). Retraining the patch models on $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$ tiles, which the patch machinery already supports, is the most direct extension, and is what would turn the present ordering into a statement about the methods at full resolution. The corrected fields are continuous overdensities, not integer count maps, which downstream weak-lensing statistics tolerate but a consumer requiring per-pixel count validity would not. The corrections were validated on matter shells and derived convergence maps at fixed total neutrino mass, without baryonic feedback, which operates on precisely the scales corrected here and would need its own treatment \cite{schneider_matter_2015, fluri_full_2022}. And each cosmology entered with a single realisation, so realisation-to-realisation generalisation at fixed cosmology remains to be quantified.

The direction, however, is clear. A Disco-DJ lightcone costs minutes where the reference costs node-hours, and the corrections add almost nothing to that, so corrected fast lightcones can populate the simulation demand of deep-learning analyses of current surveys \cite{thomsen_dark_2026, fluri_full_2022} at a small fraction of the reference suite's cost, reserving full \mbox{$N$}-body runs for validation rather than production. The longer-term prospect is tighter still: Disco-DJ is differentiable end-to-end \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}, and a differentiable corrected forward model, from cosmological parameters through the corrected lightcone to the map-level statistic, would put gradient-based inference within reach of the same pipeline.
