\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Map-level, simulation-based inference (SBI) is becoming the standard way to extract cosmological information from large-scale-structure surveys. Simulating the cosmological field directly during analyses, rather than emulating specific compressed summary statistics of interest, provides three main advantages. It retains the non-Gaussian information that gravitational collapse imprints on small scales. It allows systematic effects to be modelled in the forward direction, where they physically act and allows the different probes to be described self-consistently within a single simulated universe \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024, gatti_dark_2024, lemos_simbig_2023}. The cost of such analysis is borne almost entirely by simulation. It typically requires a suite of forward-modelled mocks spanning a large parameter space. Gravity-only lightcone suites built for exactly this purpose exist, \textsc{CosmoGridV1} \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}, \textsc{SimBIG} \cite{lemos_simbig_2023} or \textsc{GowerStreet} \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024}  among them. However, these provide simulations on a \emph{fixed} grid of cosmologies, each realisation being an \mbox{$N$}-body run costing significant computational resources. A recent DES-Y3 analysis built on \textsc{CosmoGridV1} suite required of order one million map-level mocks \cite{thomsen_dark_2026}; reproducing such coverage at the volume Stage-IV surveys demand is prohibitive.

Fast approximate solvers are the natural alternative. Particle-mesh codes such as \textsc{Disco-DJ} ({Differentiable Simulations for Cosmology -- Done with JAX}) \cite{list_disco-dj_2026} or \textsc{FastPM} \cite{feng_fastpm_2016} evolve the same initial conditions in minutes rather than hours. Their force resolution is capped by the mesh, however, so they progressively lose small-scale power. That is precisely the regime that motivated working at the map level in the first place. This thesis asks whether the deficit can be repaired.

Answering it required first constructing a controlled comparison. \textsc{CosmoGridV1} does not distribute initial conditions, so for every cosmology the linear input is therefore reconstructed from the published perturbations and handed to \textsc{PkdGrav3}, which generates a backscaled particle load. The \textsc{Disco-DJ} run is started from that load, while the reference remains \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s own lightcone. A shell builder embedded in \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s time integration reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition on \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s exact radial shell grid. The two lightcones are therefore defined shell by shell on identical radial bins, and their difference is dominated by the force solver; the residual from the reconstructed initial conditions stays sub-percent on large scales. On these pairs three correction schemes are trained and compared on held-out cosmologies: a deterministic per-multipole transfer-function rescaling in harmonic space, a conditional flow-matching U-Net that transports each map patch from low \textsc{Disco-DJ} to high \textsc{CosmoGridV1} fidelity, and a conditional diffusion model in the Elucidated Diffusion Model (EDM) formulation, which generates the missing small-scale residual from noise.

Evaluated on a common footing, all three restore the angular power spectrum to within a few percent on the scales up to \mbox{$\ell_{\max} = 1500$}. The two latter models go even further. They recover the non-Gaussian one-point statistics, specifically third and fourth moments of the weak-lensing convergence signal that a per-multipole rescaling by construction cannot: for example, the flow matching scheme lifts the convergence maps excess kurtosis from \mbox{$0.54$} to \mbox{$0.98$} of its reference value, where the transfer function reaches only \mbox{$0.64$}. Corrected fast lightcones thus approach the reference simulations at a small fraction of the cost, at least at the two-point level and, depending on the correction scheme, beyond it. Full \mbox{$N$}-body runs can therefore be reserved for validation rather than production, and coupling the fast simulators with the AI-based corrections developed in this thesis opens the door to SBI.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Modern cosmology rests on a remarkably economical model. Six parameters suffice for \mbox{$\Lambda$}CDM: the densities of cold dark matter and baryons, the Hubble constant, the amplitude and tilt of the primordial fluctuation spectrum, and the optical depth to reionisation. With these it accounts for observations spanning the age of the Universe, from the temperature and polarisation anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background and the abundance of the light elements to the clustering of galaxies, the growth of structure, and the accelerating expansion inferred by distant supernovae \cite{dodelson_modern_2020}. Two of its ingredients have no explanation in the Standard Model of particle physics: the cold dark matter that dominates the matter budget, and the cosmological constant that drives the acceleration. The sharpest tests of both come from how structure grows \cite{huterer_growth_2023}.

The large-scale structure (LSS) of the Universe is the cosmic web of halos, filaments, sheets and voids traced by galaxies and dark matter. It has been mapped observationally since the first redshift slices revealed that the galaxy distribution is not random \cite{de_lapparent_slice_1986}, and is understood theoretically as the anisotropic collapse of Gaussian initial fluctuations \cite{bond_how_1996, libeskind_tracing_2018}. It is also one of the richest probes of cosmology available, because its growth over cosmic time is sensitive to the matter density \mbox{$\Omega_m$}, the clustering amplitude \mbox{$\sigma_8$} and the dark-energy equation of state \mbox{$w_0$} (Sec.~\ref{sec:cosmic_web}) \cite{dodelson_modern_2020, huterer_growth_2023}. Current and upcoming cosmological surveys -- the Dark Energy Survey \cite{gatti_dark_2024}, DESI \cite{collaboration_desi_2016}, Euclid \cite{collaboration_euclid_2025} and the Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time \cite{ivezic_lsst_2019} -- now measure this structure over large fractions of the sky, and the most powerful analyses no longer compress the data to two-point functions, or to other summary statistics such as weak-lensing peaks and the baryon-acoustic-oscillation scale \cite{eisenstein_baryonic_1998}; they work at the \emph{map level}, extracting non-Gaussian information with deep networks trained on simulations \cite{fluri_full_2022, jeffrey_dark_2024, gatti_dark_2024, thomsen_dark_2026}.

Three recent analyses show what this buys and what it costs. \citeauthor{jeffrey_dark_2024} compress DES-Y3 weak-lensing mass maps with a neural network trained on simulations and infer \mbox{$w$}CDM parameters without ever writing down a likelihood, the compression itself being learned rather than chosen \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024}. \citeauthor{gatti_dark_2024} instead compare families of hand-built non-Gaussian statistics on the same maps -- wavelet harmonics, scattering transforms and map moments -- and find that combining them tightens \mbox{$S_8$} well beyond what the Gaussian two-point information supports \cite{gatti_dark_2024}. \textsc{SimBIG} carries the same idea to galaxy clustering, performing inference directly at the field level from a forward model built on \mbox{$N$}-body simulations with a halo-occupation prescription \cite{lemos_simbig_2023}. What unites them is the cost structure: the statistic is cheap, and the simulation suite that calibrates it is not. The DES-Y3 analysis closest to this thesis makes the scale explicit -- it builds its forward model on \textsc{CosmoGridV1} and requires of order one million map-level mock realisations \cite{thomsen_dark_2026}.

The term \emph{simulation-based} covers two distinct things, and it is worth separating them. The first is the absence of a prediction from first principles. For the linear power spectrum one exists: an Einstein--Boltzmann solver integrates the perturbation equations directly and returns \mbox{$P(k)$} without simulating anything \cite{seljak_line_1996}. At the map level there is no such shortcut. The statistics of an evolved, non-linear density field on the sky can only be obtained by evolving that field end to end. That means a many-body system under gravity, which is what \textsc{CosmoGridV1} does \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023, potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. Where baryonic physics must be modelled explicitly rather than absorbed into a correction, it means full hydrodynamics. The hydrodynamical suites that reach the volume and resolution a cosmological analysis needs currently number of order ten realisations, far too few to serve as a theory prediction across a parameter space.

The second is how the likelihood is then treated, which is a separate choice. One can write down an explicit form for it and evaluate it at the field level or even decline to write it at all. Alternatively, a neural network could learn the likelihood or the posterior from simulated pairs -- neural likelihood and neural posterior estimation, the route \citeauthor{jeffrey_dark_2024} take for DES-Y3 \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024}. Both consume the same resource: entire suites of forward-simulated lightcones spanning the parameter space, each realisation an expensive \textsc{PkdGrav3} run (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}) \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. It is this demand, not the inference machinery, that sets the cost of a map-level analysis.

Fast approximate solvers are a promising alternative. Two established examples show the range of approach. \textsc{COLA} hands the large-scale motion to Lagrangian perturbation theory, which knows it analytically, and asks the integrator only for what perturbation theory cannot supply; a handful of time steps then suffices \cite{tassev_solving_2013}. \textsc{FastPM} instead constrains a particle-mesh solver so that the linear growth comes out right however few steps it is given \cite{feng_fastpm_2016}. \textsc{Disco-DJ} belongs to the same family but is the most modern of them, written for GPUs and differentiable end to end \cite{hahn_disco-dj_2024, list_disco-dj_2026}. That is why it is the solver used here. Such particle-mesh (PM) codes evolve the same initial conditions in minutes on a single GPU (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}). Their force resolution is capped by the mesh, however, so small-scale power is progressively suppressed \cite{schneider_matter_2015} -- and with it exactly the non-Gaussian content that motivates map-level inference in the first place. The question this thesis addresses is therefore: can the small-scale content of an expensive reference simulation be restored onto a cheap particle-mesh lightcone, accurately enough for map-level statistics, at negligible additional cost?

Answering it requires two ingredients that are as much infrastructure as machine learning. The first is a controlled learning problem: the low- and high-fidelity maps must differ through the force solver and as little else as possible. That demands two things. Initial conditions matched as closely as the published reference allows (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and lightcones defined on identical radial shells. The latter is provided by a shell builder implemented inside \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s time integration (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}), since the code's native lightcone support does not reach \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s depth.

The second ingredient is the corrections themselves (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}). Three are built and compared here, and they were not chosen arbitrarily: they span a deliberate range, from an emulation technique the community already trusts to two learned, patch-based approaches that, to our knowledge, have not previously been applied in this setting.

The first is the conservative baseline. Rescaling mode amplitudes so that a cheap simulation reproduces the power spectrum of an expensive one is standard practice: it is how fast solvers are calibrated against full \mbox{$N$}-body runs \cite{feng_fastpm_2016}, how initial-condition transients are removed \cite{garrison_improving_2016}, and how a single simulation is rescaled to other cosmologies \cite{angulo_one_2009}. Its appeal here is directness. The quantity being learned is the transfer function between the high- and low-fidelity runs, one correction factor per multipole and shell. It is fitted against the angular power spectrum itself, which is the statistic the corrected lightcones are ultimately scored on. The method therefore optimises exactly what is measured, with no proxy objective in between. A small network predicts the transfer function from the cosmological parameters, which is what allows it to be applied to cosmologies never run at high fidelity.

Its limitation is equally clear, and is the reason for the other two. A per-mode correction factor multiplies amplitudes and leaves phases untouched. It can therefore restore how much power sits at each scale, but not the spatial arrangement that carries it. That is exactly right as long as the fast solver has placed the structure correctly, and Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} shows that it usually has. Where the two simulations have genuinely decorrelated, rescaling amplifies the fast solver's own noise instead of recovering the missing structure. No amount of tuning changes that: the higher-order moments a map-level analysis consumes are precisely the ones a phase-preserving operation cannot manufacture.

A learned map between the two fidelities is the natural answer, but only if it is trained on the right objective. A regression trained on paired maps with a per-pixel squared error converges to the conditional mean \cite{hastie_elements_2009}. That mean is smooth by construction, and it under-disperses exactly the small-scale variance the correction exists to restore \cite{ledig_photo-realistic_2017}. What is needed instead is a model trained to reproduce the \emph{distribution} of the high-fidelity field, so that its small-scale structure has the right statistics rather than the right average. That is what a \emph{distribution-matching} objective delivers, and both learned corrections built in this thesis rest on one. Such objectives come from generative modelling, which already has a short but encouraging history in cosmology. Deep networks have been trained to super-resolve cosmological fields \cite{ramanah_super-resolution_2020, li_ai-assisted_2020, ni_ai-assisted_2021} and to emulate \mbox{$N$}-body displacements directly \cite{he_learning_2018, jamieson_field_2022}; diffusion models have more recently joined them \cite{mudur_can_2022, rouhiainen_super-resolution_2023}, and have been extended to galaxy surveys at the field level \cite{cuesta-lazaro_point_2024}.

Two models are used here rather than one, and the difference between them is easily mistaken, so it is worth stating at the outset: only one of the two is generative. Conditional flow matching \cite{lipman_flow_2023, liu_flow_2022} learns a velocity field that transports one distribution into another. It can therefore be started from the low-fidelity map itself, so the model has only to travel the short distance between the two fidelities. Started that way it is \emph{deterministic}. The trajectory begins at a fixed map rather than at a random draw, and integrating the ODE returns one answer per patch. It is a transport map, not a sampler. A diffusion model in the Elucidated Diffusion Model (EDM) formulation \cite{karras_elucidating_2022} starts instead from pure noise and generates the missing small-scale component outright, conditioned on the low-fidelity map. It is generative, since a fresh noise draw gives a different, equally valid completion. The first is cheaper and inherits the fast solver's structure by construction. The second is more expensive but can supply genuinely new structure. Both are trained on distribution-matching objectives rather than on a per-pixel error, which is what separates them from the conditional-mean regression above. Which of the two serves this problem better is one of the questions Chapter~\ref{ch:results} answers.

The remainder of this chapter provides the background these methods rest on: the cosmological framework and the simulation codes (Sec.~\ref{sec:theoretical-background}), followed by the machine-learning concepts -- U-Nets, flow matching, and diffusion -- that the correction pipelines instantiate (Sec.~\ref{sec:sr-methods}).


\section{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:theoretical-background}
\subsection{Cosmological Framework}
\subsubsection{The Cosmic Web}
\label{sec:cosmic_web}

On scales of a few to a few hundred megaparsecs, matter in the Universe is not smoothly distributed but arranged into an interconnected network of dense compact knots, elongated filaments, two-dimensional sheets, and large under-dense voids, collectively known as the \emph{cosmic web} \cite{bond_how_1996, libeskind_tracing_2018}. The name is due to \citeauthor{bond_how_1996}, and their argument was more than descriptive. The filament-dominated pattern can already be read out of the tidal field of the initial, still-linear perturbations, which means gravitational evolution does not invent the web at all. It sharpens an outline that was present from the beginning \cite{bond_how_1996}. The same gravitational instability that seeds this pattern is responsible for its growth: initially small density fluctuations are amplified by their own self-gravity, so that overdense regions contract while voids expand and come to dominate the volume of the Universe \cite{bond_how_1996}. The pattern was first seen directly in the CfA redshift survey \cite{de_lapparent_slice_1986}, and large galaxy surveys -- 2dFGRS \cite{colless_2df_2001}, SDSS \cite{york_sloan_2000}, and the 2MASS \cite{huchra_2mass_2012} redshift survey among them -- as well as cosmological $N$-body simulations both recover this same web-like arrangement of galaxies and dark matter \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}.

Gravitational instability drives the emergence of anisotropic structure: because collapse along the direction of a region's strongest tidal compression proceeds fastest, an initially only mildly non-spherical overdensity first flattens into a sheet, then contracts further into a filament, and only finally collapses fully into a compact halo. The sequence of anisotropic collapse was first described by Zel'dovich \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. This picture can be made quantitative through the local tidal (or velocity-shear) field: following the tidal-tensor classification reviewed by \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}, one computes the Hessian of the (rescaled) gravitational potential $\phi$,
\begin{equation}
  T_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x_\alpha \partial x_\beta}, \qquad \nabla^2 \phi = \delta,
  \label{eq:tidal_tensor}
\end{equation}
where $\delta = (\rho - \bar\rho)/\bar\rho$ is the dimensionless matter overdensity and $\phi$ has been normalised by $4\pi G \bar\rho$ so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:tidal_tensor} takes the form of the Poisson equation \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. Diagonalising the (real, symmetric) tidal tensor at each point gives three eigenvalues $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \lambda_3$; counting how many of them exceed a chosen threshold $\lambda_{\rm th}$ classifies that point as part of a void (zero eigenvalues above threshold), a sheet (one), a filament (two), or a knot (three) \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. Voids, sheets, filaments and knots are thus not four independent morphologies but a nested hierarchy, each embedded within the next.

\subsubsection{Matter Power Spectrum and Transfer Function}
\label{sec:power_spectrum}

While the tidal-tensor classification above describes the cosmic web \emph{qualitatively}, its statistical properties are better quantified through the matter power spectrum \mbox{$P(k)$}. For a Gaussian field \mbox{$P(k)$} is a complete description. The late-time field is not Gaussian on small scales, however, so \mbox{$P(k)$} does not fully characterise it, and the higher-order statistics of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are precisely what the patch-based corrections are built to restore. Writing the density contrast in Fourier space at a fixed time as $\delta(\mathbf{k})$
and assuming statistical homogeneity and isotropy, the power spectrum at that time is defined through
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle \delta(\mathbf{k})\, \delta^*(\mathbf{k}')\big\rangle
  = (2\pi)^3\, \delta_D(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}')\, P(k),
  \label{eq:power_spectrum_def}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_D$ is the Dirac delta and the angle brackets denote an ensemble average over realisations of the initial conditions \cite{dodelson_modern_2020, huterer_growth_2023}. In a simplified form it can be written as
\begin{equation}
    P(k) \propto k^{n_s}\,T^2(k),
\end{equation}
with $n_s$ the scalar spectral index \cite{eisenstein_baryonic_1998, huterer_growth_2023}.
Expanding the fitting formula of \cite{bardeen_statistics_1986} on large scales,
$k \ll k_{\mathrm{eq}}$, gives
\begin{equation}
    T(k) = 1 - \alpha_1 \left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)
             + \alpha_2 \left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)^{2}
             + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{eq}}}\right),
    \qquad
    k_{\mathrm{eq}} = H_0\,\sqrt{\frac{2\Omega_m}{\Omega_r}},
    \label{eq:transfer_expansion}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$ are fixed numerical coefficients set by the shape of the fitting formula, and $k_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is the wavenumber of the mode that enters the horizon at matter--radiation equality.

The transfer function is therefore shaped by the energy content of the Universe at each epoch: the scale $k_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is set by matter--radiation equality -- the crossing of the radiation and matter curves in Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function} -- and massive neutrinos modify this budget in a scale-dependent way. The red curves of Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function} show each mass state tracking the photons while it is still relativistic and turning over to a matter-like dilution once it is not. What matters for \mbox{$T(k)$} happens in between: a neutrino that is fast but no longer relativistic streams freely out of any perturbation smaller than the distance it can cross, so it never falls into small-scale potential wells and contributes nothing to their growth. Below that free-streaming scale the transfer function is suppressed accordingly \cite{lesgourgues_neutrino_2012}.


The suppression propagates from $P(k)$ into every projected statistic, in particular the angular power spectrum $C_\ell$, which is (schematically) a line-of-sight projection of the three-dimensional power,
\begin{equation}
    C_\ell \approx \int k^2\, P(k)\, \mathrm{d}k,
    \label{eq:cl}
\end{equation}
\cite{seljak_line_1996}, and $C_\ell$ is the statistic on which the lightcone maps of this thesis are ultimately scored (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}).

Figure~\ref{fig:neutrinos_effect} shows the net effect on the linear power spectrum, and separates the two mechanisms just described. Three cosmologies are compared that share \mbox{$\omega_b$}, \mbox{$\omega_m$} and the primordial spectrum, so any difference between them comes from the neutrino sector alone. Two features stand out. The turnover, which marks \mbox{$k_{\mathrm{eq}}$}, shifts between the curves: adding a relativistic species changes the radiation density and therefore moves the epoch of equality, which is the change of energy budget visible in Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function}. On scales smaller than the free-streaming length the massive case falls below the others. The massive states do not cluster there, so they contribute to the background expansion that slows growth without contributing to the perturbations that drive it. Large scales, which the neutrinos never free-stream out of, are left unchanged.

Both features matter for this thesis for a practical reason. \textsc{CosmoGridV1} was run with massive neutrinos and \textsc{Disco-DJ} cannot follow them, so \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s own initial conditions cannot simply be reused here, and the offsets of Figure~\ref{fig:neutrinos_effect} are exactly what the shared initial conditions of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} have to be built to absorb before the two simulations can be compared at all.

\subsection{Cosmological Simulations}

Cosmological $N$-body simulations have historically tracked, and in some respects driven, the development of high-performance computing: the number of particles that can be evolved in a fixed wall-clock time has grown by many orders of magnitude, both through raw hardware improvements and through continual gains in the efficiency of the gravity solvers themselves \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. The scientific payoff lies in modelling the LSS of the Universe to the non-linear precision that the cosmological surveys require. While LSS observables are primarily sensitive to the present-day matter density $\Omega_m$ and the clustering amplitude $\sigma_8$, slicing a lightcone into tomographic redshift bins additionally tracks the growth of structure over cosmic time -- making such simulations sensitive to the dark-energy equation-of-state parameter $w_0$, and hence to the physical origin of cosmic acceleration \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}.

The method itself is simple to state. The continuous matter distribution is discretised into a finite set of massive \emph{superparticles}, each standing for a large parcel of the underlying phase-space distribution, and those particles are advanced under their mutual gravity \cite{dehnen_n-body_2011}. Richer physics can be layered on -- gas dynamics, radiative cooling, star formation, feedback -- and the hydrodynamical suites that result are what galaxy-formation studies rest on \cite{vogelsberger_cosmological_2019}, at rapidly increasing cost. For the weak-lensing and clustering statistics considered here, a gravity-only treatment is an adequate approximation, and gravity-only (\mbox{$N$}-body) simulation is the central tool of this work.

Two choices then fix the run: the initial conditions, drawn from a linear Einstein--Boltzmann solution (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and the algorithm that evaluates the gravitational force at each step. The force solver is where the codes differ from one another, and the established approaches trade resolution against cost in different ways \cite{dehnen_n-body_2011}. A particle mesh solves the Poisson equation on a grid with a single fast Fourier transform (FFT): fast, but with the force resolution capped at the cell size; P$^3$M (particle--particle/particle-mesh) schemes add a direct short-range summation on top to recover part of it, refining the mesh where clustering is strongest \cite{couchman_mesh-refined_1991}. The particle mesh is the scheme behind the fast solvers named at the start of this chapter -- COLA \cite{tassev_solving_2013}, FastPM \cite{feng_fastpm_2016} and \textsc{Disco-DJ} \cite{list_disco-dj_2026} -- which is both what makes them fast and what caps their resolution. Hierarchical trees and fast multipole expansions instead group distant particles rather than gridding them, at \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$} and \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N)$} cost respectively \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}: \textsc{Gadget} pairs a tree with a particle mesh \cite{springel_simulating_2021}, and \textsc{PkdGrav3}, which produced the reference suite used here, uses a fast multipole method (FMM) (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}) \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. Adaptive-mesh refinement takes a third route, refining the grid where the density is high, as in \textsc{Ramses} \cite{teyssier_cosmological_2002}.

Species other than cold dark matter and baryons complicate the picture: photons, massive neutrinos and dark energy contribute to the expansion history and, on large scales, to the gravitational potential, but they are not represented by the particle load. Gravity-only codes therefore either fold them into the background expansion alone, or carry them as separate \emph{linear} fields evolved alongside the particles \cite{dakin_cosmological_2022}. Which of the two routes a code takes turns out to matter a great deal for this thesis, and is taken up in Sec.~\ref{subsec:ic_linear}.

This thesis works between the two ends of that design space, and both solvers are described in turn below: the particle-mesh \textsc{Disco-DJ} (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), fast enough to generate the large volume of training data the approach requires, and the tree-based \textsc{PkdGrav3} (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), which anchors the accurate \textsc{CosmoGridV1} reference simulations. 


\subsubsection{\textsc{Disco-DJ}}
\label{sec:discodj}

\textsc{Disco-DJ} is a particle-mesh (PM) code written so that its forward model runs on the GPU from end to end and stays differentiable the whole way. The consequence is that automatic differentiation returns the gradient of any output with respect to whatever was fed in: the cosmological parameters, or the initial conditions themselves \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. Its default integrator is \textsc{bullfrog} \cite{rampf_bullfrog_2025}, which reaches a given accuracy in markedly fewer time steps than a standard symplectic scheme: six steps already give per-cent-level accuracy in the present-day power spectrum at \mbox{$k \approx 0.2\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$} \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. Like any $N$-body method it approximates the collisionless dynamics of dark matter, i.e.\ a solution of the cosmological Vlasov--Poisson system for the phase-space distribution $f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p},t)$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{p}}{a^2}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}} f = 0,
  \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^2\phi = \frac{3\,\Omega_m H_0^2}{2a}\,\delta,
  \label{eq:vlasov_poisson}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ are comoving coordinates, $\mathbf{p} = a^2\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ the conjugate momentum, $a(t)$ the scale factor, and $\phi$ the peculiar gravitational potential sourced by the density contrast $\delta$ through the Poisson equation \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.

What sets the PM scheme apart from the tree/FMM solver of Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav} is how the potential is obtained: rather than summing particle-by-particle, the particles are deposited onto a regular mesh to give $\delta$, the Poisson equation is solved in Fourier space with a single FFT (where $\nabla^2 \to -k^2$), and the force $-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi$ is interpolated back to each particle \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. A force evaluation is thus global and cheap, at the price of a spatial resolution limited by the mesh spacing. The origin of the small-scale power deficit that the correction pipelines of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} are built to remove, and, as shown in Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}, also the origin of \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s memory footprint on a cluster.

\subsubsection{PkdGrav3}
\label{sec:pkdgrav}

The gravity solver \textsc{PkdGrav3} takes the opposite approach: rather than a fixed mesh, it computes the gravitational force with a binary-tree FMM. A Barnes--Hut tree expands at one end only: a distant cell is replaced by a multipole approximation, and the field that approximation produces is then evaluated separately at every particle receiving it \cite{barnes_hierarchical_1986}. FMM expands at both ends. It describes how the distant cell's field varies \emph{across} the receiving cell as a whole, so a single cell-to-cell interaction serves every particle inside the receiver instead of one particle at a time. A simplified comparison is given in Fig. \ref{fig:bh-vs-fmm}. Removing that per-particle bookkeeping is what brings a full force evaluation from \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$} down to \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N)$} \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}.

\textsc{PkdGrav3} is among the best-performing and most scalable \mbox{$N$}-body codes currently available, and the largest runs to date were produced with it. The Euclid Flagship simulation evolves \mbox{$16000^3 \approx 4\times10^{12}$} particles in a \mbox{$3600\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} box, the largest \mbox{$N$}-body simulation performed to date, and it was run with \textsc{PkdGrav3} at the same computing centre (CSCS) that hosts the runs of this thesis \cite{collaboration_euclid_2025}, and both \textsc{CosmoGridV1} \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023} and the analyses built on it \cite{fluri_full_2022, thomsen_dark_2026} rest on \textsc{PkdGrav3} runs. Its small-scale accuracy is exactly what makes it slower than a particle-mesh code on fixed hardware, which is the trade-off this thesis sets out to exploit.

\subsubsection{\textsc{CosmoGridV1}}
\label{sec:cosmogrid}

\textsc{CosmoGridV1} is a large, publicly released suite of lightcone $N$-body simulations built specifically for map-level (rather than two-point-function-only) cosmological inference from large-scale-structure probes such as weak lensing and galaxy clustering, targeting Stage-III survey requirements such as those of DES-Y3 and KiDS-1000 \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023, thomsen_dark_2026}. Its coverage of parameter space is a Sobol sequence of 2500 points in six flat-\mbox{$w$}CDM dimensions -- \mbox{$\Omega_m$}, \mbox{$\sigma_8$}, \mbox{$w_0$}, \mbox{$H_0$}, \mbox{$n_s$} and \mbox{$\Omega_b$}, with the total neutrino mass held fixed -- and each of those points carries 7 independent realisations. Two further sets sit beside the grid. One is 200 realisations at a single fiducial cosmology, paired so that parameter derivatives can be taken; the other is a smaller, higher-resolution benchmark set whose function is to confirm that the production runs' box size, particle count and shell thickness were chosen finely enough to be trusted \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. Every simulation is run with \textsc{PkdGrav3} in a periodic box of $900\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ with $832^3$ particles, and stores its lightcone as HEALPix particle-count maps at $N_{\rm side}=2048$ out to $z=3.5$, together with friends-of-friends halo catalogues at each output step \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. These raw particle-count shells are the basis from which observable maps of weak-lensing convergence, galaxy clustering, and intrinsic alignment are subsequently constructed, and the same dataset has previously been used, together with a shell-based baryon correction model, for a deep-learning cosmological parameter analysis of the KiDS-1000 weak-lensing survey \cite{fluri_full_2022}, and for a simulation-based inference analysis of DES-Y3 \cite{thomsen_dark_2026}. It is this suite that the pipeline of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} takes as its accuracy target.


\subsubsection{Lightcones and Snapshots}
\label{sec:lightcones}

An $N$-body simulation naturally outputs \emph{snapshots}: the full particle phase-space (positions and velocities) within a periodic box of fixed comoving side length, at a fixed time. Reproducing how we actually observe the Universe, however, requires a \emph{lightcone}: an observer at a fixed spacetime point receives light emitted from progressively more distant (and hence earlier) regions as one looks to higher redshift, so a simulated lightcone must record particles at the redshift-dependent comoving distance from which they are being observed, not at a single global time. Because the lightcone is organised by radius in this way, it is naturally stored as a set of \emph{shells}: concentric radial slices centred on the observer, each spanning a narrow range of comoving distance and therefore of redshift, and each recorded as a full-sky map of the particles that crossed the lightcone within it \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}.

Because the depth of the desired lightcone (out to some $z_{\max}$) is typically larger than a single simulation box, the box is periodically replicated around a centrally placed observer: replication exploits the fact that a simulation with periodic boundary conditions can, in principle, represent an arbitrarily large volume, at the cost of the same structures reappearing at multiple replica images along a given line of sight \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. The trade-off between box size, structure repetition, and the resulting bias or excess variance in projected lightcone statistics is discussed in more detail by \citeauthor{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023} \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}, who additionally introduce a shell-permutation scheme that mitigates the repeated-structure problem by drawing different radial shells of a single lightcone from independent simulations of the same cosmology. The shell builder developed in this thesis (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}) implements the same replication idea directly inside the time integration, rather than as a post-processing step.

\section{Machine-Learning Background}
\label{sec:sr-methods}

The resolution deficit between \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s particle-mesh force solve and \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s tree-based one is what the correction $f$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} is built to remove. Before turning to that correction directly, this section reviews the machine-learning background it draws on. A central design question for any model acting on the full-sky lightcone maps of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} is how it can process an input of \mbox{$\sim 5\times10^7$} pixels per shell without exceeding the memory of a single GPU, and the choice of architecture and training paradigm shapes how that question is answered.

\subsection{The Memory Problem on the Sphere}
\label{subsec:sr-problem}

Cosmological and astrophysical observables are frequently recorded as spherical sky maps: the Cosmic Microwave Background, neutral-hydrogen intensity maps, galaxy clustering, and gravitational lensing have all been analysed in this form. The conventional route is to compress such a map into a summary statistic -- a power spectrum, a higher-order correlation function \cite{gatti_dark_2024} -- and to analyse that instead of the map. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) offer a different bargain, and it is the one that
motivates working at the map level at all: rather than fixing the statistic in advance and hoping it is sufficient, one lets the network discover whatever compression of the map carries the most information about the parameters \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024}, learning it from simulations that already contain the noise it will have to tolerate \cite{fluri_full_2022, perraudin_deepsphere_2019}.


Applying a CNN directly on the sphere, however, is not a straightforward extension of the flat-image case, and raises two distinct challenges. First, a square pixel grid has discrete translation symmetries that make convolution, and the shift of a filter by one pixel, well defined; no grid on the sphere shares a perfectly symmetric structure of this kind, so moving a spherical filter requires some form of interpolation \cite{cohen_spherical_2018}. Second, cost. Equivariance under rotation is the property one would want such a network to have, but the rotation group \mbox{$SO(3)$} is three-dimensional, and a correlation computed over it therefore carries three angular integrals rather than the two of a flat convolution \cite{cohen_spherical_2018}.

Two classical tools mitigate the cost of working at full spherical resolution. The first is a change of basis: expanding a field in spherical harmonics rather than pixels, made fast in practice by the spherical-harmonic transform (SHT) of the HEALPix pixelisation \cite{gorski_healpix_2005}.

The saving is not in the raw parameter count. \textsc{CosmoGridV1} stores each shell as a HEALPix map at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$}, that is \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{pix}}=$\mbox{$12\cdot N_{\mathrm{side}}^2$}$= 5.0\times10^{7}$} pixels, which is already a heavy compression of the \mbox{$832^3 \approx 5.8\times10^{8}$} particles the simulation evolves \cite{gorski_healpix_2005}.  Expanding one such map in spherical harmonics to the band limit the pixelisation supports, \mbox{$\ell_{\max} = 3N_{\mathrm{side}}-1 = 6143$} , gives \mbox{$(\ell_{\max}+1)^2 = 3.8\times10^{7}$} independent real coefficients -- essentially the same count as the pixels it came from, so changing basis buys nothing on its own \cite{sullivan_methods_2024}. What it buys is the form the covariance takes in the new basis. For a statistically isotropic field, invariance under rotations fixes the second-order statistics to
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle a_{\ell m}\, a^{*}_{\ell' m'} \big\rangle = C_\ell\, \delta_{\ell \ell'}\, \delta_{m m'},
  \label{eq:alm_cov}
\end{equation}
that is, \mbox{$m$} drops out \cite{challinor_lecture_2009}: different modes are uncorrelated, and the variance of each depends only on \mbox{$\ell$}. A multiplicative correction in pixel space carries one free number per pixel, \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{pix}})$}, whereas the transfer function \mbox{$T(\ell)$} of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer} carries one per multipole, \mbox{$\mathcal{O}(\ell_{\max})$}: 6\,144 numbers against 50 million at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$}, a factor of some 8\,000. The band limit \mbox{$\ell \le \ell_{\max}$} is a separate and additional saving, truncating the representation further and making the transform itself cheaper.

Harmonic-space representations also have a history inside deep learning itself. A Fourier neural operator parametrises the kernel of an integral operator directly in Fourier space and keeps only a fixed number of low modes \cite{li_fourier_2021}. Because that budget is fixed in advance, the network's size and cost no longer depend on how finely the input happens to be sampled: one trained at low resolution can be evaluated at high resolution without retraining. The correction pipeline of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer} shares part of that property. Its transfer function is indexed by multipole, which labels an angular scale on the sky rather than a position on a grid, so the same \mbox{$T(\ell)$} applies to a map at any \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}$} -- though only within the band it was fitted on, so extending it to smaller scales means refitting rather than simply evaluating. The two patch-based pipelines have no such freedom at all: their features are learned at a fixed pixel scale, which is why all three must be scored at a common resolution (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}).

The second tool is architectural: rather than making the transform cheaper, avoid ever presenting the whole map to the network at once.
\citeauthor{ronneberger_u-net_2015} face the same wall and step around it by cutting the image into overlapping tiles and segmenting each in turn, which makes the memory budget a constraint on the tile rather than on the image: an arbitrarily large input becomes tractable because the network never sees all of it at once \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}. Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow} and~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} borrow that idea wholesale, tiling gnomonic projections of the sphere instead of a plane. The alternative is to build the sphere into the architecture itself, as \textsc{DeepSphere} does by treating the HEALPix grid as a graph \cite{perraudin_deepsphere_2019, defferrard_deepsphere_2020}; the tiling route was taken here because it lets a standard planar U-Net be reused unchanged, at the cost of having to stitch the sphere back together (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}).

\subsection{Architectures}
\label{subsec:architectures}

\subsubsection{U-Net}
\label{sssec:unet-arch}

The U-Net was designed for segmenting biomedical images, a setting in which labelled examples are scarce enough that the architecture has to be economical with them \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}. Its shape gives it both -- its name and its behaviour (Figure~\ref{fig:unet_generic}). The network first descends a contracting path (encoder), trading spatial precision for an ever wider view of the image, and then climbs back out along a mirrored expanding path (decoder) that restores the resolution surrendered on the way down. The two halves answer different questions -- what is here, and exactly where is it -- and the architecture is built so that neither answer has to be sacrificed for the other \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}.

In the encoder, each pooling step halves the grid and doubles the number of channels, and the effect is the one you get by stepping back from a picture: the deeper a unit sits, the wider the region of the input it draws on, and the better it can say \emph{what} is in that region -- but the coarser the grid it sits on, so the less precisely it can say \emph{where}. Depth buys context at the cost of location. The decoder undoes the resolution loss, doubling the grid at each step, and at every level it is handed the encoder's feature map of matching size to place alongside its own. This is the \emph{skip connection}, and it is what makes the architecture work: the upsampled path knows what is present but has lost track of exactly where, while the encoder map at that same resolution still knows. Combining the two gives the decoder both, and the positional detail never has to pass through the coarse bottleneck at all \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}.

\subsection{Distribution-Matching Objectives}
\label{subsec:generative-paradigms}

The two learned pipelines of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} rest on a common idea, and it is an idea about the training objective rather than about randomness. A pixel-wise squared error against the target map has the conditional mean as its optimum \cite{hastie_elements_2009}, and that mean averages over every small-scale realisation consistent with the input, smoothing away exactly the variance the correction exists to restore \cite{ledig_photo-realistic_2017}. A map corrected that way would carry the right large-scale structure over a measurably under-dispersed small-scale field.

Both pipelines therefore optimise an objective whose solution is the target \emph{distribution}: flow matching by regressing a velocity field that transports one distribution onto the other \cite{lipman_flow_2023, liu_flow_2022}, diffusion by learning to denoise at every noise level \cite{ho_denoising_2020, karras_elucidating_2022}.

This difference returns, with practical consequences, when full-sky maps are assembled from overlapping patches, so that most sky pixels are predicted several times over and the predictions must be combined. For the deterministic flow this is harmless: every tile covering a pixel returns the same answer, and averaging identical predictions changes nothing. For the diffusion model it is destructive, because each tile draws its own independent realisation and averaging independent draws suppresses their amplitude \cite{wiegand_kish_1968} -- the same smoothing as before, arriving through the blend rather than through the loss. Reconciling overlapping generations into one coherent image is a known difficulty for diffusion models applied tile-wise \cite{bar-tal_multidiffusion_2023}; Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches} gives the size of the effect here and how it is avoided.


\subsubsection{Flow Matching}
\label{sssec:flow-matching}

Flow matching trains a continuous normalising flow (CNF) by plain regression rather than by simulation: the network is fitted to a velocity field that is available in closed form, so at no point during training does an ordinary differential equation (ODE) have to be integrated, which is what earlier CNF training required, and what made it expensive. A second restriction also falls away. The probability path supplying the supervision need not be a diffusion; any path whose velocity can be written down will serve \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. The same idea arrived independently as rectified flow \cite{liu_flow_2022} and as stochastic interpolants \cite{albergo_building_2023}. Let $x_1$ be distributed according to an unknown data distribution $q(x_1)$, of which only samples are available, and let $p_t$ be a probability path with $p_0$ a simple distribution (e.g.\ the standard normal) and $p_1 \approx q$. Given a target path $p_t$ and the vector field $u_t$ that generates it, the FM objective regresses a learned vector field $v_\theta$ onto $u_t$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta)
      = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_t}\big\lVert v_\theta(x_t, t) - u_t(x_t) \big\rVert^2,
      \qquad t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1],\quad x_t \sim p_t,
    \label{eq:flow_matching}
\end{equation}
so that a model reaching zero loss generates $p_t(x)$ by integrating the ordinary differential equation (ODE) $\dot{x} = v_t(x)$. Used naively this objective is intractable -- no closed form for a suitable $(p_t, u_t)$ pair is generally available -- but \citeauthor{lipman_flow_2023} construct both from probability paths and vector fields defined only \emph{per sample}, whose aggregation provably recovers the marginal $p_t$ and $u_t$ \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. The simplest such construction, used in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}, is the straight-line (linear) interpolant between a start point $x_0 \sim p_0$ and a data point $x_1$,
\begin{equation}
    x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1, \qquad u_t = x_1 - x_0,
    \label{eq:linear_interpolant}
\end{equation}
sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:flow_interp}: the target velocity is constant along each path, and the straighter the true transport between $p_0$ and $p_1$, the fewer ODE integration steps inference needs.

Two properties of this construction are used directly in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}. First, \mbox{$p_0$} need not be noise. Any distribution from which samples are available will do, including the low-fidelity maps themselves, and the closer \mbox{$p_0$} sits to \mbox{$p_1$} the shorter and straighter the transport. Second, once \mbox{$x_0$} is fixed the map is deterministic: the ODE \mbox{$\dot x = v_\theta(x,t)$} has a unique solution through each starting point, so all the randomness of a flow-matching sampler enters through the draw of \mbox{$x_0$}. Starting from noise therefore gives a generative sampler, whereas starting from a given map -- the choice made in this thesis -- gives a deterministic transport from that map to its corrected counterpart.

\subsubsection{Diffusion}
\label{sssec:diffusion-theory}

Diffusion models \cite{ho_denoising_2020, song_score-based_2021} approach generation from the opposite end: an image is progressively destroyed by noise, and the network is trained to undo that destruction one step at a time. The construction works as readily with conditioning as without it, and on image quality it has drawn level with generative adversarial networks (GANs) and in places moved past them \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Denoting the data distribution $p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)$ with standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}$, one considers the family of mollified distributions $p(x;\sigma)$ obtained by adding i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ to the data; for $\sigma_{\max}\gg\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}$, $p(x;\sigma_{\max})$ is practically indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise. A diffusion model samples a noise image $x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ and sequentially denoises it through decreasing noise levels $\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_0>\sigma_1>\dots>\sigma_N=0$, so that the endpoint is distributed according to the data \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. The process can equivalently be described by a probability-flow ODE that continuously raises or lowers the noise level as time runs forward or backward; \cite{karras_elucidating_2022} disentangle the design choices this leaves open -- noise schedule, network preconditioning, loss weighting, and ODE solver -- into the modular ``EDM'' (Elucidated Diffusion Model)formulation whose concrete instantiation in this thesis is given in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Compared to the flow above, which starts from the low-fidelity map rather than from noise and therefore travels a short, nearly straight path, a diffusion sampler always starts from pure noise. Its trajectory is genuinely curved and needs materially more solver steps, and its output changes with the noise draw. That is the price, and the point, of a genuinely generative process: it is the one model in this thesis that can supply small-scale structure it did not receive from the input.

\vspace{0.5cm}
Figure~\ref{fig:generative_objectives} is a synthetic illustration rather than a measurement: one low-fidelity input is constructed to be consistent with \emph{two} equally plausible small-scale outcomes, drawn as the two humps of the target distribution \mbox{$p(x_1 \mid x_0)$}. That is the essential feature of the real correction problem reduced to one dimension.

\paragraph{(a) What squared error returns.} The pale histogram is the true target
distribution, sampled: two well-separated modes, one near \mbox{$-2$} and one near
\mbox{$+2$}, marked by the green and purple diamonds below the axis. Either is a correct
answer. The dashed orange line is the minimiser of the per-pixel squared error,
\begin{equation}
  f^\star(x_0) \;=\; \arg\min_f\; \mathbb{E}\big[\big(f(x_0) - x_1\big)^2\big]
  \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[x_1 \mid x_0\big],
  \label{eq:mse_optimum}
\end{equation}
the conditional mean, and the orange diamond marks the single number it returns. It
lands at \mbox{$-0.07$}, in the valley between the two modes, where the true distribution
has almost no support: the answer it gives is not one of the possible answers. Nothing
about neural networks enters Eq.~\eqref{eq:mse_optimum} -- any regressor with enough
capacity converges there, because that is where the loss is minimised
\cite{hastie_elements_2009}. The prediction is
therefore wrong in the sense of being unrepresentative rather than merely imprecise, and
it carries \emph{no} spread at all, against a target whose own spread is of order
\mbox{$\pm 2$}. That is the under-dispersion described above, in its starkest form.

\paragraph{(b) What flow matching does instead.} The horizontal axis is now the flow
time \mbox{$t$}, running from the input at \mbox{$t=0$} to the target at \mbox{$t=1$}, and
the vertical axis is the interpolated state \mbox{$x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1$}. Each thin grey
line is one training pair: a single low-fidelity example joined to its own high-fidelity
partner along the straight-line path of Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear_interpolant}. The grey arrows
are the quantity the network \mbox{$v_\theta$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_matching} is actually
fitted to, the marginal velocity field
\begin{equation}
  u_t(x) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[x_1 - x_0 \;\big|\; x_t = x\big],
  \qquad
  \dot x = u_t(x)
  \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
  x(0) \sim p_0 \;\longmapsto\; x(1) \sim p_1 ,
  \label{eq:marginal_velocity}
\end{equation}
estimated here by kernel-averaging those individual paths. Note that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:marginal_velocity} is itself a conditional mean, built exactly the way the
failed prediction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mse_optimum} was -- and yet its right-hand side says
that integrating it carries the \emph{whole} distribution from \mbox{$p_0$} to
\mbox{$p_1$}. The three thick curves integrate that
field as an ordinary differential equation from three starting points differing by only a
few hundredths, and the diamonds mark where they arrive. They do not converge on the mean.
They separate, and land near the true modes. Averaging the \emph{local velocities} and then
integrating is not the same operation as averaging the \emph{targets}, and only the first
reconstructs the distribution; this is the marginalisation property proved by
\citeauthor{lipman_flow_2023} \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. The orange trajectory starts closest
to the degenerate midpoint and is the one that resolves least cleanly, which is the honest
limit of a short deterministic transport.

\paragraph{(c) What diffusion does.} The third panel is drawn differently on purpose,
because diffusion here is not a transport map integrated once but an iterative denoiser
(Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}). The horizontal axis is the denoising schedule, with the
noise level \mbox{$\sigma$} decreasing from left to right as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:karras_schedule},
and each violin is exactly one of the mollified distributions \mbox{$p(x;\sigma)$} of
Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}: the shape of the whole distribution of \mbox{$x$} once
Gaussian noise of that width has been added, following the same corruption relation
\mbox{$x = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$} that the denoiser \mbox{$D_\theta$} is trained to
invert. The optimum of that training is again a conditional mean, and again it is not the
whole story:
\begin{equation}
  D^\star(x;\sigma) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[x_1 \;\big|\; x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon = x\big],
  \qquad
  \nabla_{\!x} \log p(x;\sigma) \;=\; \frac{D^\star(x;\sigma) - x}{\sigma^2} ,
  \label{eq:denoiser_score}
\end{equation}
so that one denoiser at one noise level is a smoothed best guess, exactly like
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mse_optimum}, while the \emph{family} of them across all \mbox{$\sigma$}
delivers the score of every mollified distribution, and therefore
\mbox{$p_{\mathrm{data}}$} itself \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. That is what the panel
shows. On the left, at \mbox{$\sigma_{\max}$}, there is a single broad featureless hump: no
information about which mode is correct is present at all. Moving right, as \mbox{$\sigma$}
falls, that hump visibly splits, and by the last step the distribution has resolved into the
two-mode shape of the target. Nothing is being transported along a field; what changes is
the shape of the density itself. The three marked paths follow individual samples through
the same schedule, and the orange one carries the point: its true value belongs to the
left-hand mode, yet when fully noised it sits far up inside the right-hand mode's
territory, and the denoising still returns it to the correct side. A noisy start carries no
information about which answer is right, which is precisely why the diffusion sampler must
begin from noise and why it needs many more steps than the flow (Table~\ref{tab:pipelines}).

\chapter{Methods and implementation}
\section{Pipeline Architecture}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The reference suite \textsc{CosmoGridV1} evolves every realisation with \textsc{PkdGrav3} (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), whose FMM force solver and adaptive time-stepping resolve structure accurately down to small scales. That accuracy is expensive: each lightcone costs many node-hours, so the large number of realisations required for map-level, simulation-based inference dominates the total cost of the analysis \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023, potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. \textsc{Disco-DJ} offers the opposite trade-off: its particle-mesh solver (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}) reproduces the same large-scale clustering in minutes rather than hours, but the force resolution is capped by the PM grid, so power is progressively lost on small scales, exactly the scales that carry much of the non-Gaussian information exploited by map-level analyses \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.

The aim of this work is therefore to keep \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s speed while recovering the small-scale content that \textsc{PkdGrav3} would have produced. Formally, we seek a correction $f$ acting on the cheap lightcone maps such that
\begin{equation}
  f\!\left(\mathrm{Map}_{\textsc{disco}}\right) \;\approx\; \mathrm{Map}_{\textsc{CosmoGridV1}},
  \label{eq:correction_goal}
\end{equation}
where both maps are generated from the same cosmology and the same realisation of the initial density field, so that \mbox{$f$} has only to model the resolution-dependent difference between the two solvers, not any change of phases. The match is close but not exact: the reference is CosmoGridV1's own lightcone, evolved from concept's forward linear input, whereas Disco-DJ starts from the backscaled load rebuilt here. Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation} measures what that leaves behind — a sub-percent, redshift-dependent offset in the large-scale amplitude. The requirement -- \emph{matched initial conditions, same output format} -- dictates the entire architecture of the pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview}), which consists of three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Initial-condition generation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}). \textsc{CosmoGridV1} publishes its cosmologies and shell tables, but not initial conditions that \textsc{Disco-DJ} could consume directly. For every cosmology we therefore rebuild the exact linear input: the \emph{CDM+baryon} transfer function, extracted from the \textsc{CosmoGridV1} run's stored \textsc{concept} perturbations, with a matched \textsc{class} solve supplying the derived radiation density. The table describes the CDM+baryon field rather than the total matter field, because the particles carry cold dark matter and baryons only and neutrinos never become particles. This does not discard the neutrinos: the table is extracted from a solve that contained them, so their free-streaming suppression of CDM+baryon growth is already imprinted on it, and their mass is separately retained in the background expansion both codes integrate (Sec.~\ref{sec:power_spectrum}).\textsc{PkdGrav3} then generates backscaled initial conditions from it, and the resulting particle state is handed to \textsc{Disco-DJ} unmodified. The residual difference between the two output maps is then dominated by the force solvers, which is precisely the difference \mbox{$f$} must learn.
  \item \textbf{Shell building} (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}). \textsc{CosmoGridV1} stores its lightcone as 69 HEALPix particle-count shells out to $z=3.5$ at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$ \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. \textsc{Disco-DJ}, however, ships only a restricted lightcone mode limited to the simulation box volume, far short of the \mbox{$z=3.5$} depth, which requires many periodic box replications (Sec.~\ref{sec:lightcones}). We therefore implemented a dedicated \emph{shell builder} inside \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s time integration (\texttt{shell\_builder/}) that reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition and \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s shell binning exactly, so the low- and high-fidelity maps are defined shell-by-shell on identical radial bins.
  \item \textbf{Correction pipelines} (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}). On the resulting paired shells, three independent corrections $f$ are built and compared: a deterministic \emph{transfer-function} rescaling in harmonic space, a \emph{flow-matching U-Net} that transports each map patch from low to high fidelity, and an \emph{EDM diffusion} model that generates the missing small-scale residual from noise. All three are evaluated with the same shared statistics (per-shell and tomographic $C_\ell$, one-point moments, and weak-lensing convergence maps built from the corrected lightcone).
\end{enumerate}

Two structurally different routes to $f$ coexist in stage 3, and keeping both is deliberate. The harmonic-space route corrects the angular power spectrum $C_\ell$ mode-amplitude by mode-amplitude: it is cheap, exact on the sphere, and targets the deficit exactly where Eq.~\eqref{eq:cl} relates it to $P(k)$, but it corrects a summary statistic rather than the full field. The image-level route is expressive enough to restore \emph{structure}, not just power, but a full-sky HEALPix map at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$} (\mbox{$\sim 5\times10^7$} pixels) does not fit through a CNN on a single GPU, so it must be tiled into patches and stitched back (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}). This is the memory problem anticipated in Sec.~\ref{subsec:sr-problem}. The remainder of this chapter follows the pipeline in data order: initial conditions, shell builder, correction pipelines, and finally the computational infrastructure everything runs on.

\subsection{Reconstructing the Linear Input and Handing It Over}
\label{subsec:ic_linear}
\label{subsec:ic_why_tk}
\label{subsec:ic_neutrinos}
\label{subsec:ic_handoff}

\textsc{CosmoGridV1} does not distribute initial conditions, and the way its own runs were
started cannot be reused here. Recovering a starting point that both solvers can share
therefore takes three steps: establishing what has to be rebuilt, rebuilding it, and
checking that the two codes really do start from the same thing.

\paragraph{Why a tabulated transfer function is needed at all.} The published
configuration drives \textsc{PkdGrav3} in its \texttt{bClass=1} mode, in which the
cosmology is not specified through parameters but read wholesale from an HDF5 file
produced by \textsc{concept} \cite{dakin_cosmological_2022}, which calls \textsc{class}
internally and tabulates the full linear perturbation history of every species. In that
mode \textsc{PkdGrav3} does more than draw a particle load: it carries photons, the
massive-neutrino species, the dark-energy fluid and the metric perturbations as
\emph{linear} fields on their own $208^3$ grid
(\texttt{achLinSpecies = "g+ncdm[0]+fld+metric"}, \texttt{nGridLin = 208}) and adds
their gravitational effect to the $832^3$ nonlinear particles at every step. The linear
cosmology is an active ingredient throughout the run, not a boundary condition at the
start.

\textsc{Disco-DJ} has no counterpart to that machinery: it is a pure particle-mesh code
(Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}) and evolves particles under the Poisson equation alone. Were
\textsc{PkdGrav3} run with its linear species active while \textsc{Disco-DJ} was not, the two
lightcones would differ through the treatment of photons, neutrinos and dark energy
\emph{in addition} to the force solver, and the correction of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:correction_goal} would be learning that difference too, defeating the
controlled comparison the whole thesis rests on. Both runs must therefore start from
one plain particle load with no linear species carried afterwards, which forces
\textsc{PkdGrav3} into \texttt{bClass=0} and replaces the HDF5 reference by
\texttt{achTfFile}, a static tabulated transfer function. \textsc{CosmoGridV1} ships no such
table, so it has to be reconstructed.

\paragraph{Rebuilding it.} Each \textsc{CosmoGridV1} run ships the linear perturbations its own
initial conditions were built from, as tabulated CDM+baryon density contrasts
$\delta_{cb}(k, a)$. From the $a=1$ slice the dimensional power spectrum is
reconstructed as
\begin{equation}
  P_{cb}(k) \;=\; \frac{2\pi^2}{k^3}\, P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k)\, \delta_{cb}^2(k)\, h^3,
  \qquad P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k) = A_s \left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{pivot}}}\right)^{n_s - 1},
  \label{eq:pk_reconstruct}
\end{equation}
with $k_{\mathrm{pivot}} = 0.05\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ and $(A_s, n_s, h)$ read from the
run's parameter file (\texttt{get\_transfer\_function.py}), and exported in the
\textsc{CAMB} table convention that \textsc{PkdGrav3} reads directly. Two
derived quantities complete the configuration file. The clustering amplitude follows
from the same spectrum through the standard top-hat integral
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{8,cb}^2 \;=\; \int \frac{k^2\,P_{cb}(k)}{2\pi^2}\, W^2(kR)\;\mathrm{d}k,
  \qquad W(x) = \frac{3\,(\sin x - x\cos x)}{x^{3}},\quad R = 8\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h,
  \label{eq:sigma8}
\end{equation}
and the radiation density $\Omega_r$ from a \textsc{class} solve of the same cosmology,
with the neutrino sector declared as \textsc{concept} declares it: one species of three
degenerate states at the grid's fixed mass, at the temperature that reproduces
$N_{\rm eff} = 3.046$, with the cold dark matter density reduced accordingly so that
$\Omega_m$ is preserved. \texttt{IC\_preparation.py} writes these values, together
with $\Omega_0$ and $w_0$, into each run's \texttt{cosmology.par}, idempotently, so a
re-run only changes what is still inconsistent.

Getting the two codes to agree took four attempts, each fixing one ingredient of the
linear input; because that history is a result in its own right rather than a
specification, it is presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:res-validation} alongside the shell-builder
validation.


\paragraph{The hand-off.} With the configuration settled, \textsc{PkdGrav3} generates the
backscaled initial conditions: the $a=1$ spectrum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pk_reconstruct} is
scaled back to the starting redshift with the linear growth factor, and particles are
displaced accordingly at \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s resolution of $832^3$ particles in the
$900\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ box. The resulting positions and velocities are exported in the
\texttt{tipsy} format (\texttt{CosmoML.tipsy}) and used \emph{unmodified} as the initial
state of the corresponding \textsc{Disco-DJ} run, which evolves them to $a=1$ and accumulates
the lightcone with the shell builder of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder} over the same
redshift range and at the same HEALPix resolution. Where \textsc{PkdGrav3} is re-run here for validation (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation}) it is handed the same file, so that pair is byte-identical; the production reference remains \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s own lightcone, which shares its phases with this load but was evolved from a different linear input.

This load is deliberately \emph{not} a reproduction of \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s own native initial conditions, which came from \textsc{concept}'s forward evolution rather than from backscaling a static table. The two share phases and are normalised to agree on the large-scale power spectrum, but a single scale-independent growth factor cannot reproduce a scale-dependent forward evolution at every redshift at once: the construction is anchored where the table is taken, at $z=0$, and drifts slowly away from it towards higher redshift. That drift is not a hidden approximation. It is
measurable, and Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation} quantifies it shell by shell as a sub-percent, monotonically redshift-dependent offset in the large-scale amplitude of the resulting lightcone.

\section{Shell Builder}
\label{sec:shell_builder}

With matched initial conditions in place, the remaining prerequisite for Eq.~\eqref{eq:correction_goal} is that \textsc{Disco-DJ} emit its lightcone in exactly \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s format: particle-count maps on the same $S=69$ radial shells. This section describes the shell builder (\texttt{shell\_builder/Shell\_Builder.py}) that adds this capability to \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s time integration.

\subsection{Streaming Shell Accumulation}
\label{sec:shell_builder_core}

A lightcone output requires the particle distribution to be resampled at every integration step, since the observable past-lightcone shell changes continuously as the universe evolves. The most direct implementation, store the full particle snapshot at each of the \mbox{$n_{\mathrm{steps}}$} integration steps and decompose into shells afterwards, is very impractical: retaining \mbox{$n_{\mathrm{steps}}$} full-resolution snapshots simultaneously exceeds the available device memory for the particle counts considered here  (cf.\ Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}).
The shell decomposition is therefore \emph{interleaved} with the time integration, so that at any moment only the particle states immediately before and after the current step, plus the low-dimensional set of pixelised count maps, are held in memory (Figure~\ref{fig:shell_stream}).

\paragraph{Time discretization.} The radial shells are not defined by the integrator but
supplied externally, through a \textsc{CosmoGridV1}-format metainfo table: each shell
$s \in \{1,\dots,S\}$ is a redshift interval $[z_s^{\mathrm{lo}}, z_s^{\mathrm{hi}}]$ with a
corresponding comoving-distance interval $[r_s^{\mathrm{lo}}, r_s^{\mathrm{hi}}]$. Since the
shells are contiguous, their edges form a single ordered sequence of boundaries
$z_0 > z_1 > \dots > z_S$, which we take directly as the integration nodes, so that each shell
coincides with exactly one integration interval:
\begin{equation}
  a_0 < a_1 < \dots < a_S, \qquad a_j = \frac{1}{1+z_j}, \qquad
  \text{shell } s = [\,a_{s-1},\ a_s\,], \quad s = 1,\dots,S.
  \label{eq:step_grid}
\end{equation}
No shell edge is therefore ever crossed within a step. The interval
$[a_{\mathrm{ini}}, a_0]$ preceding the first shell is integrated separately with
$n_{\mathrm{pre}}$ sub-steps and without shell accumulation, to bring the particle state into
registry with the grid of Eq.~\eqref{eq:step_grid}; each of the remaining $S$ intervals
$[a_j, a_{j+1}]$, $j = 0,\dots,S-1$, is then advanced with a single sub-step. The practical consequence is that the time steps are not chosen for numerical convenience but inherited: the integration nodes are \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s shell edges. This is why both the shell binning and the step grid are supplied externally rather than set inside the integrator, and it is what allows the two lightcones to be compared shell by shell on identical radial bins, with no rebinning after the fact.


\paragraph{Accumulation.} At step $j$, the comoving positions of all particles are recorded before and after the sub-step, $\{\mathbf{x}_p^{(j)}\}$ and $\{\mathbf{x}_p^{(j+1)}\}$, and the step's scale-factor edges are converted to comoving lightcone radii through the cosmology-dependent map $\chi(a)$,
\begin{equation}
  R_j = \chi(a_j), \qquad R_{j+1} = \chi(a_{j+1}), \qquad R_j > R_{j+1},
  \label{eq:step_radii}
\end{equation}
so the step sweeps the annulus between the near radius \mbox{$R_{j+1}$} and the far radius \mbox{$R_j$}. Because the integration nodes are the shell edges (Eq.~\eqref{eq:step_grid}), that annulus is a shell: step \mbox{$j$} sweeps shell \mbox{$s = j+1$} and no other, so there is no set of affected shells to determine and no shell is touched by more than one step. The crossing test of Sec.~\ref{sec:crossing} is applied to the pre- and post-step states once for each periodic replica \mbox{$\mathbf{d}$} (Eq. \eqref{eq:replica_set}), and every accepted crossing is pixelised into a HEALPix count map \mbox{$\mathbf{M}_s(\mathbf{d})$}. Accumulation therefore runs over replicas within the step rather than over steps,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{S}_s \;=\; \sum_{\mathbf{d}\,\in\,\mathcal{D}} \mathbf{M}_s(\mathbf{d}), \qquad s = j+1,
  \label{eq:shell_update}
\end{equation}
and the shell is complete once its own step has been processed. The pre-step state is then discarded and the post-step state becomes the next step's pre-step state. Once all steps are processed, the per-shell maps $\{\mathbf{S}_s\}_{s=1}^{S}$ are serialized together with the shell table. At no point are more than two full particle snapshots and \mbox{$S$} integer maps of size \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{pix}}$} resident. This is what removes the memory bottleneck of the naive end-of-run decomposition.

\subsection{Crossing Condition}
\label{sec:crossing}

Which particles contribute to a shell, and where, is decided by the moving-lightcone formulation of \textsc{PkdGrav3} \cite{noauthor_examplepkdgrav3_lightcone_2022}, which the shell builder reproduces exactly (\texttt{Shell\_Builder.py} implements the same crossing equation as \texttt{lightcone.cxx}); Figure~\ref{fig:crossing} illustrates the two ingredients.

\paragraph{Periodic replicas.} 
The observer is placed at the box centre, \mbox{$\mathbf{o} = (L/2, L/2, L/2)$}, with \mbox{$L$} the box side length. These coordinates are dictated by \textsc{Disco-DJ}, which expects particle positions in \mbox{$[0, L)$}: the tipsy load therefore applies the canonical \mbox{$(x + 0.5)\,L$} transform (\texttt{disco/read\_tipsy\_file.py}), and that transform carries \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s origin $(0,0,0)$ -- where its own observer sits, in its native \mbox{$[-L/2, L/2)$} convention -- onto \mbox{$(L/2,\,L/2,\,L/2)$}. Both lightcones are therefore built about the same physical point, expressed in different conventions. Since the requested lightcone depth $\chi_{\max} = \chi\big(1/(1+z_{\max})\big)$ exceeds $L$, the simulation volume is periodically replicated (Sec.~\ref{sec:lightcones}): the finite replica set
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D} = \Big\{\, \mathbf{d} = (n_1, n_2, n_3)\,L \;:\; \mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^3, \ \; \text{replicated cube intersects the ball of radius } \chi_{\max} \text{ about } \mathbf{o} \,\Big\}
  \label{eq:replica_set}
\end{equation}
is precomputed once from $(\chi_{\max}, L)$. For each $\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}$ the nearest and farthest possible distances of the replicated cube from the observer,
\begin{equation}
  r_{\min}(\mathbf{d}) = \big\lVert \max(0, |\mathbf{d}| - L/2) \big\rVert, \qquad
  r_{\max}(\mathbf{d}) = \big\lVert |\mathbf{d}| + L/2 \big\rVert,
  \label{eq:replica_bounds}
\end{equation}
are also precomputed (componentwise $\max$ and absolute value, then the Euclidean norm), so that a replica whose radial range cannot overlap a step's lightcone annulus is rejected without evaluating a single particle.


\paragraph{Linear crossing formula.} For step $j$ with boundaries $a_j < a_{j+1}$, write $r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} = \chi(a_j)$ and $r_j^{\mathrm{lo}} = \chi(a_{j+1})$. For a particle with pre-/post-step positions $\mathbf{x}_p(a_j), \mathbf{x}_p(a_{j+1})$ relative to the observer and a replica offset $\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}$, define
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{R}_0 = \mathbf{x}_p(a_j) + \mathbf{d}, \qquad \mathbf{R}_1 = \mathbf{x}_p(a_{j+1}) + \mathbf{d}, \qquad
  r_0 = \lVert \mathbf{R}_0 \rVert, \qquad r_1 = \lVert \mathbf{R}_1 \rVert.
  \label{eq:replica_radii}
\end{equation}
Following \textsc{PkdGrav3}, both the particle radius $r_p(v)$ and the inward-moving lightcone front $R(v)$ are interpolated linearly over the step,
\begin{equation}
  r_p(v) = r_0 + v\,(r_1 - r_0), \qquad R(v) = r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - v\,(r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_j^{\mathrm{lo}}), \qquad v\in[0,1],
  \label{eq:scalar_interp}
\end{equation}
and the crossing $r_p(v^\ast) = R(v^\ast)$ is solved in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  v^\ast = \frac{r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_0}{\big(r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_j^{\mathrm{lo}}\big) + (r_1 - r_0)}.
  \label{eq:vx_formula}
\end{equation}
A particle--replica pair is accepted only if $v^\ast \in [0,1)$. Since the half-open acceptance intervals of consecutive steps partition the time axis, every particle is caught in at most one step per replica; evaluating the crossing test against the (generally narrower) metainfo shell bounds instead of the step bounds would break this partition and could double-count particles crossing two adjacent shells within one step.

\paragraph{Shell assignment and pixelization.} The crossing position is obtained by applying $v^\ast$ to the vector positions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}} = (1-v^\ast)\,\mathbf{R}_0 + v^\ast\,\mathbf{R}_1, \qquad r_{\mathrm{cross}} = \lVert \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}} \rVert,
  \label{eq:cross_pos}
\end{equation}
and the pair is kept only if its crossing radius falls inside the shell bounds,
\begin{equation}
  r_s^{\mathrm{lo}} \le r_{\mathrm{cross}} < r_s^{\mathrm{hi}}.
  \label{eq:shell_filter}
\end{equation}
Surviving crossings are pixelised with the equal-area HEALPix \textsc{ring} scheme at resolution $N_{\mathrm{side}}$ from the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{cross}} = \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}}/r_{\mathrm{cross}}$, and the corresponding pixel of $\mathbf{M}_s^{(j)}$ is incremented. In the implementation, the loop over all active replicas runs inside a single JIT-compiled JAX kernel (\texttt{jax.lax.fori\_loop}), so the per-replica work is dispatched to the GPU as one program rather than from Python making it more efficient.

This procedure reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s box-replication lightcone
\cite{noauthor_examplepkdgrav3_lightcone_2022}. The replica set \mbox{$\mathcal{D}$}
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_set} is sized here from \mbox{$(\chi_{\max}, L)$} alone -- every
tile whose nearest corner falls within \mbox{$\chi_{\max}$} is retained, and those that
cannot meet a given step's annulus are rejected by Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_bounds}
(\texttt{shell\_builder/utils/compute\_replica\_offsets}) -- so the number of replicas
never has to be set by hand.

Together, Secs.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} and \ref{sec:shell_builder} deliver what the learning problem needs: for every cosmology, two lightcones on matched initial conditions and identical shells, whose remaining difference is dominated by the force solver (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation}). The next section builds the corrections that close that difference.

\section{Correction Pipelines}
\label{sec:correction-pipelines}

Three correction pipelines were implemented, each a self-contained module under \texttt{ml/} (\texttt{transfer/}, \texttt{unet/}, \texttt{diffusion/}) sharing only the neutral analysis library \texttt{ml/analysis/}. They span the two paths identified in Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} -- one deterministic rescaling in harmonic space and two learned, patch-based models, of which only the diffusion model is generative -- and are deliberately evaluated through the same shared statistics and plotting code, so their outputs are comparable figure-by-figure. Table~\ref{tab:pipelines} summarises the contrast; Figure~\ref{fig:shared_problem} states the shared problem.

Two maps of the same patch of sky can disagree in only two ways: in how much power sits at each
scale, and in where on the sky that power is placed. Both are measured per multipole and per shell
across the training runs, and together they dictate every
design choice that follows.

The amplitude side is the ratio of the two angular power spectra, whose square root is the transfer
function \cite{feng_fastpm_2016, garrison_improving_2016}
\begin{equation}
  T(\ell, s) \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}(s)}{C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}(s)}},
  \label{eq:transfer_meas}
\end{equation}
the factor that would have to multiply
each \textsc{Disco-DJ} mode to reproduce the reference power. It is unity on large scales on every
shell, confirming that the particle-mesh solver gets those right, and rises once the multipole reaches
the scale where the mesh stops resolving structure: on the nearest shells \mbox{$T$} approaches
\mbox{$1.75$} by \mbox{$\ell = 1500$}, close to a factor \mbox{$3$} in \mbox{$C_\ell$}, while the most
distant shells stay within half a percent of unity across the whole measured range
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer}). This is the deficit every pipeline exists to close, and it says
\emph{how much} correction each scale of each shell needs.

What it does not say is whether multiplying by that factor is a legitimate way to supply it, and that is
the second measurement. The per-multipole cross-correlation \cite{feng_fastpm_2016}
\begin{equation}
  r(\ell, s) \;=\; \frac{ C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}\times\mathrm{high}}}{\sqrt{ C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}\, C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}}},
  \label{eq:phase_corr}
\end{equation}
where  \mbox{$C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}\times\mathrm{high}}$} is the cross-spectrum of the two maps of the same shell, measures whether the two maps put their structure in the same place. Over most of the lightcone they do: \mbox{$r$} stays between \mbox{$0.9$} and \mbox{$1.0$} out to high multipole.

\textsc{Disco-DJ} therefore mostly gets the small-scale \emph{amplitude} wrong rather than the \emph{positions} of the structures that carry it, which is why a pure amplitude rescaling (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer}) works at all. How well that works depends on how far \mbox{$r$} has fallen, and it degrades gradually rather than failing at a threshold: while the two maps still agree on where the structure is, multiplying the amplitude back restores that structure, but the further \mbox{$r$} drops the more the same multiplication amplifies \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s own noise instead.
The distant shells are not better resolved -- the mesh limit applies at every redshift -- their deficit is simply smaller and pushed beyond the band the map represents, so the two regimes differ in the kind of error rather than in whether there is one. Where the phases have decorrelated, the missing structure has to be supplied rather than amplified: a field whose statistics match the reference even where its features cannot coincide with the truth pixel by pixel. That is what the two patch-based corrections deliver -- the flow of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow} by transporting each low-fidelity patch onto the high-fidelity distribution, the diffusion model of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} by drawing the residual from noise -- and it is why both can repair \mbox{$r$} where the transfer function structurally cannot.

\paragraph{Correct only the small scales; pin the large scales to \textsc{Disco-DJ}.} The correction must not touch the already-correct large
scales, and ``large'' is a physical statement: the PM resolution deficit sets in at a fixed
comoving scale, which maps to a different multipole on every shell. Using the flat-sky (Limber) relation
\begin{equation}
  \ell + \tfrac{1}{2} = k\,\chi(z), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{L},
  \label{eq:limber}
\end{equation}
of \cite{loverde_extended_2008}, a fixed scale $L$ (calculated in Sec. \ref{sec:res-onset}) with wavenumber \mbox{$k = 2\pi/L$} is converted per shell to
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\min}(s) = \left\lceil \frac{2\pi\,\chi(z_s)}{L} - \frac{1}{2} \right\rceil
  \;\approx\; \left\lceil \frac{2\pi\,\chi(z_s)}{L} \right\rceil,
  \label{eq:ell_min}
\end{equation}
where the $\tfrac{1}{2}$, retained by \citeauthor{loverde_extended_2008} because dropping it degrades the Limber approximation at $\mathcal{O}(\ell^{-1})$, is negligible for the threshold multipoles $\ell_{\min}\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{2}\text{--}10^{3})$ relevant here, and is dropped in the implementation (\texttt{ell\_min\_from\_mpc\_h}). Below \mbox{$\ell_{\min}(s)$} nothing is corrected; above it the correction is applied in full, with no transition band. Each pipeline realises that cutoff in its own representation: the harmonic pipeline forces \mbox{$T = 1$} below \mbox{$\ell_{\min}(s)$} (Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_def}), while the two patch-based models put the same scale into the high-pass filter that defines their learning target. Choosing one angular cutoff for all shells instead would place it correctly on a single shell and wrongly on the other sixty-eight; converting a fixed \mbox{$L$} per shell places it correctly on every one. This matches the measured behaviour: nearby shells visibly deviate from \textsc{CosmoGridV1} from \mbox{$\ell \sim 150$}--\mbox{$300$}, while the most distant shells show no deviation out to
\mbox{$\ell = 3000$}, and correctly receive no correction, because their \mbox{$\ell_{\min}$} exceeds \mbox{$\ell_{\max}$}.

\paragraph{Hold out whole cosmologies, not random patches.} The train/validation split is by cosmology \\ (\texttt{dataset.split\_by\_cosmo}): patches from the same run share large-scale structure, so a random per-patch split would leak the validation set into training. All reported evaluations are on cosmologies the models never saw.

\subsection{Patch Extraction and Full-Sky Reconstruction}
\label{subsec:pipe-patches}

Training pairs are built by \texttt{make\_patch\_dataset.py}: for each patch a random cosmology and a random shell are drawn, together with a pointing and an orientation -- a centre pixel drawn uniformly on the sphere, fixing \emph{where} the patch sits, and a rotation angle about that centre, fixing \emph{how} the square is laid over the sky. Both the low and the high shell map are then projected at that same pointing and orientation, with a gnomonic (flat-sky) projection at the map's native pixel resolution: a like-for-like \mbox{$256\times256$} crop with no resampling beyond the projection itself. Each patch stores the metadata needed to recreate it exactly (centre, orientation, shell redshift bounds, comoving distance) together with the run's varied cosmological parameters $(\Omega_m, \Omega_b, n_s, \sigma_8, w_0, h)$ from the \textsc{CosmoGridV1} metainfo catalogue. From these, the conditioning vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c} = \big(h,\ \Omega_{cdm},\ \Omega_b,\ \Omega_m,\ n_s,\ \sigma_8,\ w_0,\ z_{\mathrm{shell}}\big) \in \mathbb{R}^8
  \label{eq:cosmo_z}
\end{equation} 
is assembled in one place (\texttt{dataset.cosmo\_z\_vector}) and consumed identically at train and inference time, so the two can never drift apart.

At inference, a full-sky shell is corrected by the overlap-tile scheme of Figure~\ref{fig:tiling} in \\ (\texttt{ml/analysis/patch\_tiling.py}). The sphere is tiled with overlapping gnomonic patches centred on a coarse HEALPix grid and the model corrects each tile independently, so a given sky pixel \mbox{$n$} generally receives a prediction from several tiles at once and those predictions have to be reduced to one number. Let \mbox{$\mathcal{T}(n)$} be the set of tiles covering pixel \mbox{$n$} and \mbox{$\hat m_i(n)$} the value tile \mbox{$i$} predicts there. The reconstruction is the weighted average
\begin{equation}
  \hat m(n) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{T}(n)} w_i(n)^{\,p}\; \hat m_i(n)}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{T}(n)} w_i(n)^{\,p}},
  \label{eq:tile_blend}
\end{equation}
where the weight \mbox{$w_i(n)$} says how far pixel \mbox{$n$} lies from the centre of tile \mbox{$i$}. Writing \mbox{$r_i(n)$} for that distance, measured in the tile's own flat coordinates and normalised so that \mbox{$r = 0$} at the tile centre and \mbox{$r = 1$} at its edge, the weight is the radial cosine taper
\begin{equation}
  w(r) = \tfrac12\big(1 + \cos \pi r\big), \qquad r \in [0,1],
  \label{eq:cosine_taper}
\end{equation}
which equals \mbox{$1$} at the centre and falls smoothly to \mbox{$0$} at the edge. A pixel is thus weighted most by the tile that sees it most centrally, and the exponent \mbox{$p$} controls how sharply that preference is enforced. The tile-to-sphere pixel indices depend only on the geometry, so they are built once and reused for every shell and cosmology, replacing a per-tile re-projection that would otherwise dominate the entire reconstruction cost.

The averaging in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tile_blend} is where the difference between the two patch-based pipelines becomes concrete, and it is the practical consequence of the distinction drawn in Sec.~\ref{subsec:generative-paradigms}. The flow is a deterministic transport map: started from the same low-fidelity patch it returns the same answer every time, so all tiles covering a pixel predict essentially the same value and averaging them is harmless, even beneficial, since it smooths the residual disagreement near tile edges. The default \mbox{$p=1$} is therefore kept for the flow.

The diffusion model is a genuine sampler, and for it the same average is destructive. Each tile would otherwise draw its own independent realisation of the small-scale field; those realisations are equally valid but mutually uncorrelated, so averaging them suppresses the generated amplitude to \mbox{$1/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}$} of its true value, where \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i^{\,p})^2 / \sum_i w_i^{\,2p}$} is the effective number of contributing tiles \cite{wiegand_kish_1968}. This is prevented by sharpening the taper to \mbox{$p=32$} (\texttt{apply\_diffusion.py}), which drives \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{eff}} \to 1$}: as \mbox{$p$} grows, \mbox{$w^{\,p}$} collapses onto the tile centre, so for each pixel the single nearest tile dominates the sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tile_blend} and the reconstruction becomes a soft Voronoi partition of the sky rather than an average.

\subsection{Harmonic-Space Transfer-Function Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipe-transfer}

The first pipeline (\texttt{ml/transfer/}) contains no image-level network at all. It acts on the two measurements of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} in the most direct way available: the amplitude deficit \mbox{$T(\ell, s)$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_def} multiplies every mode directly, while the phase correlation \mbox{$r$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr} is measured alongside it but never consulted. The correction is applied above \mbox{$\ell_{\min}(s)$} whatever \mbox{$r$} happens to be there. Every shell is transformed to its harmonic coefficients $a_{\ell m}$ (up to \mbox{$\ell_{\max}=1500$} in the production configuration, the band limit of the common \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$} footing of Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}), and each mode is rescaled by the transfer function of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_meas}, with the per-shell cutoff imposed,
\begin{equation}
  a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{corr}} = a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{low}} \cdot T(\ell, s),
  \qquad
  T(\ell, s) =
  \begin{cases}
    \sqrt{\dfrac{ C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}(s)}{C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}(s)}}, & \ell \ge \ell_{\min}(s),\\[2mm]
    1, & \ell < \ell_{\min}(s),
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:transfer_def}
\end{equation}
which by construction matches the target power spectrum, and leaves every mode below the per-shell onset $\ell_{\min}(s)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min} untouched. What it does to the phases is nothing at all, and that is worth stating precisely: \mbox{$T$} is real, positive and independent of \mbox{$m$}, so it rescales every coefficient without rotating any of them. The phase correlation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr} is therefore exactly invariant under the correction \mbox{$r^{\mathrm{corr}}(\ell,s) = r^{\mathrm{low}}(\ell,s)$}. This pipeline thus repairs \mbox{$T$} by construction and cannot move \mbox{$r$} by any choice of correction factor, which is exactly why the patch-based pipelines of Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow} and~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} are needed wherever \mbox{$r$} has decayed. Both spectra are smoothed before the ratio is taken: each is passed through a boxcar (moving-average) filter \cite{smith_chapter_2003} of \mbox{$21$} multipoles in \mbox{$\log_{10}$}-space, that is, \mbox{$\log_{10} C_\ell$} is replaced by its unweighted mean over the window \mbox{$[\ell-10, \ell+10]$}. The physical \mbox{$T(\ell)$} is smooth, whereas a single realisation's \mbox{$C_\ell$} carries sample-variance and shot-noise scatter that would otherwise be fitted and amplified by the correction.

In the implementation the correction is never applied by rebuilding the map from the rescaled coefficients. It is applied as a \emph{residual} added onto the unmodified \textsc{Disco-DJ} shell \mbox{$\delta_{\mathrm{low}}$}, at its own full pixel resolution,
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\mathrm{corr}} \;=\; \underbrace{\delta_{\mathrm{low}}}_{\text{all scales, untouched}}
  \;+\; \mathrm{SHT}^{-1}\!\Big[\big(T(\ell,s) - 1\big)\, a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{low}}\Big],
  \label{eq:transfer_residual}
\end{equation}
which is algebraically identical to Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_def} for every \mbox{$\ell \le \ell_{\max}$}, since \mbox{$a_{\ell m} + (T-1)a_{\ell m} = T a_{\ell m}$}. The two differ only outside that band, and in exactly the way that matters: below \mbox{$\ell_{\min}(s)$} the bracket is zero, so nothing is added and the large scales stay \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s bit for bit, while above \mbox{$\ell_{\max}$} the map $\delta_{\mathrm{low}}$ keeps whatever structure it holds beyond the transform's band limit instead of being truncated to it. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_flow} shows the apply chain.

\paragraph{Estimating $T$.} The estimator trains a small MLP emulator of $T$ on features $\big(\log_{10}(\ell{+}1),\ z,\ H_0,\ \Omega_{cdm},\ \Omega_b,\ \\\Omega_m, n_s,\ \sigma_8,\ \log_{10} C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}\big)$, standardised per feature; this interpolates the transfer function to unseen cosmologies of the grid. The emulator is a plain feed-forward network (hidden layers $256$--$256$--$128$, ReLU, linear output) implemented in PyTorch and trained on GPU (Adam, learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch size $4096$, $L_2$ weight decay $\alpha = 10^{-4}$), with a 15\% held-out validation split, early stopping, and best-validation weights restored (\texttt{transfer\_function.py}). The GPU implementation matters for a reason worth recording: the training set is millions of \mbox{$(\ell, s)$} rows with only 9 features each, a workload where a CPU implementation is bound by per-batch overhead rather than arithmetic. A GPU handles exactly this trivial-per-sample, huge-throughput shape. Both estimators also store the phase correlation $R(\ell, s)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr}.

    \label{fig:transfer_function_learned}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{What the emulator learns.} Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function_learned}
shows the two objects the pipeline actually runs on, as predicted for one
held-out cosmology, and both behave as the two measurements of
Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} lead one to expect. The transfer function \mbox{$T(\ell, s)$} stays at \mbox{$1$} on large scales and begins to rise near
\mbox{$\ell_{\min}(s)$}, the multipole at which the particle-mesh solver starts to lose power
on that shell. The nearest shell shown
(\mbox{$z \approx 0.09$}) needs a correction of \mbox{$T = 1.12$} at \mbox{$\ell = 500$},
\mbox{$1.46$} at \mbox{$\ell = 1000$} and \mbox{$1.75$} at \mbox{$\ell = 1500$} the intermediate shells progressively less and only at higher \mbox{$\ell$} (\mbox{$T = 1.15$}, \mbox{$1.05$} and \mbox{$1.02$} at \mbox{$\ell = 1500$} for \mbox{$z \approx 0.26$},
\mbox{$0.49$} and \mbox{$0.77$}), and the most distant shell (\mbox{$z \approx 2.2$}) stays
within half a percent of unity across the entire measured range. What the emulator applies to a shell is thus set almost
entirely by how far away that shell is. The phase correlation \mbox{$r(\ell, s)$} in the right-hand panel never enters the correction -- it is measured and stored, but $T$ is applied whatever its value -- yet it is what decides whether that correction recovers structure or merely repackages noise. 


\paragraph{Negative pixels.} The corrected field inevitably contains negative pixels, and it is worth being precise about why. The truth is a non-negative, integer count map, and on the nearest, sparsest shells the mean count per pixel lies far below unity, so most pixels are exactly zero. The correction, by contrast, is a band-limited real-space fluctuation field: the residual $(T-1)\,a_{\ell m}$ reconstructed by the inverse SHT is a smooth, zero-mean oscillation with both signs. Adding such a field onto a map dominated by empty pixels necessarily drives every pixel where the negative half-wave exceeds the local count below zero, negativity is a structural consequence of representing a boosted continuous overdensity on top of a nearly empty discrete map, not a numerical defect of the method. These are also the shells on which \mbox{$r$} has collapsed, and the coincidence is not accidental. Where the phases have decorrelated, the field being amplified is \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s own shot noise rather than recovered structure, and band-limited shot noise is a symmetric oscillation about zero. Every cheap pixel-wise repair was measured and rejected: flooring at zero deletes only the negative excursions, which raises the mean and injects spurious power exactly on the small scales the correction just restored, and re-balancing the mean afterwards or correcting the logarithm of the density instead merely redistributes the same bias. The corrected field is therefore deliberately not post-processed: it is treated as a continuous overdensity field (\texttt{--no-clip}) and passed unchanged into every downstream statistic -- angular power spectra, moments, and the weak-lensing convergence maps of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} -- none of which requires per-pixel count validity.

\subsection{U-Net Flow-Matching Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}

The second pipeline (\texttt{ml/unet/}) is the first of the two patch-based models: a conditional flow-matching model (Sec.~\ref{sssec:flow-matching}) acting on the gnomonic patches of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}. Its defining choice is the start point of the flow: instead of Gaussian noise, the ODE starts at the low-fidelity patch itself,
\begin{equation}
  x_0 = \delta_{\mathrm{low}}, \qquad
  x_1 = x_0 + \mathrm{HP}\!\left(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}}\right), \qquad
  x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1,
  \label{eq:flow_bridge}
\end{equation}
so the model learns a bridge from low to high rather than a generative map from noise (\texttt{flow\_model.py}). This is what makes the pipeline deterministic in the sense of Sec.~\ref{subsec:generative-paradigms}: \mbox{$x_0$} is fixed by the input rather than drawn, and the Euler integration adds no randomness, so each patch has exactly one corrected counterpart. Because \mbox{$x_0$} already carries the correct large-scale structure, the target velocity \mbox{$u = x_1 - x_0 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}})$} is a pure high-pass field, integrating it can only ever add small-scale content, and the trajectory is nearly straight, so few integration steps are needed: 8 Euler steps are used at inference, the point beyond which raising the step count left the reconstructed shell spectra unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:pipelines}). The training loss is the flow-matching regression of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_matching} on this interpolant,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\;
  \frac{\big\lVert v_\theta(x_t, t, \mathbf{c}) - (x_1 - x_0) \big\rVert^2}{\max\!\big(\mathrm{Var}(x_1{-}x_0)\,/\,\overline{\mathrm{Var}},\ 0.1\big)},
  \label{eq:flow_loss}
\end{equation}
where the denominator is a bounded, batch-relative per-patch variance normalisation: a dense, well-resolved shell's true residual is naturally tiny, so a plain mean-squared-error (MSE) loss gives it far weaker gradients than a sparse shell's large residual, and the network never learns to calibrate the ``correct answer is nearly zero here'' case, visible as a persistent few-percent bias on the densest shells. Here \mbox{$\mathrm{Var}(x_1{-}x_0)$} is the variance of the target residual of the individual patch and \mbox{$\overline{\mathrm{Var}}$} is the mean of that quantity over the patches of the current mini-batch, so the ratio is unity for a typical patch and the normalisation only redistributes weight \emph{within} a batch rather than changing its overall scale. Dividing by it makes every patch contribute comparably in relative terms; the floor of \mbox{$0.1$} caps the resulting upweighting at \mbox{$10\times$}, which removes the imbalance without the gradient blow-ups that an unbounded reweighting was observed to cause (\texttt{train\_flow.py}).

\paragraph{Architecture.} The velocity network \texttt{FlowUNet} instantiates the generic U-Net of Figure~\ref{fig:unet_generic} with four levels and base width 32: channels $1\to32\to64\to128\to256\to512$ at spatial resolutions $256^2 \to 16^2$, mirrored by the decoder with skip concatenations, and a $1\times1$ output head (Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}). Two conditioning paths are threaded through it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flow time $t$, everywhere.} Time is embedded sinusoidally (dimension 128, widened to 512 by a two-layer MLP) and then allowed to reach into every convolution block through FiLM \cite{perez_film_2017}. A block is two \mbox{$3\times3$} convolutions, each followed by a GroupNorm over 8 channel groups -- a normalisation that, unlike batch normalisation, uses only statistics within a single sample and is therefore insensitive to batch size -- and the SiLU activation \mbox{$\mathrm{SiLU}(x) = x\,\sigma(x)$}, with \mbox{$\sigma$} the logistic sigmoid; between them, a linear layer belonging to that block alone turns the shared embedding into one scale and one shift per channel, applied as \mbox{$h \mapsto h\,(1+\mathrm{scale}) + \mathrm{shift}$}. The time \mbox{$t$} therefore enters as a per-channel affine transform rather than as an extra input channel, which is what lets one set of weights represent the whole time-dependent velocity field \mbox{$v_\theta(x, t)$}.
  \item \textbf{Cosmology and redshift, once.} The 8-vector $\mathbf{c}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cosmo_z} passes through a two-layer MLP and is added, broadcast over space, into the $512$-channel bottleneck latent.
\end{itemize}
The output head is likewise zero-initialised, so the untrained model predicts $v = 0$ (``stay at $x_0$''), the correct baseline from which to learn a correction. Training uses Adam (learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$, scaled linearly with the number of data-parallel ranks), cosine-annealed over the epochs, gradient clipping at norm 1, and PyTorch DDP (Distributed Data Parallel) across the four GPUs of a node (\texttt{train\_flow.py}). At inference, \texttt{apply\_flow.py} integrates $\dot x = v_\theta(x, t, \mathbf{c})$ with Euler steps, composes $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(x(1) - x_0)$ through the same filter code used to build the training target, and reconstructs full skies via Figure~\ref{fig:tiling}.

\subsection{Diffusion Pipeline (EDM)}
\label{subsec:pipe-diffusion}

The third pipeline (\texttt{ml/diffusion/}) replaces the low to high bridge with a genuinely generative process in the EDM formulation of \citeauthor{karras_elucidating_2022} (Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}): every sample starts from pure Gaussian noise, and the low map enters only as conditioning. What is diffused is again not the full field but the high-pass residual \mbox{$x_1 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}})$}, for the reasons of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}; the conditioning, by contrast, is the \emph{full} low patch at all scales, channel-concatenated to the noisy input. The denoiser needs the large-scale context to know which small-scale structure to synthesise, even though its output is band-limited.

\paragraph{Preconditioned denoiser.} The network is the two-channel variant of Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}, wrapped in the EDM preconditioning (\texttt{model.EDMPrecond}). Here $x$ (analogous to $x_t$ in flow matching) is the noisy high-pass residual whose clean target is $x_1 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{high}-\delta_{low})$, and $\delta_{low}$ is the low-resolution conditioning map (used at all scales) that steers the denoiser.
\begin{equation}
  D_\theta(x, \sigma, \mathrm{cond}) = c_{\mathrm{skip}}(\sigma)\,x
    + c_{\mathrm{out}}(\sigma)\, F_\theta\!\big(\,[\,c_{\mathrm{in}}(\sigma)\,x \,\oplus\, \delta_\mathrm{low}\,],\ c_{\mathrm{noise}}(\sigma),\ \mathbf{c}\big),
  \label{eq:edm_precond}
\end{equation}
with the standard coefficients
\begin{equation}
  c_{\mathrm{skip}} = \frac{\sigma_d^2}{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{\sigma\,\sigma_d}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{noise}} = \tfrac14 \ln \sigma,
  \label{eq:edm_coeffs}
\end{equation}
\cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Each coefficient has a distinct job, and together they let \emph{one} network operate across five orders of magnitude in noise level (Figure~\ref{fig:edm_sampling}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \mbox{$c_{\mathrm{in}}$} normalises the input: \mbox{$x = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$} has variance \mbox{$\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2$}, so \mbox{$c_{\mathrm{in}}\,x$} has unit variance at \emph{every} noise level. The raw network \mbox{$F_\theta$} never has to adapt its operating range to \mbox{$\sigma$}.
  \item $c_{\mathrm{skip}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}}$ blend an identity path with the network output. At small $\sigma$, $c_{\mathrm{skip}} \to 1$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}} \to 0$: the noisy input is already almost the answer, and the network only supplies a small correction. At large $\sigma$, $c_{\mathrm{skip}} \to 0$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}} \to \sigma_d$: the input is essentially pure noise, and the network must synthesise the answer outright. The denoiser thus interpolates continuously between ``pass through'' and ``generate'', instead of one set of weights having to learn both regimes unaided.
  \item $c_{\mathrm{noise}}$ log-compresses $\sigma \in [0.002, 80]$ into an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ scalar, which is what the sinusoidal embedding of Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}(a) receives, the diffusion counterpart of the flow time $t$.
\end{itemize}
The conditioning channel ($\delta_{low}$) is neither noised nor rescaled by $c_{\mathrm{in}}$. It is not part of the diffusion process, it only steers it. One EDM default is deliberately not adopted: the data scale $\sigma_d$, tuned to $0.5$ for natural images in \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}, is instead measured as the standard deviation of the actual training target -- the high-pass residual, whose variance differs materially from a natural image's -- over a few real batches at start-up (\texttt{estimate\_sigma\_data}); using the wrong $\sigma_d$ would mis-scale all four coefficients of Eq.~\eqref{eq:edm_coeffs} at once.

\paragraph{Training.} Per example, a noise level is drawn as $\ln\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(-1.2, 1.2^2)$, the target is noised to $x = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$, and the denoising error is weighted per noise level and normalised per patch:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, \lambda(\sigma)\,
      \frac{\big\lVert D_\theta(x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon,\ \sigma,\ \delta_{\mathrm{low}}) - x_1 \big\rVert^2}
           {\max\!\big(\mathrm{Var}(x_1)/\overline{\mathrm{Var}},\ 0.1\big)} \right],
  \qquad
  \lambda(\sigma) = \frac{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}{(\sigma\,\sigma_d)^2},
  \label{eq:edm_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda(\sigma)$ is EDM's weighting that equalises gradient magnitude across noise levels \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}, and the bounded per-patch normalisation is the same device, and the same measured motivation, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_loss}. The bound is essential here: an unbounded $1/\mathrm{Var}$ divisor compounds multiplicatively with $\lambda(\sigma)$'s own dynamic range, and the combination was observed to produce loss spikes that prevented convergence entirely. The optimiser setup mirrors the flow pipeline's.

\paragraph{Sampling.} Inference follows EDM's deterministic Algorithm 1 (\texttt{model.sample\_heun}): the Karras noise schedule
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_i = \Big(\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho} + \tfrac{i}{N-1}\big(\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho} - \sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho}\big)\Big)^{\rho},
  \qquad \rho = 7,\ \ \sigma \in [0.002,\ 80],
  \label{eq:karras_schedule}
\end{equation}
integrated with Heun's second-order method over $N = 32$ steps from $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:edm_sampling}). The step count is not a tuning failure but the point of this pipeline: a trajectory from pure noise is genuinely curved (Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}), and with up to two denoiser calls per Heun step, this is the most expensive of the three corrections per shell. The corrected patch is composed exactly as in the flow pipeline, by passing the sampled residual through the same high-pass filter and adding it to the unmodified low-fidelity patch. Further the full skies are reconstructed with the \mbox{$p=32$} blending of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}, which gives each sky pixel to essentially a single tile rather than averaging the independent draws of the tiles covering it.

In summary, the three pipelines answer the same question at three levels of ambition: transfer restores the power spectrum by construction but cannot restore structure; unet-flow transports each patch along a short, nearly straight bridge and restores stochastic small-scale power at moderate cost; diffusion generates the missing band from noise through a full multi-step sampler, at the highest cost. All three inherit the same guarantees from the shared formulation, large scales pinned to \textsc{Disco-DJ}, corrections confined to the scales where the deficit lives, and evaluation on held-out cosmologies. Running them at production scale is the subject of the next section.

\subsection{Local Development}
\label{sec:local_dev}

Both codes were first set up on a local machine to establish a minimal working configuration with an interactive debugging loop: a bug in the shell-builder logic of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder} or the IC hand-off of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} is caught in seconds, rather than after a queued cluster job has run to completion. Tests at $16^3 = 4096$ particles complete in under a minute on a single core; such a run is not physically meaningful, it exercises the full pipeline -- initial conditions, integration, lightcone accumulation -- end to end before committing to a production configuration.

The binding constraint at production size is memory. Storing only the raw phase-space state -- six single-precision components per particle -- for a \textsc{CosmoGridV1}-sized run gives
\begin{equation}
    N_p = 832^3 \approx 5.8\times10^{8}, \qquad
    N_p \times 24\ \text{bytes} \approx 14\ \text{GB},
    \label{eq:phasespace_mem}
\end{equation}
already beyond a typical workstation's RAM once any working headroom is included -- and this floor is exceeded differently by the two codes. \textsc{PkdGrav3} carries, on top of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phasespace_mem}, its per-particle tree, per-particle rung state, and domain-decomposition buffers (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), so its footprint grows with tree depth and rung occupancy. \textsc{Disco-DJ} is instead bounded by its force mesh: the Poisson solve is one global FFT (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), so the whole PM grid -- density, its Fourier transform, and force components -- must be resident simultaneously, and the demand scales with the chosen mesh resolution rather than the particle count. Production runs of either code therefore require a cluster.

\subsection{Euler (ETH Zurich)}

On Euler, each code gets its own virtual environment: \textsc{Disco-DJ} is built on JAX and needs a matched GPU software stack, while \textsc{PkdGrav3} is a compiled tree code with its own toolchain. A per-code split that avoids the version conflicts a shared environment invites. Euler provides the memory and GPU partitions for the full $832^3$ resolution; the GPU acceleration specifically benefits \textsc{Disco-DJ}, whose entire force solve is GPU-native, whereas the \textsc{PkdGrav3} build used here is CPU-bound. Long-running jobs are distributed across multiple nodes where the code supports it.

\subsection{CSCS (Alps)}

The largest runs use the CSCS Alps system, whose four NVIDIA GH200 modules per node each provide 96~GB of high-bandwidth GPU memory. This directly addresses \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s binding constraint: since its memory demand tracks the PM mesh (Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}), a larger memory pool translates into a finer force mesh, and hence better small-scale force resolution, per node. Production runs use \texttt{RES\_PM} $= 1664 = 2\times832$ mesh cells per dimension at the \textsc{CosmoGridV1} box and particle count (Appendix~\ref{app:slurm}). The same nodes also host the correction-pipeline training of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}, where the memory allows large per-GPU batches of $256^2$ patches, and DDP training spans the four GPUs of a node.

\section{Software Engineering}
\subsection{Code and Data Layout}

Executables and virtual environments live in the cluster's backed-up but quota-limited \texttt{HOME}; simulation snapshots, shell maps, per-job logs, and diagnostic plots -- bulk, regenerable output -- go to \texttt{SCRATCH}, which trades persistence for effectively unbounded (periodically purged) capacity. Keeping every submission's full log proved valuable for debugging and cross-run comparison alike: a run's log is often the only remaining record of exactly which configuration produced a given output. The \textsc{CosmoGridV1} reference dataset (\mbox{$\sim110$}~TB) was transferred to CSCS with a managed Globus transfer job, which runs unattended and recovers from dropped connections. An interactive single-stream copy at this scale would be both too slow and too fragile.

\subsection{Job Scheduling and Submission}

Every Python driver is paired with a Slurm batch script: the driver runs unchanged from the local tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev} up to a production allocation, while only the surrounding batch script changes. Jobs are launched interactively (\texttt{bash}) for fast-turnaround debugging or queued (\texttt{sbatch}) for long unattended runs. Each batch script follows the same two-part layout (Appendix~\ref{app:slurm}): a header of \texttt{\#SBATCH} directives declaring \emph{resources} (nodes, GPUs, task layout, exclusivity, wall-clock limit), \emph{accounting} (account, partition), and \emph{bookkeeping} (job name, working directory, \texttt{\%j}-tagged log paths so concurrent runs never overwrite each other), followed by a short execution body that activates the code-specific environment, fixes input paths (e.g.\ the \texttt{CosmoML.tipsy} IC of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and sets the physical parameters as shell variables (\texttt{RES=832}, \texttt{RES\_PM=1664}, \texttt{BOXSIZE=900.0}).

The resource header also fixes the job's cost against the allocation,
\begin{equation}
    C = n_{\mathrm{nodes}} \times t \quad [\text{node-hours}],
    \label{eq:node_hours}
\end{equation}
charged on the wall-clock time the job actually occupies the nodes rather than on the time requested, so a job that exits early is billed only for what it used, while one that sits idle inside a long allocation is billed in full. What this penalises is therefore not an over-generous time limit but wasted occupancy, which is why debugging is done in short single-node interactive sessions and exclusive multi-node allocations are reserved for production runs that keep the nodes busy. One detail keeps the scripts scalable: the header reserves one task per node, and the execution body computes the real degree of parallelism as one process per GPU, \texttt{NTASKS=\$((SLURM\_NNODES * 4))}, passed explicitly to \texttt{srun}. Scaling a job up therefore means editing a single \texttt{--nodes} line; the process-per-GPU launch follows automatically.

\subsection{Version Control}

The whole project, the analysis and ML pipelines together with the modified \textsc{Disco-DJ} containing the shell builder, is kept in a dedicated Git repository. Beyond protection against machine failure, this gives a reliable way back to a known-good state when an experimental change breaks the pipeline, and keeps the many configuration variants organised.

\section{Validating the Simulation Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-validation}

Both lightcones are built in this work and further checked before any correction is trained. Sec.~\ref{subsec:res-ic} follows the linear input through the four fixes that brought \textsc{Disco-DJ} onto \textsc{PkdGrav3}; Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation}
compares the resulting lightcone against the published \textsc{CosmoGridV1} one, shell by shell. Only then does the chapter turn to the corrections themselves.

\subsection{Reconstructing the Linear Input}
\label{subsec:res-ic}

Getting the linear input right took four attempts, and the route to it is a result in its
own right. Figure~\ref{fig:ic_error_budget} shows the steps that brought the large-scale power
of the \textsc{Disco-DJ} runs onto the \textsc{PkdGrav3} reference: four matter power spectra,
each from a simulation of the same cosmology that was actually performed, divided by the
\textsc{PkdGrav3} run started from the same initial conditions. Each stage fixes one ingredient.

One feature of the figure is worth noting before the stages themselves. The offsets are
essentially \emph{flat} across the largest scales the box represents. A wrong ingredient
displaces the whole ratio bodily rather than bending it, which is the signature of a
mis-configured simulation setup driving the wrong overall growth of structure, rather than of
physics acting at any particular scale. The improvement is also not monotonic in the obvious way,
because several of the errors partly cancelled one another.

Stage~(1) is \textsc{Disco-DJ} run with no radiation density in its background at all: early
versions of the code offered no input for it, even though \textsc{PkdGrav3} was being handed its
own radiation abundance. Radiation is negligible today but not at the starting redshift, where it
contributes at the percent level to the expansion rate and hence to the growth factor that
carries the initial conditions forward, and the run sits \mbox{$5.7\%$} high. Supplying it,
stage~(2), with the neutrinos counted as massless and therefore folded into \mbox{$\Omega_r$}, removes
roughly half of the offset and leaves \mbox{$3.1\%$}.

Stage~(3) is the decisive one. \textsc{Disco-DJ} is no longer started from \textsc{concept}'s
linear input, but from the backscaled particle load that \textsc{PkdGrav3} itself produces.
Backscaling carries a \mbox{$z=0$} spectrum to the starting redshift with a single growth factor, and
since the \textsc{Disco-DJ} background has no massive neutrinos in it, that growth factor is not
the one \textsc{CosmoGridV1} evolved with. The remedy is to backscale from a deliberately
larger \mbox{$\sigma_8$}, chosen together with the shifted \mbox{$\Omega_r$} so that a \textsc{Disco-DJ}
run started from those initial conditions arrives at \mbox{$z=0$} with the same large-scale power as the  \textsc{PkdGrav3} reference. Handing both codes the same \emph{theory} is not the same as handing them the same
\emph{initial conditions}; this stage brings the offset to \mbox{$-0.8\%$}.

Stage~(4) closes the last of it, and concerns which field the table describes. The particle load
carries cold dark matter and baryons only, since neutrinos never become particles in either code,
so the transfer function must be the CDM+baryon part \mbox{$\delta_{cb}$} of the \textsc{CosmoGridV1}
linear input rather than the total-matter \mbox{$\delta_m$}. A table built from the total matter field
would imprint the neutrinos' free-streaming suppression on a particle field that contains no
neutrinos, and the \mbox{$\sigma_8$} that \textsc{PkdGrav3} normalises the load to sits in the same file
and must refer to the same field. \texttt{get\_transfer\_function.py} writes out both variants, so
the pairing is a real choice; making it consistently leaves \mbox{$-0.2\%$}.

\subsection{Validation against \textsc{CosmoGridV1}}
\label{sec:shell_validation}

\textsc{PkdGrav3} is run with its native lightcone module on the exact box, resolution, and cosmology used by \textsc{CosmoGridV1}, and the resulting matter angular power spectrum $C_\ell$ is compared shell-by-shell against the corresponding published \textsc{CosmoGridV1} lightcone. Agreement here confirms that the reconstructed transfer function, the derived parameters of Sec.~\ref{subsec:ic_linear}, and the run configuration together reproduce the reference suite; only then is the same IC handed to \textsc{Disco-DJ}, whose shell output additionally validates the shell builder itself (\texttt{pkdgrav/validate\_shell\_builder.py}). Both runs describe the same cosmology
on the same 69-shell radial grid, so their angular power spectra can be compared
shell by shell (Figure~\ref{fig:cl_comparison}) and quantified through the ratio
\begin{equation}
  R(\ell, s) \;=\; \frac{C_\ell^{\textsc{disco}}(s)}{C_\ell^{\textsc{CosmoGrid}}(s)},
  \label{eq:shell_validation_ratio}
\end{equation}
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cl_ratio_summary}, at the native $N_{\mathrm{side}} =
2048$ out to $\ell_{\max} = 3000$ for four shells spanning the depth of the
lightcone. Here \mbox{$s$} indexes the radial shell and \mbox{$\ell$} the multipole, so \mbox{$R(\ell, s)$} is one ratio curve per shell. On the logarithmic scale of Figure~\ref{fig:cl_comparison} the two
simulations are visually indistinguishable over most of the range, itself the
first result, separating only at high \mbox{$\ell$}, and only on the nearer shells. The ratio resolves what that
comparison cannot, and three distinct effects emerge from it.

\paragraph{Lightcone geometry.} Figure~\ref{fig:cl_ratio_summary} validates the shell builder itself. An
error in the geometry -- a mis-solved crossing condition (Eq.~\ref{eq:vx_formula}),
a missing periodic replica (Eq.~\ref{eq:replica_set}), a wrong shell assignment
(Eq.~\ref{eq:shell_filter}), or a HEALPix ordering mismatch -- would misplace or
double-count particles on the sphere and corrupt the spectrum at every
multipole, most conspicuously at the largest scales. No such corruption is present.

\paragraph{The backscaling trade-off.} The second effect is a small residual in the
large-scale amplitude, and it follows directly from how the initial
conditions are built. The linear spectrum is extracted at $a = 1$ and scaled back
to the starting redshift with the linear growth factor
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:ic_handoff}), which anchors the construction at $z = 0$: there
the reconstructed field matches its target by design. That anchoring holds exactly
at one epoch only. Growth is not strictly scale-independent once massive neutrinos
and radiation contribute to the expansion (Sec.~\ref{sec:power_spectrum}), so a
single backscaling cannot hold at every redshift at once, and the agreement drifts
away from the anchor. The measured large-scale plateau follows exactly that
pattern, shifting monotonically with the depth of the shell:

\toprule
shell & $z$ & $R$ at $\ell \lesssim 100$ \\
\midrule
5  & $0.07$ & $1.000$ \\
20 & $0.31$ & $1.000$ \\
40 & $0.81$ & $0.999$ \\
60 & $2.04$ & $0.997$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
$R$ is the median of Eq.~\eqref{eq:shell_validation_ratio} over $20 \le \ell \le 100$, chosen to sit above the few-mode regime and below the resolution deficit. The drift stays sub-percent across the whole lightcone, small compared with the
deficit the corrections must repair, but systematic rather than noise. It is the price of backscaled initial conditions: the closer a shell lies to $z = 0$, the better its overall amplitude is reproduced.

\paragraph{The resolution deficit.} The third effect is the one
Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} exists to remove, and
Figure~\ref{fig:cl_ratio_summary} exposes its defining property: it is a function
of comoving scale, not of multipole. The dashed markers show where one fixed comoving scale of \mbox{$17\,\mathrm{cMpc}/h$} lands on each shell. This scale is not chosen by hand but measured, by reading off the multipole at which each shell's spectrum first departs from the reference and converting it back to a comoving length; Sec.~\ref{sec:res-onset} gives the procedure and the fit in full. The markers track the onset of the departure of the particle-mesh spectrum from the high-fidelity one shell by shell: the nearest
shell departs from unity around $\ell \sim 70$ and falls to $R \approx 0.24$ near
$\ell \approx 2000$; shell~20 departs at $\ell \sim 300$ and reaches $R \approx
0.56$ at $\ell = 3000$; shell~40 departs at $\ell \sim 600$ and reaches only
$R \approx 0.92$; and shell~60 shows no departure at all within $\ell \le 3000$.
The onset is monotonic in \mbox{$\chi$} and consistent with a single fixed comoving scale across all four shells, and the dashed markers make the agreement quantitative: at \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{cMpc}/h$} they fall at \mbox{$\ell = 79$}, \mbox{$326$} and \mbox{$754$} on shells~5, 20 and~40, against measured onsets of \mbox{$\ell \sim 70$}, \mbox{$300$} and \mbox{$600$}. This is what justifies the per-shell correction onset \mbox{$\ell_{\min}(s)$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min}: no single global \mbox{$\ell_{\min}$} describes these curves, whereas one fixed length in \mbox{$\mathrm{cMpc}/h$} describes them all at once.

Shell~60 is the instructive exception. Its marker sits at \mbox{$\ell = 1395$}, comfortably inside the measured range, yet no departure is visible there at all. The onset scale says where a deficit would begin if there were one; it says nothing about how large it is. By \mbox{$z \approx 2$} the field on these scales is still close to linear, so the particle-mesh solver has little non-linear structure to miss and the deficit never rises above the sample-variance scatter. The two statements are consistent: the correction is switched on for such shells and simply finds almost nothing to do, which is exactly what Sec.~\ref{sec:res-transfer} measures. The most distant redshift bin sits at \mbox{$0.998$}--\mbox{$1.004$} both before and after correction.

\subsection{Where the 17 Mpc/h Onset Comes From}
\label{sec:res-onset}

All three corrections switch on at the same place: a per-shell multipole
\mbox{$\ell_{\min}(s)$} obtained by projecting one fixed comoving length \mbox{$L$} onto
each shell through Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min}. That single number is the only free parameter
the three pipelines share, so it is worth showing how it was measured rather than
asserting it. The procedure is the one plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:onset_scale} and
implemented in \texttt{vis/plot\_onset\_scale.py}.

\paragraph{Measuring an onset.} For each shell, the ratio
\mbox{$C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}/C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}$} is formed from the stored harmonic
coefficients at the native \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$} resolution, out to
\mbox{$\ell_{\max}=3000$}, and smoothed with a boxcar. The onset
\mbox{$\ell_{\mathrm{onset}}$} is the first multipole at which the smoothed ratio falls one percent below unity and stays below it.

\paragraph{One length, every shell.} Each onset is converted back to a
comoving length through \mbox{$L = 2\pi\chi(s)/\ell_{\mathrm{onset}}$}. If the deficit is
really set by the particle-mesh force resolution, which is a fixed physical scale, then
this length is the same on every shell, and \mbox{$\ell_{\mathrm{onset}}$} plotted against
\mbox{$\chi$} is a straight line through the origin of slope \mbox{$2\pi/L$}.
Figure~\ref{fig:onset_scale}(b) shows that it is. Fitting that slope over the shells, which span a factor \mbox{$12$} in comoving distance from
\mbox{$\chi = 159$} to \mbox{$1940\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$}, gives
\begin{equation}
  L \;=\; 16.7\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h ,
  \label{eq:onset_L}
\end{equation}
with a per-shell spread of only \mbox{$16.1$} to \mbox{$17.1\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} between
the 16th and 84th percentiles. The production configuration uses
\mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$}, which is this measurement rounded to the nearest
megaparsec.

A limit of the calibration should be stated. It was carried out on a single cosmology and the resulting length is then held fixed across the whole grid, so \mbox{$17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} is a representative value rather than one refitted per cosmology, which should be fixed in a future work.

\section{Simulation Production Run}
\label{sec:res-production}

With the pipeline validated, the full grid was produced in a single \textsc{Slurm} array
job \\(\texttt{disco/run\_disco\_gen\_all\_cscs.sh}): one array task per cosmology, 198
tasks, each generating a complete 69-shell lightcone from the backscaled particle load of
Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}. 
This section reports what that cost, since the cost is the entire motivation for the thesis, and Figure~\ref{fig:production_timing} breaks it down.

\paragraph{What one lightcone runs on.} A single lightcone is \emph{one} simulation
distributed over \mbox{$8$} NVIDIA GH200 GPUs -- two Alps nodes with four GPUs each,
launched as \texttt{srun -{}-ntasks=8} -- at \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s own resolution of
\mbox{$832^3$} particles in a \mbox{$900\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} box, with the force mesh at
\mbox{$N_{\mathrm{pm}} = 1664$}. The eight tasks are not eight independent runs: \textsc{jax}
shards the particle load across all eight devices (\texttt{sharding=\{devices=[8]\}} on
\texttt{jit(run\_nbody)}), so each holds roughly \mbox{$7.2\times10^{7}$} of the
\mbox{$5.8\times10^{8}$} particles and every integration step is executed collectively.
Each task writes its own view of the same step to the log, which is why the timings below
are averaged over the eight tasks rather than summed.

\paragraph{Cost per cosmology.} End to end, the median cosmology takes
\mbox{$35.7$} minutes, and Figure~\ref{fig:production_timing}(b) shows where it goes:
\mbox{$66\,\mathrm{s}$} to convert the \texttt{tipsy} initial conditions to HDF5,
\mbox{$24\,\mathrm{s}$} to load them and compile the \textsc{jax} kernels,
\mbox{$4.5$} minutes to integrate from the starting redshift down to the outermost shell,
\mbox{$7.9$} minutes of on-lightcone integration, \mbox{$7.2$} minutes of shell building, and \mbox{$14.5$} minutes for dispatch, host and Input/Output (I/O). At two nodes that is \mbox{$1.19$} node-hours, or \mbox{$4.8$} GPU-hours, per
cosmology, and \mbox{$236$} node-hours for the grid as a whole. The spread across the 198
runs is small -- \mbox{$33.6$} minutes at the fastest, \mbox{$34.6$} median, with a single
outlier at \mbox{$53.3$} -- so the figure quotes medians rather than means throughout.

The \mbox{$4.5$}-minute pre-lightcone phase is easy to overlook but is not overhead.
The particle load starts at \mbox{$z = 99$} while the outermost shell sits at
\mbox{$z = 3.5$}, so the integrator has to cross that entire range before the first shell can be written, and it does so without accumulating anything
(Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder_core}). It is the price of starting the simulation early enough for the backscaled initial conditions to be valid, and it is charged once per lightcone regardless of how many shells are subsequently written.

\paragraph{The lightcone is not free.} The per-step breakdown in
Figure~\ref{fig:production_timing}(a) is the more interesting result. The \textsc{Disco-DJ}
integration step is flat at \mbox{$7.0\,\mathrm{s}$} across the entire lightcone,
as it must be: the particle-mesh solve performs the same global FFT at every step,
regardless of where the lightcone front happens to be. The shell builder does not behave
that way at all. It costs \mbox{$9.3\,\mathrm{s}$} on the outermost shell and
\mbox{$4.9\,\mathrm{s}$} on the innermost, and the lower panel shows why: the number of
periodic box replicas that survive the radial reject of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_bounds}
falls from \mbox{$572$} on average to \mbox{$1$} as the front comes in. The crossing test is evaluated
once per replica (Sec.~\ref{sec:crossing}), so its cost tracks that count directly.

Integrated over the lightcone the two are comparable -- \mbox{$481\,\mathrm{s}$} of
\mbox{$N$}-body against \mbox{$437\,\mathrm{s}$} of shell building, a ratio of
\mbox{$0.91$}. Building the lightcone is therefore not a by-product of the integration but
a second cost of the same order, which is worth stating plainly because it is easy to
assume otherwise. It also identifies where any future optimisation should go: the replica
loop dominates at high redshift, and the transfer of particle positions to the device
dominates at low redshift, where few replicas remain.

One caveat is built into the figure. Every segment named above comes from the
code's own internal timers, and together they account for \mbox{$58\%$} of the wall-clock. The remaining \mbox{$14.5$} minutes per cosmology -- the pale band in
Figure~\ref{fig:production_timing}(b) -- is not attributable to any one timed kernel: it is the work that falls between them, chiefly asynchronous dispatch that the timers do not capture, host-side bookkeeping between steps, and writing the finished shells to disk. Future work should focus on minimizing it. The production cost quoted above, and used in every cost comparison in this thesis, is therefore the full \mbox{$35.7$} minutes.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:res-protocol}

\paragraph{Held-out cosmologies.} All pipelines share one split convention (\texttt{transfer\_function.split\_val\_cosmos}, \texttt{dataset.split\_by\_cosmo}): of the 198 grid cosmologies with both a \textsc{Disco-DJ} and a \textsc{CosmoGridV1} lightcone available, whole cosmologies are shuffled with a fixed seed and 30 ($15\%$) are withheld from every fit and training run. Figure~\ref{fig:heldout_params} shows where they sit in parameter space and Table~\ref{tab:heldout} lists their parameters. All 30 are used for every diagnostic, including the compute-heavy full-sky and weak-lensing ones, and the same 30 for every pipeline, so all figures of this chapter are directly comparable.

\paragraph{Statistics and resolutions.} Every pipeline is scored with the same shared diagnostics (\texttt{ml/analysis/}): per-shell full-sky angular power spectra pooled into three redshift bins, one-point moments (variance, skewness, excess kurtosis) as a function of shell depth, and weak-lensing convergence maps. The $\kappa$ maps are built with the \textsc{UFalcon} lightcone integration \cite{sgier_fast_2018} (\texttt{ml/analysis/weak\_lensing.py}): all corrected shells within a source bin's redshift range are combined into one convergence map per cosmology at the same $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, $\ell \le 1500$ as the shells they integrate using the DES-Y3 \textsc{metacal} source redshift distributions of tomographic bins 1 ($z \lesssim 1.05$) and 4 ($z \lesssim 1.85$) -- the lowest- and highest-redshift bins, which weight the nearby (hardest) and distant (easiest) shells respectively. 

\paragraph{A common evaluation footing.} The flow and diffusion models work on flat \mbox{$256\times256$} patches cut from shells at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$}; the transfer function works on those same shells in harmonic space, transformed to \mbox{$\ell_{\max}=1500$}. That is simply the band such a map contains -- a HEALPix map at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$} carries no information above \mbox{$\ell \approx 1535$} -- so the two representations cover the same scales. All three corrections then switch on at the same place, the fixed comoving scale \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min}, and so act on the same range of every shell, differing only in what they do there.

\paragraph{Percentile bands.} The shaded bands in the $C_\ell$-ratio figures are \emph{percentile bands across the held-out evaluation set}, not error bars on a mean. For the shell-level grids (e.g.\ Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_cl}), five shells are sampled from each redshift bin for each of the 30 cosmologies, and the ratio \mbox{$C_\ell/C_\ell^{\mathrm{true}}$} is computed per (cosmology, shell) pair, 150 samples per bin. The solid line is their median; the band spans the 16th to 84th percentile, i.e.\ the central 68\% of the actual cosmology-to-cosmology and shell-to-shell scatter. A narrow band therefore means the correction is \emph{uniformly} good across cosmologies and depths, not merely good on average; for the $\kappa$ figures the band is taken across the 30 cosmologies at fixed $\ell$. Figure~\ref{fig:band_construction} shows the construction.

\section{Transfer-Function Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-transfer}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The emulator was trained on the 168 training cosmologies with $\ell_{\max}=1500$, the band limit of the common $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$ footing (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}), spectra smoothed with a 21-point boxcar (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer}), and the per-shell correction onset fixed at $17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ as a hard step, with no transition band; the MLP ($256$--$256$--$128$, Adam, learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch $4096$, $L_2$ decay $10^{-4}$) is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:training}. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_loss} shows its loss: training and validation track each other throughout, separated by a constant \mbox{$\approx 9\%$} that never widens: with only nine features and millions of training rows the model has far too little capacity per sample to overfit. They converge within the first \mbox{$\sim 10^4$} gradient steps to a best validation loss of \mbox{$\tfrac12\mathrm{MSE} = 1.26\times10^{-4}$}, corresponding to an RMS error on the predicted transfer function of \mbox{$\sqrt{2 \times 1.26\times10^{-4}} \approx 1.6\%$} per \mbox{$(\ell, s)$} sample.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{plots/results/transfer/emulator.loss.png}
    \caption{Training and validation loss of the transfer-function emulator (squared error on $T$, without the $L_2$ term; 15-step rolling mean). Both curves plateau within the first $\sim 2.5\times10^4$ steps and stay separated by only $\approx 9\%$ ($1.16\times10^{-4}$ train against $1.26\times10^{-4}$ validation), a gap far too small to indicate memorisation; the best validation loss corresponds to an RMS emulation error of $\approx 1.6\%$ on $T(\ell, s)$.}
    \label{fig:transfer_loss}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Full-sky \mbox{$C_\ell$}.} With that onset, Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_cl} shows the correction working across the lightcone rather than in one bin. In the nearest bin it lifts the ratio from \mbox{$0.953$} to \mbox{$1.003$} at \mbox{$\ell \in [400,900]$}, from \mbox{$0.854$} to \mbox{$1.001$} at \mbox{$[900,1300]$}, and from \mbox{$0.768$} to \mbox{$1.003$} at \mbox{$[1300,1500]$} -- a repair worth up to \mbox{$23$} percentage points, landing within \mbox{$0.3\%$} of the truth. The middle bin, whose uncorrected deficit reaches \mbox{$2.7\%$} at the band edge, is likewise brought to \mbox{$1.003$}. The most distant bin is essentially unaffected by the correction scheme: the corrected and uncorrected curves are indistinguishable below \mbox{$\ell \approx 800$} and drift apart by only \mbox{$0.1\%$} at \mbox{$\ell = 1000$}.

    \label{fig:transfer_cl}

\paragraph{Patches.} The example patches of Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_patches} make the mechanism visible: because the correction rescales amplitudes but keeps \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s phases, the corrected patch shows the same structures as the input, with the missing small-scale graininess restored on top. The per-patch power-ratio panels come with a caveat: they measure a spherical-harmonic-calibrated correction through flat-sky 2D FFTs of individual gnomonic patches, and on several shells this diagnostic shows band-limited overshoots that the full-sky $C_\ell$ of Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_cl} -- the statistic the method is calibrated on, and the one used downstream -- does not exhibit. The discrepancy is a property of the patch diagnostic (projection and pixelisation of a full-sky correction), and its residual cause has not been fully isolated.


\paragraph{Moments.} Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_moments} exposes the pipeline's designed-in limitation. The full-sky variance of the corrected shells lands nearly on the truth, variance is an integral of \mbox{$C_\ell$}, which the method matches by construction. Skewness and excess kurtosis, however, remain at the level of the uncorrected maps, far below the reference on the sparse shells: a deterministic, phase-preserving amplitude rescale cannot manufacture the one-point non-Gaussianity that the missing small-scale structure carries. This is precisely the gap the two patch-based pipelines exist to close.

\paragraph{Weak lensing.} At the convergence-map level (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_kappa}), the uncorrected lightcones lose power steadily with multipole, reaching a \mbox{$43\%$} deficit in bin~1 and \mbox{$20\%$} in bin~4 by \mbox{$\ell = 1500$}. The correction removes essentially all of it. In bin~1 the corrected ratio is \mbox{$1.000$} at \mbox{$\ell = 100$}, \mbox{$0.996$} at \mbox{$\ell = 400$}, \mbox{$1.005$} at \mbox{$\ell = 800$} and \mbox{$1.004$} at \mbox{$\ell = 1200$} -- within half a percent of unity across two decades in multipole, against an uncorrected curve that has fallen to \mbox{$0.623$} by the last of those. Bin~4 behaves the same way at smaller amplitude, holding \mbox{$0.996$}--\mbox{$1.004$} throughout. Percentile bands are a few percent wide. Only in the last few multipoles does the ratio dip, to \mbox{$0.980$} (bin~1) and \mbox{$0.991$} (bin~4) at \mbox{$\ell = 1500$}, where the boxcar smoothing of the fitted \mbox{$T(\ell)$} runs into the edge of the band.

The convergence maps also carry one-point information, and Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_kappa_moments} shows it cosmology by cosmology. The same pattern as for the shells (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_moments}) reappears at the \mbox{$\kappa$} level, which is the level a map-based analysis actually consumes: the corrected variance sits on the truth, at \mbox{$0.967 \pm 0.006$} of the reference in bin~1, while skewness and excess kurtosis close only part of their gap, reaching \mbox{$0.793 \pm 0.027$} and \mbox{$0.638 \pm 0.058$} against \mbox{$0.733$} and \mbox{$0.537$} uncorrected. Variance is an integral of \mbox{$C_\ell$} and is therefore repaired by construction; the higher moments are not functions of the power spectrum at all, and a rescaling that leaves every phase untouched cannot manufacture them. This is the quantitative statement of the pipeline's designed-in limitation, and the gap the two patch-based models are built to close.

\section{U-Net Flow-Matching Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-flow}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The flow was trained for 200 epochs on the $10^5$-patch dataset (Table~\ref{tab:training}), distributed over 16 DDP ranks (4 Alps nodes $\times$ 4 GH200), with the base learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$ scaled linearly to $4.8\times10^{-4}$ and cosine-annealed; the high-pass residual used the same per-shell onset \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} as the harmonic pipeline, so the model is asked to correct exactly the band that is actually deficient on each shell. Figure~\ref{fig:flow_loss} shows the variance-normalised flow-matching loss of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_loss}: validation improves until epoch \mbox{$\approx 109$} (best \mbox{$0.145$} at epoch 109 -- the checkpoint used for everything below) and then drifts slowly upward while the training loss continues to fall, i.e.\ mild but genuine overfitting past that point.

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Full-sky $C_\ell$.} After tiling the corrected patches back onto the sphere (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}), the shell spectra of Figure~\ref{fig:flow_cl} track the truth to within a few parts in a thousand across most of the corrected range: in the nearest bin the ratio is \mbox{$1.007$} over \mbox{$\ell \in [400,900]$} and \mbox{$1.000$} over \mbox{$[900,1300]$}, against an uncorrected deficit of \mbox{$0.953$} and \mbox{$0.854$}, falling away only in the last multipoles (\mbox{$0.970$} over \mbox{$[1300,1500]$}) as the \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$} band limit is approached. \paragraph{Weak lensing.} The convergence spectra (Figure~\ref{fig:flow_kappa}) sit on the truth through the low and intermediate multipoles -- \mbox{$1.004$} at \mbox{$\ell = 200$}, \mbox{$0.994$} at \mbox{$400$}, \mbox{$0.991$} at \mbox{$600$} in bin~1 -- and then fall away gradually, to \mbox{$0.963$} at \mbox{$\ell = 1000$} and \mbox{$0.898$} at \mbox{$\ell = 1500$}; bin~4 holds unity to \mbox{$\ell \approx 1000$} and reaches \mbox{$0.961$} at the band edge. The decline is towards the top of the reconstruction's own band, where the tiled \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$} patches have least power to work with, and it is smooth rather than abrupt -- unlike the harmonic pipeline, the flow has no band edge to fall off, it simply runs out of resolution. The \mbox{$\kappa$} moments (Figure~\ref{fig:flow_kappa_moments}) are the pipeline's strongest single result, and the clearest demonstration of what a distribution-matching correction buys. Averaged over the 30 held-out cosmologies, the corrected-to-reference ratios in bin~1 are \mbox{$0.973 \pm 0.008$} (variance), \mbox{$0.989 \pm 0.011$} (skewness) and \mbox{$0.979 \pm 0.037$} (excess kurtosis), against \mbox{$0.803 \pm 0.018$}, \mbox{$0.733 \pm 0.020$} and \mbox{$0.537 \pm 0.042$} uncorrected; in bin~4 they reach \mbox{$0.992/0.992/0.983$}. The flow therefore recovers essentially the whole one-point non-Gaussian signal -- the excess kurtosis, which the uncorrected lightcone gets barely half right, is restored to within \mbox{$2\%$} -- and does so with the tightest cosmology-to-cosmology scatter of the three pipelines.

\section{Diffusion Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-diffusion}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The diffusion model shares the flow's data, architecture width, schedule length, and high-pass formulation (Table~\ref{tab:training}); it differs in the objective, in the unscaled learning rate of \mbox{$3\times10^{-5}$}, and in the 32-step Heun sampler with \mbox{$p=32$} blending at inference. Its loss curve (Figure~\ref{fig:diff_loss}) looks qualitatively different from the flow's: the EDM-weighted denoising loss collapses from $\approx 5.9$ to $\approx 0.6$ within the first $\sim 20$ epochs and is then almost flat, with training and validation coinciding to the end (best validation \mbox{$0.512$} at epoch 165) -- no overfitting is observed. This is characteristic of the objective: every example is presented at a freshly drawn noise level and noise realisation, an implicit augmentation that makes memorisation far harder than for the flow's fixed low to high pairs.

\paragraph{Full-sky $C_\ell$.} The reconstructed shell spectra (Figure~\ref{fig:diff_cl}) match the flow's picture closely over most of the corrected range. In the nearest bin the two agree exactly where the correction opens -- both reach \mbox{$1.007$} over \mbox{$\ell \in [400,900]$}, against an uncorrected \mbox{$0.953$} -- and separate progressively thereafter, the diffusion falling to \mbox{$0.987$} over \mbox{$[900,1300]$} and \mbox{$0.948$} over \mbox{$[1300,1500]$} where the flow holds \mbox{$1.000$} and \mbox{$0.970$}. In the middle bin the two are indistinguishable, both landing within three parts in a thousand of unity across the whole band. The summary is that the two patch-based pipelines reproduce the shell spectra equally well wherever the deficit is moderate, and the flow is the better of the two on the nearest shells, where the correction has the most work to do. That a 32-step stochastic sampler, stitched from thousands of independently sampled tiles, reproduces full-sky spectra at this level is the central validation of the tiling scheme of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:res-synthesis}

Figure~\ref{fig:method_comparison} reduces all three pipelines to a single view: for each held-out cosmology the ratio of a $\kappa$-map moment to its reference value, averaged over the 30 cosmologies, with the cosmology-to-cosmology scatter as an errorbar. Because every method is scored at the same $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, $\ell_{\max}=1500$ footing, the vertical ordering is a statement about the corrections themselves rather than about the resolutions they were given.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/results/comparison/method_comparison_kappa_moments_bin1.png}
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{plots/results/comparison/method_comparison_kappa_moments_bin4.png}
    \caption{All three corrections on one axis (\texttt{vis/plot\_method\_comparison.py}), for DES-Y3 \textsc{metacal} source bin~1 (top) and bin~4 (bottom). For each of the 30 held-out cosmologies the $\kappa$-map variance, skewness and excess kurtosis are divided by their \textsc{CosmoGridV1} reference value; the marker is the mean of that ratio over cosmologies and the errorbar its standard deviation, so a perfect correction sits on the dashed line at unity with a short bar. The errorbar measures cosmology-to-cosmology \emph{consistency}, not uncertainty on the mean: a method may sit on unity with a wide bar (right on average, unreliable per cosmology) or slightly below it with a tight bar (biased but predictable). The uncorrected \textsc{Disco-DJ} lightcone is shown on the same axis. The distance from it to unity is the gap each method is trying to close. All three are evaluated at the common $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, $\ell_{\max}=1500$ footing, which handicaps the transfer function relative to its native-resolution capability (see the common-footing discussion in Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}).}
    \label{fig:method_comparison}
\end{figure}

Three consistent messages emerge. First, every pipeline repairs the two-point statistics on the scales it acts on, and at a small fraction of the \mbox{$N$}-body cost: the uncorrected bin~1 variance ratio of \mbox{$0.803 \pm 0.018$} is lifted to \mbox{$0.973 \pm 0.008$} by the flow, \mbox{$0.967 \pm 0.006$} by the transfer function and \mbox{$0.948 \pm 0.010$} by the diffusion model. The three are within three percentage points of one another, which is the substantive point: on a two-point statistic the cheapest correction is competitive with the most expensive, and the transfer function achieves its result at essentially zero inference cost and with the tightest scatter.

Second, the higher moments separate the harmonic rescaling from the two patch-based models cleanly, and the separation is structural rather than a matter of tuning. A pure amplitude rescale preserves phases, so it cannot manufacture the phase correlations that skew a distribution; the transfer function accordingly moves skewness only from \mbox{$0.733 \pm 0.020$} to \mbox{$0.793 \pm 0.027$} and excess kurtosis from \mbox{$0.537 \pm 0.042$} to \mbox{$0.638 \pm 0.058$}. The two patch-based models go far beyond it: the flow reaches \mbox{$0.989 \pm 0.011$} in skewness and \mbox{$0.979 \pm 0.037$} in excess kurtosis, the diffusion \mbox{$0.896 \pm 0.013$} and \mbox{$0.772 \pm 0.036$}.

Third, the flow is the strongest correction overall. It leads on every one of the three moments in both source bins, and it does so with the whole non-Gaussian signal essentially recovered rather than merely improved -- an excess kurtosis within \mbox{$2\%$} of the reference where the uncorrected lightcone retains barely half of it. The residual across all methods remains one-sided -- every correction undershoots, none overshoots -- so what is left is a small systematic deficit rather than added noise.

The same picture holds in bin~4 with uniformly smaller gaps (flow \mbox{$0.992/0.992/0.983$}, diffusion \mbox{$0.986/0.963\\/0.906$}, transfer \mbox{$0.979/0.892/0.784$}), as expected: the distant shells are the ones \textsc{Disco-DJ} already gets nearly right, so less has to be repaired. Taken together the results suggest a division of labour rather than an outright winner. Where an analysis consumes two-point statistics, the transfer function delivers them to within a few percent for free, and is the pragmatic choice. Where it consumes the field -- which is the entire premise of map-level inference -- only a model trained to match the distribution supplies what is missing, and on this evidence the deterministic flow supplies almost all of it. It is worth noting that the better of the two is not the generative one: the diffusion model is the only pipeline here that genuinely samples, and it is outperformed on every moment by a flow that draws nothing at all. On this problem the decisive ingredient is the distribution-matching objective together with a starting point that already carries the correct large scales, not stochasticity.


\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis addressed a practical question: can a fast particle-mesh simulation be corrected retrospectively so that it yields the same results as a computationally expensive N-body simulation, at a fraction of the cost? The idea is to pair \textsc{Disco-DJ}, which runs in minutes, with a learned correction that restores the small-scale structure its mesh cannot resolve. Stage-III-like weak-lensing convergence maps serve as the testbed observable, since they are what map-level analyses actually consume. The answer is yes, with one qualification: which correction suffices depends on which statistic has to be right.

Reaching that answer took three pieces of infrastructure and three models. The first piece is the initial conditions (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}). \textsc{CosmoGridV1} was run with \textsc{PkdGrav3}, which carries massive neutrinos as their own linear species; \textsc{Disco-DJ} cannot. The neutrinos are therefore declared massless, and the resulting shifts in the radiation density and in the growth normalisation are absorbed by adjusting the transfer function and the background parameters. A \textsc{Disco-DJ} run started from those initial conditions then matches \textsc{CosmoGridV1} on large scales with an accuracy of less than a percent, across the cosmologies and redshifts used here (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-validation}). The small-scale deficit that remains is expected, and is exactly what the rest of the thesis repairs.

The second piece is the shell builder (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}). It accumulates the lightcone inside \textsc{Disco-DJ}'s time integration reproducing \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition on \textsc{CosmoGridV1}'s radial shell grid. Compared against matched \textsc{CosmoGridV1} lightcones it agrees on large scales with no coherent offset, which is what validates the geometry (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation}).

The third piece is the production run (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-production}): 198 lightcones, one per grid cosmology and each paired to its \textsc{CosmoGridV1} counterpart, giving 13\,662 shell maps out to \mbox{$z = 3.5$}. One lightcone costs \mbox{$35.7$} minutes on eight GH200 GPUs.

On these pairs three corrections were trained and compared on cosmologies none of them saw (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}). They share one guarantee: each acts only above a per-shell cutoff, so the large scales, which \textsc{Disco-DJ} already gets right, are left untouched.

All three repair the angular power spectrum. At the convergence level the variance ratio rises from \mbox{$0.803$} uncorrected to \mbox{$0.973$} for the flow, \mbox{$0.967$} for the transfer function and \mbox{$0.948$} for the diffusion model. Beyond two-point statistics they separate. A per-multipole rescaling preserves phases and therefore cannot manufacture one-point non-Gaussianity: the transfer function lifts the convergence excess kurtosis only from \mbox{$0.537$} to \mbox{$0.638$}. The two patch-based models do far better, the flow reaching \mbox{$0.979 \pm 0.037$} and the diffusion \mbox{$0.772$}. The flow is the most robust of the three. It leads on every moment in both source bins, with the tightest cosmology-to-cosmology scatter, and it costs a single deterministic ODE integration rather than a 32-step sampler.

Several directions follow. The most direct is resolution. All three pipelines were scored on the same footing, maps at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}} = 512$} and multipoles up to \mbox{$\ell_{\max} = 1500$}, chosen so that the comparison measures the corrections rather than the resolutions they were given. Retraining the patch models at \mbox{$N_{\mathrm{side}} = 2048$}, which the patch machinery already supports, would push that limit towards \mbox{$\ell \sim 6000$} and let fast simulations mimic full-gravity ones over the whole range a Stage-IV analysis uses.

The corrections should also be tested against more of the statistics a map-level analysis consumes. Peak counts and Minkowski functionals are the obvious next ones, since the convergence moments used here probe only part of the non-Gaussian information. On the modelling side, architectures that act natively on the sphere are the natural benchmark: \textsc{DeepSphere} \cite{perraudin_deepsphere_2019, defferrard_deepsphere_2020}, or transformers on the \textsc{HEALPix} grid. Either would remove the patch-and-stitch step altogether and allow training and inference on the full map. Two gaps also remain from this work. Each cosmology entered with a single realisation, so realisation-to-realisation generalisation is still to be quantified; and baryonic feedback, which acts on precisely the scales corrected here, would need its own treatment \cite{schneider_matter_2015, fluri_full_2022}.

The prospect that motivates all of it is inference. A corrected fast lightcone costs minutes rather than node-hours, so the mock suites that deep-learning analyses consume \cite{thomsen_dark_2026, fluri_full_2022} could be produced at a small fraction of the reference cost, with full \mbox{$N$}-body runs reserved for validation. \textsc{Disco-DJ} is differentiable end to end \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}, so a differentiable corrected forward model -- from cosmological parameters through the corrected lightcone to the map-level statistic -- would put gradient-based inference within reach of the same pipeline. Running a full mock cosmological analysis on this setup is the natural next test, and on the present evidence a promising route to fast and robust simulation-based inference.

\section{Code Availability}
\label{app:code-availability}

All code written for this thesis is public, in a single repository. It contains the
three stages of the pipeline described in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} -- initial-condition
generation, the shell builder, and the three correction pipelines -- together with the
shared analysis library, every plotting script, and the \textsc{Slurm} batch scripts that
reproduce the production runs.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cl@{}}
\raisebox{-0.32\height}{\includegraphics[height=4.2ex]{plots/github_mark.pdf}} &
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.82\textwidth}
\textbf{\href{https://github.com/Daveofs/masterProject}{github.com/Daveofs/masterProject}}\\[0.6mm]
{\small Complete project: IC generation (\texttt{pkdgrav/}), the \textsc{Disco-DJ} shell
builder (\texttt{shell\_builder/}), the three correction pipelines
(\texttt{ml/transfer/}, \texttt{ml/unet/}, \texttt{ml/diffusion/}), the shared evaluation
library (\texttt{ml/analysis/}), and all figure scripts (\texttt{vis/}).}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The repository's \texttt{README} gives the directory map and the order in which the
stages are run. Two conventions are worth noting for anyone reading the code. The three
correction pipelines are deliberately decoupled: they never import from one another, and
small shared helpers are duplicated rather than factored out, so that changing one
pipeline cannot silently alter the others; the single sanctioned shared module is
\texttt{ml/analysis/}, which is precisely what makes the three comparable
figure-by-figure. And every Python driver is paired with a batch script, so the same
entry point runs unchanged from the local tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev} to a
production allocation (Sec.~\ref{sec:infrastructure}).

The shell builder is not distributed as a separate package: it is implemented inside
\textsc{Disco-DJ}'s time integration and versioned with the rest of the project, in
\texttt{shell\_builder/}. The \textsc{Disco-DJ} base it extends
\cite{hahn_disco-dj_2024, list_disco-dj_2026} is a private, project-internal copy and is not
published alongside this thesis; \texttt{masterProject} is the only repository made public here.

Simulation outputs are not in the repository. The \textsc{CosmoGridV1} reference suite is
public and documented by \citeauthor{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}
\cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}; the \mbox{$\sim110\,\mathrm{TB}$} working copy, the
generated lightcones, and the trained model checkpoints live on CSCS scratch storage
(Sec.~\ref{sec:infrastructure}) rather than under version control, since they are bulk
and regenerable from the code and the published reference.

\section{Figure Provenance}
\label{app:figure-provenance}

Every figure in this thesis was produced by code in this repository, from the data the
surrounding text describes. There are no reproduced or redrawn figures from other
publications, with two explicitly marked exceptions: the multi-stepping diagram of
Figure~\ref{fig:pkd_rungs} is a schematic redraw after
\citeauthor{potter_pkdgrav3_2016} \cite{potter_pkdgrav3_2016}, and the generic U-Net
topology of Figure~\ref{fig:unet_generic} follows
\citeauthor{ronneberger_u-net_2015} \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}; both are drawn from
scratch in Ti\emph{k}Z here rather than copied. The remaining diagrams are original
Ti\emph{k}Z. All data figures are generated by the scripts in \texttt{vis/} and
\texttt{ml/analysis/}, each named in the caption of the figure it produces, so any plot
can be traced to the script and the run that made it.

\section{Use of AI Tools}
\label{app:ai-tools}

AI-based writing assistants were used during the preparation of this thesis for language
work on the manuscript: improving clarity and readability, correcting grammar and
spelling, and suggesting rephrasings of text already drafted by the author. They were
also used as a coding aid, in the way an autocomplete or a documentation search is used,
for routine implementation and debugging.

The research questions, the design of
the pipeline and of the three corrections, the choice and execution of every experiment,
the analysis of the results, and the conclusions drawn from them were carried out by the
author and the supervisors throughout. Every quantitative statement in this thesis was produced by the code in
Appendix~\ref{app:code-availability} and verified against its output; no numerical result,
measurement, or citation was taken from an AI tool. All references were checked against
their primary sources.
