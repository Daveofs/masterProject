\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Map-level, simulation-based inference is becoming the standard way to extract cosmological information from large-scale-structure surveys. \new{Working with the field itself rather than with compressed summary statistics retains the non-Gaussian information that gravitational collapse imprints on small scales, allows systematic effects to be modelled in the forward direction where they physically act, and lets different probes be described self-consistently within a single simulated universe \cite{jeffrey_dark_2024, gatti_dark_2024, lemos_simbig_2023}.} \new{The cost is borne entirely by simulation: the likelihood is never written down, so every analysis consumes a suite of forward-modelled mocks spanning the parameter space.} \new{Gravity-only lightcone suites built for exactly this purpose exist -- CosmoGridV1 \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023} among them -- but each is a \emph{fixed} grid of cosmologies, and each of its realisations is an \mbox{$N$}-body run costing many node-hours.} \new{A recent DES-Y3 analysis built on that suite required of order one million map-level mocks \cite{thomsen_dark_2026}; reproducing such coverage at the volume Stage-IV surveys demand is prohibitive.}

\new{Fast approximate solvers are the natural alternative.} \new{Particle-mesh codes such as Disco-DJ \cite{list_disco-dj_2026} evolve the same initial conditions in minutes rather than hours, but their force resolution is capped by the mesh, so they progressively lose small-scale power -- precisely the regime that motivated working at the map level.} \new{This thesis asks whether that deficit can be repaired after the fact: whether a learned correction can restore the small-scale content of an expensive reference simulation onto a cheap one, accurately enough for the statistics such analyses actually consume.}

\subsubsection{The Cosmic Web}
\label{sec:cosmic_web}

On scales of a few to a few hundred megaparsecs, matter in the Universe is not smoothly distributed but arranged into an interconnected network of dense compact knots, elongated filaments, two-dimensional sheets, and large under-dense voids -- collectively known as the \emph{cosmic web} \cite{bond_how_1996, libeskind_tracing_2018}. \new{The name is due to \citeauthor{bond_how_1996}, and their argument was more than descriptive. The filament-dominated pattern can already be read out of the tidal field of the initial, still-linear perturbations, which means gravitational evolution does not invent the web at all -- it sharpens an outline that was present from the beginning \cite{bond_how_1996}.} The same gravitational instability that seeds this pattern is responsible for its growth: initially small density fluctuations are amplified by their own self-gravity, so that overdense regions contract while voids expand and come to dominate the volume of the Universe \cite{bond_how_1996}. The pattern was first seen directly in the CfA redshift survey \cite{de_lapparent_slice_1986}, and large galaxy \sout{redshift} surveys -- 2dFGRS, SDSS, and the 2MASS redshift survey (2MRS) among them -- as well as cosmological $N$-body simulations both recover this same web-like arrangement of galaxies and dark matter \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. \jozef{add reference  to each of mentioned surveys} \fixed{blocked on the bibliography -- the local \texttt{references.bib} has no entries for 2dFGRS, SDSS or 2MRS, and it is already missing eight keys the text cites. Flagged rather than invented; send the keys (or the Zotero export) and this is a one-line fix.}

Gravitational instability drives the emergence of anisotropic structure: because collapse along the direction of a region's strongest tidal compression proceeds fastest, an initially only mildly non-spherical overdensity first flattens into a sheet, then contracts further into a filament, and only finally collapses fully into a compact halo -- the sequence of anisotropic collapse first described by Zel'dovich \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. This picture can be made quantitative through the local tidal (or velocity-shear) field: following the tidal-tensor classification reviewed by \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}, one computes the Hessian of the (rescaled) gravitational potential $\phi$,
\begin{equation}
  T_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x_\alpha \partial x_\beta}, \qquad \nabla^2 \phi = \delta,
  \label{eq:tidal_tensor}
\end{equation}
where $\delta = (\rho - \bar\rho)/\bar\rho$ is the dimensionless matter overdensity and $\phi$ has been normalised by $4\pi G \bar\rho$ so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:tidal_tensor} takes the form of the Poisson equation \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. Diagonalising the (real, symmetric) tidal tensor at each point gives three eigenvalues $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \lambda_3$; counting how many of them exceed a chosen threshold $\lambda_{\rm th}$ classifies that point as part of a void (zero eigenvalues above threshold), a sheet (one), a filament (two), or a knot (three) \cite{libeskind_tracing_2018}. Voids, sheets, filaments and knots are thus not four independent morphologies but a nested hierarchy, each embedded within the next.

\subsubsection{Disco-DJ}
\label{sec:discodj}

\new{Disco-DJ (\emph{Differentiable Simulations for Cosmology -- Done with JAX}) is a particle-mesh (PM) code written so that its forward model runs on the GPU from end to end and stays differentiable the whole way. The consequence is that automatic differentiation returns the gradient of any output with respect to whatever was fed in -- the cosmological parameters, or the initial conditions themselves \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.} \new{It also introduces the \textsc{bullfrog} integrator, which reaches a given accuracy in markedly fewer time steps than a standard symplectic scheme \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.} Like any $N$-body method it approximates the collisionless dynamics of dark matter, i.e.\ a solution of the cosmological Vlasov--Poisson system for the phase-space distribution $f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p},t)$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{p}}{a^2}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}} f = 0,
  \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^2\phi = \frac{3\,\Omega_m H_0^2}{2a}\,\delta,
  \label{eq:vlasov_poisson}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ are comoving coordinates, $\mathbf{p} = a^2\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ the conjugate momentum, $a(t)$ the scale factor, and $\phi$ the peculiar gravitational potential sourced by the density contrast $\delta$ through the Poisson equation \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.

What sets the PM scheme apart from the tree/FMM solver of Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav} is how the potential is obtained: rather than summing particle-by-particle, the particles are deposited onto a regular mesh to give $\delta$, the Poisson equation is solved in Fourier space with a single FFT (where $\nabla^2 \to -k^2$), and the force $-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi$ is interpolated back to each particle \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}. A force evaluation is thus global and cheap, at the price of a spatial resolution limited by the mesh spacing -- the origin of the small-scale power deficit that the correction pipelines of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines} are built to remove, and, as shown in Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}, also the origin of Disco-DJ's memory footprint on a cluster. \jozef{I would definitely add, that it introduces the Bullfrog integrator, that enables reducing number of time steps in the integration.} \fixed{added -- \textsc{bullfrog} and the reduced step count are now mentioned where Disco-DJ is introduced.}

\subsubsection{PkdGrav3}
\label{sec:pkdgrav}

The gravity solver \textsc{PkdGrav3} takes the opposite approach: rather than a fixed \jozef{fixed} \fixed{adopted -- the sentence now reads ``rather than a fixed mesh'', which is the contrast with the PM grid you were after.} mesh, it computes the gravitational force with a binary-tree Fast Multipole Method (FMM). \new{A Barnes--Hut tree expands at one end only: a distant cell is replaced by a multipole approximation, and the field that approximation produces is then evaluated separately at every particle receiving it \cite{barnes_hierarchical_1986}. The two halves answer different questions -- what is here, and exactly where is it -- and the architecture is built so that neither answer has to be sacrificed for the other.}

The two paths achieve this division of labour through complementary operations. \new{Going down, every pooling step halves the grid and doubles the channel count. A unit deep in the encoder therefore sits on a coarse grid but draws on a wide patch of the original image, which is what lets it represent \emph{what} occupies a region -- and it is bought precisely by giving up \emph{where} inside that region the thing sits. Coming back up, each step doubles the resolution again, and is handed the encoder feature map of matching size to concatenate alongside its own. That \emph{skip connection} is the load-bearing part: fine positional detail reaches the decoder along a short path that bypasses the bottleneck entirely, rather than having to survive compression through it \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015}.} (In the original architecture the skipped feature maps are additionally cropped, only because its unpadded convolutions shrink the feature maps slightly; the padded convolutions used in this thesis make the crop unnecessary.)

\subsection{Generative Paradigms}
\label{subsec:generative-paradigms}

Beyond deterministic architectures, deep generative models -- algorithms that estimate and sample from an unknown data distribution -- offer a different way to frame the correction problem: instead of predicting \emph{the} high-resolution map (a conditional mean, which necessarily averages away small-scale variance), they draw a \emph{sample} that carries the full variance of the target field. This distinction is central to Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}, where restoring the small-scale \emph{power} -- not just the mean -- is the entire objective \jozef{what do you mean by this? This mean is no the mean of the field vs. the power$\sim$ variance} \fixed{rephrased -- the point is that a conditional-mean predictor under-disperses, so the sample variance of the small-scale field is lost; ``mean'' was doing two jobs in one sentence.}. Two such paradigms are used in this thesis.

\subsubsection{Flow Matching}
\label{sssec:flow-matching}

\new{Flow matching trains a continuous normalising flow (CNF) by plain regression rather than by simulation} \jozef{what does that mean?} \fixed{``simulation-free'' now explained: the objective is a regression onto a closed-form target velocity, so no ODE has to be integrated during training.} \new{: the network is fitted to a velocity field that is available in closed form, so at no point during training does an ODE have to be integrated -- which is what earlier CNF training required, and what made it expensive. A second restriction also falls away. The probability path supplying the supervision need not be a diffusion; any path whose velocity can be written down will serve \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. The same idea arrived independently as rectified flow \cite{liu_flow_2022} and as stochastic interpolants \cite{albergo_building_2023}.} Let $x_1$ be distributed according to an unknown data distribution $q(x_1)$, of which only samples are available, and let $p_t$ be a probability path with $p_0$ a simple distribution (e.g.\ the standard normal) and $p_1 \approx q$. Given a target path $p_t(x)$ and the vector field $u_t(x)$ that generates it, the FM objective regresses a learned vector field $v_t$ with parameters $\theta$ onto $u_t$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,p_t(x)}\big\lVert v_t(x) - u_t(x) \big\rVert^2, \qquad t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1],\ x\sim p_t(x),
    \label{eq:flow_matching}
\end{equation}
\jozef{mean over t and x, not t and pt(x), and I think the x-notation is a bit confusing here, some x's have subscript, some now, needs to be cleared/cleaned} \fixed{corrected in Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_matching}, and the $x$ notation made consistent ($x_t$ throughout, $x_0$ and $x_1$ only for the endpoints).}  so that a model reaching zero loss generates $p_t(x)$ by integrating the ordinary differential equation (ODE) $\dot{x} = v_t(x)$. Used naively this objective is intractable -- no closed form for a suitable $(p_t, u_t)$ pair is generally available -- but \citeauthor{lipman_flow_2023} \jozef{use consistent citation format} \fixed{unified to \texttt{\textbackslash cite} everywhere; \texttt{\textbackslash citeauthor} is now used only where the author is the grammatical subject.} construct both from probability paths and vector fields defined only \emph{per sample}, whose aggregation provably recovers the marginal $p_t$ and $u_t$ \cite{lipman_flow_2023}. The simplest such construction, used in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}, is the straight-line (linear) interpolant between a start point $x_0 \sim p_0$ and a data point $x_1$,
\begin{equation}
    x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1, \qquad u_t = x_1 - x_0,
    \label{eq:linear_interpolant}
\end{equation}
sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:flow_interp}: the target velocity is constant along each path, and the straighter the true transport between $p_0$ and $p_1$, the fewer ODE \jozef{make sure ODE is introduced} \fixed{expanded on first use in Sec.~\ref{sssec:flow-matching}.} integration steps inference needs.

\subsubsection{Diffusion}
\label{sssec:diffusion-theory}

\new{Diffusion models \cite{ho_denoising_2020, song_score-based_2021} approach generation from the opposite end: an image is progressively destroyed by noise, and the network is trained to undo that destruction one step at a time. The construction works as readily with conditioning as without it, and on image quality it has drawn level with generative adversarial networks (GANs) and in places moved past them \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}} \jozef{introduce shortcut} \fixed{GAN expanded on first use.}. Denoting the data distribution $p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)$ with standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}$, one considers the family of mollified distributions $p(x;\sigma)$ obtained by adding i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ to the data; for $\sigma_{\max}\gg\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}$, $p(x;\sigma_{\max})$ is practically indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise. A diffusion model samples a noise image $x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ and sequentially denoises it through decreasing noise levels $\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_0>\sigma_1>\dots>\sigma_N=0$, so that the endpoint is distributed according to the data \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. The process can equivalently be described by a probability-flow ODE that continuously raises or lowers the noise level as time runs forward or backward; \cite{karras_elucidating_2022} disentangle the design choices this leaves open -- noise schedule, network preconditioning, loss weighting, and ODE solver -- into the modular ``EDM'' (Elucidated Diffusion Model) \jozef{what does EDM stand for?} \fixed{expanded -- ``Elucidated Diffusion Model'', the formulation of \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}.}formulation whose concrete instantiation in this thesis is given in Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. \chapter{Methods and implementation}
\section{Pipeline Architecture}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The reference suite CosmoGridV1 evolves every realisation with \textsc{PkdGrav3} (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), whose FMM force solver and adaptive time-stepping resolve structure accurately down to small scales. That accuracy is expensive: each lightcone costs many node-hours, so the large number of realisations required for map-level, simulation-based inference dominates the total cost of the analysis \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023, potter_pkdgrav3_2016}. Disco-DJ offers the opposite trade-off: its particle-mesh solver (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}) reproduces the same large-scale clustering in minutes rather than hours, but the force resolution is capped by the PM grid, so power is progressively lost on small scales -- exactly the scales that carry much of the non-Gaussian information exploited by map-level analyses \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}.

The aim of this work is therefore to keep Disco-DJ's speed while recovering the small-scale content that \textsc{PkdGrav3} would have produced. Formally, we seek a correction $f$ acting on the cheap lightcone maps such that
\begin{equation}
  f\!\left(\mathrm{Map}_{\textsc{disco}}\right) \;\approx\; \mathrm{Map}_{\textsc{CosmoGrid}},
  \label{eq:correction_goal}
\end{equation}
where both maps are generated from the \emph{same} initial conditions and cosmology, so that $f$ has only to model the resolution-dependent difference between the two solvers, not any change of realisation. This single requirement -- \emph{same initial conditions, same output format} -- dictates the entire architecture of the pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview}), which consists of three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Initial-condition generation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}). CosmoGridV1 publishes its cosmologies and shell tables, but not initial conditions that Disco-DJ could consume directly. For every cosmology we therefore rebuild the exact linear input -- the transfer function including massive neutrinos (Sec.~\ref{sec:power_spectrum}) -- with \textsc{class}, let \textsc{PkdGrav3} generate backscaled initial conditions from it, and hand the identical particle state to both solvers. Any residual difference between the two output maps is then attributable to the force solvers alone, which is precisely the difference $f$ must learn.
  \item \textbf{Shell building} (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}). CosmoGridV1 stores its lightcone as 69 HEALPix particle-count shells out to $z=3.5$ at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$ \cite{kacprzak_cosmogridv1_2023}. Disco-DJ, however, ships only a restricted lightcone mode limited to the simulation box volume -- far short of the $z=3.5$ depth, which requires many periodic box replications (Sec.~\ref{sec:lightcones}). We therefore implemented a dedicated \emph{shell builder} inside Disco-DJ's time integration (\texttt{shell\_builder/}) that reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition and CosmoGridV1's shell binning exactly, so the low- and high-fidelity maps are defined shell-by-shell on identical radial bins.
  \item \textbf{Correction pipelines} (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}). On the resulting paired shells, three independent corrections $f$ are built and compared: a deterministic \emph{transfer-function} rescaling in harmonic space, a \emph{flow-matching U-Net} that transports each map patch from low to high fidelity, and an \emph{EDM diffusion} model that generates the missing small-scale residual from noise. All three are evaluated with the same shared statistics (per-shell and tomographic $C_\ell$, one-point moments, and weak-lensing convergence maps built from the corrected lightcone).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reconstructing the Linear Input and Handing It Over}
\label{subsec:ic_linear}
\label{subsec:ic_why_tk}
\label{subsec:ic_neutrinos}
\label{subsec:ic_handoff}

CosmoGridV1 does not distribute initial conditions, and the way its own runs were
started cannot be reused here. Recovering a starting point that both solvers can share
therefore takes three steps: establishing what has to be rebuilt, rebuilding it, and
checking that the two codes really do start from the same thing.

\paragraph{Why a tabulated transfer function is needed at all.} The published
configuration drives \textsc{PkdGrav3} in its \texttt{bClass=1} mode, in which the
cosmology is not specified through parameters but read wholesale from an HDF5 file
produced by \textsc{concept} \cite{dakin_cosmological_2022}, which calls \textsc{class}
internally and tabulates the full linear perturbation history of every species. In that
mode \textsc{PkdGrav3} does more than draw a particle load: it carries photons, the
massive-neutrino species, the dark-energy fluid and the metric perturbations as
\emph{linear} fields on their own $208^3$ grid
(\texttt{achLinSpecies = "g+ncdm[0]+fld+metric"}, \texttt{nGridLin = 208}) and adds
their gravitational effect to the $832^3$ nonlinear particles at every step. The linear
cosmology is an active ingredient throughout the run, not a boundary condition at the
start.

Disco-DJ has no counterpart to that machinery: it is a pure particle-mesh code
(Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}) and evolves particles under the Poisson equation alone. Were
\textsc{PkdGrav3} run with its linear species active while Disco-DJ was not, the two
lightcones would differ through the treatment of photons, neutrinos and dark energy
\emph{in addition} to the force solver, and the correction of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:correction_goal} would be learning that difference too -- defeating the
controlled comparison the whole thesis rests on. Both runs must therefore start from
one plain particle load with no linear species carried afterwards, which forces
\textsc{PkdGrav3} into \texttt{bClass=0} and replaces the HDF5 reference by
\texttt{achTfFile}, a static tabulated transfer function. CosmoGridV1 ships no such
table, so it has to be reconstructed. The file itself is minimal: \textsc{PkdGrav3}
parses two whitespace-separated columns per line, $k$ and $-T(k)/k^2$, and takes the
logarithm of both (\texttt{master.c}), so the tabulated values must be strictly
positive. This is neither \textsc{class}'s nor \textsc{camb}'s multi-column transfer
output -- the $-T/k^2$ normalisation follows \textsc{camb}'s convention, while the
two-column layout is \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s own.

\paragraph{Rebuilding it.} Each CosmoGridV1 run ships the linear perturbations its own
initial conditions were built from, as tabulated CDM+baryon density contrasts
$\delta_{cb}(k, a)$. From the $a=1$ slice the dimensional power spectrum is
reconstructed as
\begin{equation}
  P_{cb}(k) \;=\; \frac{2\pi^2}{k^3}\, P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k)\, \delta_{cb}^2(k)\, h^3,
  \qquad P_{\mathrm{prim}}(k) = A_s \left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{pivot}}}\right)^{n_s - 1},
  \label{eq:pk_reconstruct}

\section{Shell Builder}
\label{sec:shell_builder}

With identical initial conditions in place, the remaining prerequisite for Eq.~\eqref{eq:correction_goal} is that Disco-DJ emit its lightcone in exactly CosmoGridV1's format: particle-count maps on the same 69 radial shells. This section describes the shell builder (\texttt{shell\_builder/Shell\_Builder.py}) that adds this capability to Disco-DJ's time integration.

\subsection{Streaming Shell Accumulation}
\label{sec:shell_builder_core}

A lightcone output requires the particle distribution to be resampled at every integration step, since the observable past-lightcone shell changes continuously as the universe evolves. The most direct implementation -- store the full particle snapshot at each of the $n_{\mathrm{steps}}$ integration steps and decompose into shells afterwards -- is memory-prohibitive: retaining $n_{\mathrm{steps}}$ full-resolution snapshots simultaneously exceeds the available device memory for the particle counts considered here (cf.\ Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}). The shell decomposition is therefore \emph{interleaved} with the time integration, so that at any moment only the particle states immediately before and after the current step, plus the low-dimensional set of pixelised count maps, are held in memory (Figure~\ref{fig:shell_stream}).

\paragraph{Time discretization.} The radial shells are not defined by the integrator but
supplied externally, through a CosmoGridV1-format metainfo table: each shell
$s \in \{1,\dots,S\}$ is a redshift interval $[z_s^{\mathrm{lo}}, z_s^{\mathrm{hi}}]$ with a
corresponding comoving-distance interval $[r_s^{\mathrm{lo}}, r_s^{\mathrm{hi}}]$. Since the
shells are contiguous, their edges form a single ordered sequence of boundaries
$z_0 > z_1 > \dots > z_S$, which we take directly as the integration nodes, so that each shell
coincides with exactly one integration interval:
\begin{equation}
  a_0 < a_1 < \dots < a_S, \qquad a_j = \frac{1}{1+z_j}, \qquad
  \text{shell } s = [\,a_{s-1},\ a_s\,], \quad s = 1,\dots,S.
  \label{eq:step_grid}
\end{equation}
No shell edge is therefore ever crossed within a step. The interval
$[a_{\mathrm{ini}}, a_0]$ preceding the first shell is integrated separately with
$n_{\mathrm{pre}}$ sub-steps and without shell accumulation, to bring the particle state into
registry with the grid of Eq.~\eqref{eq:step_grid}; each of the remaining $S$ intervals
$[a_j, a_{j+1}]$, $j = 0,\dots,S-1$, is then advanced with a single sub-step.


\paragraph{Accumulation.} At step $j$, the comoving positions of all particles are recorded before and after the sub-step, $\{\mathbf{x}_p^{(j)}\}$ and $\{\mathbf{x}_p^{(j+1)}\}$, and the step's scale-factor edges are converted to comoving lightcone radii through the cosmology-dependent map $\chi(a)$,
\begin{equation}
  R_j = \chi(a_j), \qquad R_{j+1} = \chi(a_{j+1}), \qquad R_j > R_{j+1}.
  \label{eq:step_radii}
\end{equation}
The shells traversed during the step are those whose radial bin overlaps the swept annulus,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}_j = \big\{\, s \;:\; r_s^{\mathrm{lo}} < R_j \ \wedge\ r_s^{\mathrm{hi}} > R_{j+1} \,\big\};
  \label{eq:active_shells}
\end{equation}
because the step grid is built from the shell edges themselves, $\mathcal{A}_j$ contains a single shell in the generic case (the general form covers degenerate or adjacent bins in the input table). For each active shell, the crossing test of Sec.~\ref{sec:crossing} converts the pre-/post-step states into a HEALPix count map $\mathbf{M}_s^{(j)}$, which is added to the shell's running total,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{S}_s \;\mathrel{+}=\; \mathbf{M}_s^{(j)}, \qquad s \in \mathcal{A}_j.
  \label{eq:shell_update}
\end{equation}
The pre-step state is then discarded and the post-step state becomes the next step's pre-step state. Once all steps are processed, the per-shell maps $\{\mathbf{S}_s\}_{s=1}^{S}$ are serialized together with the shell table. At no point are more than two full particle snapshots and $S$ integer maps of size $N_{\mathrm{pix}}$ resident -- this is what removes the memory bottleneck of the naive end-of-run decomposition.

\subsection{Crossing Condition}
\label{sec:crossing}

Which particles contribute to a shell, and where, is decided by the moving-lightcone formulation of \textsc{PkdGrav3} \cite{noauthor_examplepkdgrav3_lightcone_2022}, which the shell builder reproduces exactly (\texttt{Shell\_Builder.py} implements the same crossing equation as \texttt{lightcone.cxx}); Figure~\ref{fig:crossing} illustrates the two ingredients.

\paragraph{Periodic replicas.} The observer is placed at the box centre, $\mathbf{o} = (L/2, L/2, L/2)$, with $L$ the box side length. Since the requested lightcone depth $\chi_{\max} = \chi\big(1/(1+z_{\max})\big)$ exceeds $L$, the simulation volume is periodically replicated (Sec.~\ref{sec:lightcones}): the finite replica set
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D} = \Big\{\, \mathbf{d} = (n_1, n_2, n_3)\,L \;:\; \mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^3, \ \; \text{replicated cube intersects the ball of radius } \chi_{\max} \text{ about } \mathbf{o} \,\Big\}
  \label{eq:replica_set}
\end{equation}
is precomputed once from $(\chi_{\max}, L)$. For each $\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}$ the nearest and farthest possible distances of the replicated cube from the observer,
\begin{equation}
  r_{\min}(\mathbf{d}) = \big\lVert \max(0, |\mathbf{d}| - L/2) \big\rVert, \qquad
  r_{\max}(\mathbf{d}) = \big\lVert |\mathbf{d}| + L/2 \big\rVert,
  \label{eq:replica_bounds}
\end{equation}
are also precomputed (componentwise $\max$ and absolute value, then the Euclidean norm), so that a replica whose radial range cannot overlap a step's lightcone annulus is rejected without evaluating a single particle.

\paragraph{Linear crossing formula.} For step $j$ with boundaries $a_j < a_{j+1}$, write $r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} = \chi(a_j)$ and $r_j^{\mathrm{lo}} = \chi(a_{j+1})$. For a particle with pre-/post-step positions $\mathbf{x}_p(a_j), \mathbf{x}_p(a_{j+1})$ relative to the observer and a replica offset $\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}$, define
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{R}_0 = \mathbf{x}_p(a_j) + \mathbf{d}, \qquad \mathbf{R}_1 = \mathbf{x}_p(a_{j+1}) + \mathbf{d}, \qquad
  r_0 = \lVert \mathbf{R}_0 \rVert, \qquad r_1 = \lVert \mathbf{R}_1 \rVert.
  \label{eq:replica_radii}
\end{equation}
Following \textsc{PkdGrav3}, both the particle radius $r_p(v)$ and the inward-moving lightcone front $R(v)$ are interpolated linearly over the step,
\begin{equation}
  r_p(v) = r_0 + v\,(r_1 - r_0), \qquad R(v) = r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - v\,(r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_j^{\mathrm{lo}}), \qquad v\in[0,1],
  \label{eq:scalar_interp}
\end{equation}
and the crossing $r_p(v^\ast) = R(v^\ast)$ is solved in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  v^\ast = \frac{r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_0}{\big(r_j^{\mathrm{hi}} - r_j^{\mathrm{lo}}\big) + (r_1 - r_0)}.
  \label{eq:vx_formula}
\end{equation}
A particle--replica pair is accepted only if $v^\ast \in [0,1)$. Since the half-open acceptance intervals of consecutive steps partition the time axis, every particle is caught in at most one step per replica; evaluating the crossing test against the (generally narrower) metainfo shell bounds instead of the step bounds would break this partition and could double-count particles crossing two adjacent shells within one step. Numerically, pairs with $|r_1 - r_0| \le 10^{-20}$ are treated as non-moving and rejected.

\paragraph{Shell assignment and pixelization.} The crossing position is obtained by applying $v^\ast$ to the vector positions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}} = (1-v^\ast)\,\mathbf{R}_0 + v^\ast\,\mathbf{R}_1, \qquad r_{\mathrm{cross}} = \lVert \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}} \rVert,
  \label{eq:cross_pos}
\end{equation}
and the pair is kept only if its crossing radius falls inside the shell bounds,
\begin{equation}
  r_s^{\mathrm{lo}} \le r_{\mathrm{cross}} < r_s^{\mathrm{hi}}.
  \label{eq:shell_filter}
\end{equation}
Surviving crossings are pixelised with the equal-area HEALPix \textsc{ring} scheme at resolution $N_{\mathrm{side}}$ from the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{cross}} = \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cross}}/r_{\mathrm{cross}}$, and the corresponding pixel of $\mathbf{M}_s^{(j)}$ is incremented. In the implementation, the loop over all active replicas runs inside a single JIT-compiled JAX kernel (\texttt{jax.lax.fori\_loop}), so the per-replica work is dispatched to the GPU as one program rather than from Python making it more efficient.

This procedure reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s box-replication lightcone \cite{noauthor_examplepkdgrav3_lightcone_2022} with two deliberate differences: the observer sits at the box centre rather than a corner, and the replica set $\mathcal{D}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_set} is sized adaptively from $(\chi_{\max}, L)$ -- with the per-step radial reject of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replica_bounds} -- rather than fixing the number of replicas a priori (\texttt{shell\_builder/utils/compute\_replica\_offsets} ).

Together, Secs.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} and \ref{sec:shell_builder} deliver what the learning problem needs: for every cosmology, two lightcones on identical shells that differ only through the force solver. The next section builds the corrections that close that difference.

\section{Correction Pipelines}
\label{sec:correction-pipelines}

Three correction pipelines were implemented, each a self-contained module under \texttt{ml/} (\texttt{transfer/}, \texttt{unet/}, \texttt{diffusion/}) sharing only the neutral analysis library \texttt{ml/analysis/}. They span the two paths identified in Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} -- one deterministic harmonic-space rescaling and two generative, patch-based models -- and are deliberately evaluated through the same shared statistics and plotting code, so their outputs are comparable figure-by-figure. Table~\ref{tab:pipelines} summarises the contrast; Figure~\ref{fig:shared_problem} states the shared problem.

One measured property of the data drives every design choice that follows. The per-multipole cross-correlation \cite{feng_fastpm_2016}
\begin{equation}
  r(\ell, s) \;=\; \frac{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}\times\mathrm{high}}\big\rangle}{\sqrt{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}\big\rangle\,\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}\big\rangle}},
  \label{eq:phase_corr}
\end{equation}
computed across the training runs (\texttt{ml/transfer/transfer\_function.py}), shows that on the dense, high-$z$ shells that carry signal, the low and high maps share phases ($r \approx 0.9$--$1.0$) well into the small scales: Disco-DJ mostly gets the small-scale amplitude wrong, not the positions of structures. This is why a pure amplitude rescaling (Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-transfer}) works at all. Only where $r$ decays -- on faint, shot-noise-dominated shells and at the highest multipoles (e.g.\ $r=0.56$ at $\ell=1000$ and $0.06$ at $\ell=2500$ on the faintest shells) -- do the maps genuinely decorrelate, and there the correct behaviour is not to amplify Disco-DJ's own noise but to generate statistically fresh small-scale structure, which is precisely the role of the two generative pipelines (Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}, \ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion}).

Two further design decisions are shared by all learned components and are motivated by measured failures of their alternatives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Correct only the small scales; pin the large scales to Disco-DJ.} On large scales Disco-DJ is already correct: the low and high maps agree both in phase ($r\approx 1$) and in amplitude, the transfer function $T\approx 1$ (defined below in Eq. \ref{eq:transfer_def}), so both generative pipelines are formulated as high-pass residual models: the learning target is $\mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{high}-\mathrm{low})$, where $\mathrm{HP}$ is a radial Fourier high-pass whose cutoff is set \emph{per shell} by a fixed comoving scale $L$, exactly as for the harmonic pipeline (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min}): the deficit begins at a fixed physical length, so its onset multipole $2\pi\chi(s)/L$ moves with the shell, and a single angular cutoff could be right for at most one of them. The corrected patch is composed as $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{sample})$. The composition re-applies the filter, so the output's large scales equal the low map's exactly, regardless of integration error (\texttt{ml/unet/flow\_model.py}, \texttt{ml/diffusion/model.py}). This formulation is a direct response to two observed failures: a full-field diffusion model drew each tile's large scales from independent noise, so after stitching, the cross-tile phases averaged incoherently and destroyed the low-$\ell$ convergence spectrum; and even the low$\to$high flow drifted the already-correct large scales by a few percent per shell, which compounded coherently across the dozens of shells a convergence map integrates. The harmonic pipeline applies the same principle exactly in $\ell$-space, forcing its correction factors to unity below $\ell_{\min}(s)$. In all three the hand-over is a step rather than a ramp: below the cutoff the map is already correct, above it the correction is wanted in full, and an earlier raised-cosine ramp was found to starve precisely the band the convergence spectra were most deficient in (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-transfer}).
  \item \textbf{Hold out whole cosmologies, not random patches.} The train/validation split is by cosmology (\texttt{dataset.split\_by\_cosmo}): patches from the same run share large-scale structure, so a random per-patch split would leak the validation set into training. All reported evaluations are on cosmologies the models never saw.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Patch Extraction and Full-Sky Reconstruction}
\label{subsec:pipe-patches}

The two patch-based pipelines share their data machinery. Training pairs are built by \texttt{make\_patch\_dataset.py}: for each patch, a random (cosmology, run), a random shell, a random centre pixel (uniform on the sphere), and a random orientation angle are drawn, and both the low and the high shell map are projected at that same pointing with a gnomonic (flat-sky) projection at the map's native pixel resolution -- a like-for-like $256\times256$ crop with no resampling beyond the projection itself. Each patch stores the metadata needed to recreate it exactly (centre, orientation, shell redshift bounds, comoving distance) together with the run's varied cosmological parameters $(\Omega_m, \Omega_b, n_s, \sigma_8, w_0, h)$ from the CosmoGridV1 metainfo catalogue. From these, the conditioning vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c} = \big(h,\ \Omega_{cdm},\ \Omega_b,\ \Omega_m,\ n_s,\ \sigma_8,\ w_0,\ z_{\mathrm{shell}}\big) \in \mathbb{R}^8
  \label{eq:cosmo_z}
\end{equation}
is assembled in one place (\texttt{dataset.cosmo\_z\_vector}) and consumed identically at train and inference time, so the two can never drift apart.

At inference, a full-sky shell is corrected by the overlap-tile scheme of Figure~\ref{fig:tiling} (\texttt{ml/analysis/patch\_tiling.py}): the sphere is tiled with overlapping gnomonic patches centred on a coarse HEALPix grid (chosen so the patch field of view is at least four times the centre spacing), the model corrects each tile, and the tiles are blended back with a radial cosine taper $w(r) = \tfrac12(1+\cos \pi r)$ that falls to zero at the tile edge, hiding the seams. The tile-to-sphere pixel indices depend only on the geometry, so they are built once and reused for every shell and cosmology -- replacing a per-tile re-projection that would otherwise dominate the entire reconstruction cost.

Two subtleties matter only for the stochastic pipeline (diffusion). First, each sky pixel is covered by many overlapping tiles and the blend averages them; if every tile drew independent randomness, the average would suppress the generated small-scale amplitude to $1/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ of its true value, with $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$ the effective number of contributing tiles. \cite{wiegand_kish_1968} The fix is to draw one global noise field on the sphere and hand each tile the crop matching its own pixels, so overlapping tiles agree by construction. Second, the taper is raised to a power $p$ before normalisation ($p=32$ by default in \texttt{apply\_diffusion.py}): large $p$ makes the nearest tile ``own'' each pixel (a soft Voronoi partition), in the spirit of the original overlap-tile strategy \cite{ronneberger_u-net_2015} -- overlap provides context, but each output pixel comes from essentially one prediction. Deterministic per-tile models are unaffected ($p=1$ is kept for the flow), since their overlapping predictions already agree. \new{The same tension -- fusing many overlapping diffusion samples into one globally consistent image -- is what \cite{bar-tal_multidiffusion_2023} address by binding the per-tile denoising to a shared global solution; the shared-noise construction here plays that role on the sphere.}

\subsection{Harmonic-Space Transfer-Function Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipe-transfer}

The first pipeline (\texttt{ml/transfer/}) contains no image-level network at all: it exploits the phase-sharing fact of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr} and corrects only mode amplitudes, in the spherical-harmonic basis anticipated in Sec.~\ref{subsec:sr-problem}. Every shell is transformed to its harmonic coefficients $a_{\ell m}$ (up to $\ell_{\max}=3000$), and each mode is rescaled by a per-shell transfer function \cite{feng_fastpm_2016, garrison_improving_2016}
\begin{equation}
  a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{corr}} = a_{\ell m}^{\mathrm{low}} \cdot T(\ell, s),
  \qquad
  T(\ell, s) = \sqrt{\frac{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{high}}(s) \big\rangle}{\big\langle C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}(s) \big\rangle}},
  \label{eq:transfer_def}
\end{equation}
which by construction matches the target power spectrum while preserving the (correct) phases. Both spectra are smoothed in $\log_{10}$-space with a boxcar filter before the ratio is taken: the physical $T(\ell)$ is smooth, whereas a single realisation's $C_\ell$ carries sample-variance and shot-noise scatter that would otherwise be fitted and amplified. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_flow} shows the apply chain.

\paragraph{Two estimators of $T$.} The \texttt{fit} mode averages the smoothed spectra over the training runs and treats $T$ as a property of the solver pair, independent of cosmology -- a robust deterministic baseline. The \texttt{emulate} mode instead trains a small MLP emulator of $T$ on features $\big(\log_{10}(\ell{+}1),\ z,\ H_0,\ \Omega_{cdm},\ \Omega_b,\ \Omega_m,\ n_s,\ \sigma_8,\ \log_{10} C_\ell^{\mathrm{low}}\big)$, standardised per feature; this interpolates the transfer function to unseen cosmologies of the grid. The emulator is a plain feed-forward network (hidden layers $256$--$256$--$128$, ReLU, linear output) implemented in PyTorch and trained on GPU (Adam, learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch size $4096$, $L_2$ weight decay $\alpha = 10^{-4}$), with a 15\% held-out validation split, early stopping, and best-validation weights restored (\texttt{transfer\_function.py}). The GPU implementation matters for a reason worth recording: the training set is millions of $(\ell, s)$ rows with only 9 features each, a workload where a CPU implementation is bound by per-batch overhead rather than arithmetic -- a GPU handles exactly this trivial-per-sample, huge-throughput shape. Both estimators also store the phase correlation $R(\ell, s)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr}.

\paragraph{What the emulator learns.} Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_function_learned}
shows the two objects the pipeline actually runs on, as predicted for one
held-out cosmology. Both behave exactly as the construction of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer_def} and the phase argument of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase_corr}
require, and both do so as a function of shell depth. The gain $T(\ell, s)$ sits
at unity across the large scales on every shell -- the emulator recovers "no
correction needed here" on its own, without being told, since the ramp of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min} is imposed only later at apply time -- and rises only once
the multipole reaches the scale where the particle-mesh solver has begun to lose
power. Where that happens tracks the shell's comoving distance, in the same way
the validation of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation} showed the deficit itself
tracking it: the nearest shell shown ($z \approx 0.09$) needs a gain of $T = 1.12$
at $\ell = 500$, $1.46$ at $\ell = 1000$ and $1.75$ at $\ell = 1500$ -- close to a
factor $3$ in $C_\ell$, since the correction enters as $T^2$ -- the intermediate
shells progressively less and later ($T = 1.15$, $1.05$ and $1.02$ at $\ell = 1500$
for $z \approx 0.26$, $0.49$ and $0.77$), and the most distant shell
($z \approx 2.2$) stays within half a percent of unity across the entire measured
range. The correction the emulator
applies to a shell is thus set almost entirely by how far away that shell is.

The phase correlation $r(\ell, s)$ in the right-hand panel is what licenses this
whole approach, and it also marks its limit. On the shells that carry most of the
signal it stays at unity far into the nonlinear regime -- $r = 0.996$ at
$\ell = 1500$ on the shell at $z \approx 0.77$, and $0.999$ at $z \approx 2.2$ --
meaning Disco-DJ has placed the
structure correctly and only mis-weighted it, which is precisely the regime in
which rescaling mode amplitudes and keeping their phases is the right operation.
On the nearest, sparsest shells it decays instead ($r = 0.79$ at $\ell = 1000$ and
$0.51$ at $\ell = 1500$ for $z \approx 0.09$), and there
the two maps no longer describe the same structures at all: multiplying by
$T$ still restores the correct power, but the small-scale pattern it
produces is amplified Disco-DJ noise rather than recovered structure. That is the
gap the generative pipelines of Secs.~\ref{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}
and~\ref{subsec:pipe-diffusion} are built to close.

A final feature is the turnover of $T$ at the highest multipoles on the nearest
shell, which peaks near $\ell \approx 2200$ and then declines. It is the same
shot-noise floor identified in Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation}: both simulations
carry the same particle count, so once their spectra are shot-noise dominated
their ratio returns towards unity and $T \to 1$ with it. The emulator is not
failing there -- it is correctly reporting that there is no longer a clustering
deficit to repair, only two equal noise floors.

\paragraph{Where to correct.} The correction must not touch the already-correct large
scales, and ``large'' is a physical statement: the PM resolution deficit sets in at a fixed
comoving scale (of order the PM grid cell), which maps to a different multipole on every
shell. Using the flat-sky (Limber) relation
\begin{equation}
  \ell + \tfrac{1}{2} = k\,\chi(z), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{L},
  \label{eq:limber}
\end{equation}
of \cite{loverde_extended_2008}, a fixed scale $L$ ($17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$ in the production
\new{configuration, \texttt{run\_transfer.sh}; see Sec.~\ref{sec:res-transfer} for how it was}
\new{chosen) with wavenumber \mbox{$k = 2\pi/L$} is converted per shell to}
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\min}(s) = \left\lceil \frac{2\pi\,\chi(z_s)}{L} - \frac{1}{2} \right\rceil
  \;\approx\; \left\lceil \frac{2\pi\,\chi(z_s)}{L} \right\rceil,
  \label{eq:ell_min}
\end{equation}
where the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ -- retained by \citeauthor{loverde_extended_2008} because dropping it
degrades the Limber approximation at $\mathcal{O}(\ell^{-1})$ -- is negligible for the threshold
multipoles $\ell_{\min}\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{2}\text{--}10^{3})$ relevant here, and is dropped in
the implementation (\texttt{ell\_min\_from\_mpc\_h}). Below $\ell_{\min}$, $T$ is forced to $1$,
with a raised-cosine ramp $w(\ell)$ handing over smoothly above it (a hard step in harmonic space
rings in real space). This matches the measured behaviour: nearby shells visibly deviate from
CosmoGridV1 from $\ell \sim 150$--$300$, while the most distant shells show no deviation out to
$\ell = 3000$ -- and correctly receive no correction, because their $\ell_{\min}$ exceeds
$\ell_{\max}$.

\paragraph{Negative pixels.} The corrected field inevitably contains negative pixels, and it is worth being precise about why. The truth is a non-negative, integer count map, and on the distant, sparse shells the mean count per pixel lies far below unity, so most pixels are exactly zero. The correction, by contrast, is a band-limited real-space fluctuation field: the residual $(T-1)\,a_{\ell m}$ reconstructed by the inverse SHT is a smooth, zero-mean oscillation with both signs. Adding such a field onto a map dominated by empty pixels necessarily drives every pixel where the negative half-wave exceeds the local count below zero -- negativity is a structural consequence of representing a boosted continuous overdensity on top of a nearly empty discrete map, not a numerical defect of the method. Every cheap pixel-wise repair was measured and rejected: flooring at zero deletes only the negative excursions, which raises the mean and injects spurious power exactly on the small scales the correction just restored, and re-balancing the mean afterwards or correcting the logarithm of the density instead merely redistributes the same bias. The corrected field is therefore deliberately not post-processed: it is treated as a continuous overdensity field (\texttt{--no-clip}) and passed unchanged into every downstream statistic -- angular power spectra, moments, and the weak-lensing convergence maps of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} -- none of which requires per-pixel count validity.

\subsection{U-Net Flow-Matching Pipeline}
\label{subsec:pipe-unet-flow}

The second pipeline (\texttt{ml/unet/}) is the first of the two generative ones: a conditional flow-matching model (Sec.~\ref{sssec:flow-matching}) acting on the gnomonic patches of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches}. Its defining choice is the start point of the flow: instead of Gaussian noise, the ODE starts at the low-fidelity patch itself,
\begin{equation}
  x_0 = \delta_{\mathrm{low}}, \qquad
  x_1 = x_0 + \mathrm{HP}\!\left(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}}\right), \qquad
  x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1,
  \label{eq:flow_bridge}
\end{equation}
so the model learns a bridge from low to high rather than a full generative map from noise (\texttt{flow\_model.py}). Because $x_0$ already carries the correct large-scale structure, the target velocity $u = x_1 - x_0 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}})$ is a pure high-pass field -- integrating it can only ever \add small-scale content -- and the trajectory is nearly straight, so 8 Euler steps suffice at inference (Table~\ref{tab:pipelines}). The training loss is the flow-matching regression of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_matching} on this interpolant,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\;
  \frac{\big\lVert v_\theta(x_t, t, \mathbf{c}) - (x_1 - x_0) \big\rVert^2}{\max\!\big(\mathrm{Var}(x_1{-}x_0)\,/\,\overline{\mathrm{Var}},\ 0.1\big)},
  \label{eq:flow_loss}
\end{equation}
where the denominator is a bounded, batch-relative per-patch variance normalisation: a dense, well-resolved shell's true residual is naturally tiny, so plain MSE gives it far weaker gradients than a sparse shell's large residual, and the network never learns to calibrate the ``correct answer is nearly zero here'' case -- visible as a persistent few-percent bias on the densest shells. Normalising by each patch's own residual variance, relative to the batch mean and clamped to at most a $10\times$ upweighting, removes this imbalance without the gradient blow-ups an unbounded reweighting was observed to cause (\texttt{train\_flow.py}).

\paragraph{Architecture.} The velocity network \texttt{FlowUNet} instantiates the generic U-Net of Figure~\ref{fig:unet_generic} with four levels and base width 32: channels $1\to32\to64\to128\to256\to512$ at spatial resolutions $256^2 \to 16^2$, mirrored by the decoder with skip concatenations, and a $1\times1$ output head (Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}). Two conditioning paths are threaded through it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flow time $t$, everywhere.} \new{Time is embedded sinusoidally (dimension 128, widened to 512 by a two-layer MLP) and then allowed to reach into every convolution block through FiLM \cite{perez_film_2017}. A block is two \mbox{$3\times3$} convolutions with GroupNorm(8) and SiLU; between them, a linear layer belonging to that block alone turns the shared embedding into one scale and one shift per channel, applied as \mbox{$h \mapsto h\,(1+\mathrm{scale}) + \mathrm{shift}$}. Conditioning thus enters as a per-channel affine transform rather than as an extra input, which is what lets a single set of weights represent the whole time-dependent velocity field.} One network thus represents the whole time-dependent velocity field $v_\theta(x, t)$.
  \item \textbf{Cosmology and redshift, once.} The 8-vector $\mathbf{c}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cosmo_z} passes through a two-layer MLP and is added, broadcast over space, into the $512$-channel bottleneck latent. The MLP's last layer is zero-initialised, so conditioning starts as an exact no-op and the conditioned model is architecturally identical to the unconditioned one at initialisation -- keeping training stable and the ablation clean.
\end{itemize}
The output head is likewise zero-initialised, so the untrained model predicts $v = 0$ (``stay at $x_0$'') -- the correct baseline from which to learn a correction. Training uses Adam (learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$, scaled linearly with the number of data-parallel ranks), cosine-annealed over the epochs, gradient clipping at norm 1, and PyTorch DDP (Distributed Data Parallel) across the four GPUs of a node (\texttt{train\_flow.py}). At inference, \texttt{apply\_flow.py} integrates $\dot x = v_\theta(x, t, \mathbf{c})$ with Euler steps, composes $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(x(1) - x_0)$ through the same filter code used to build the training target, and reconstructs full skies via Figure~\ref{fig:tiling}.

\subsection{Diffusion Pipeline (EDM)}
\label{subsec:pipe-diffusion}

The third pipeline (\texttt{ml/diffusion/}) replaces the low$\to$high bridge with a genuinely generative process in the EDM formulation of \citeauthor{karras_elucidating_2022} (Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}): every sample starts from pure Gaussian noise, and the low map enters only as conditioning. What is diffused is again not the full field but the high-pass residual $x_1 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{\mathrm{high}} - \delta_{\mathrm{low}})$, for the reasons of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}; the conditioning, by contrast, is the \emph{full} low patch at all scales, channel-concatenated to the noisy input -- the denoiser needs the large-scale context to know which small-scale structure to synthesise, even though its output is band-limited.

\paragraph{Preconditioned denoiser.} The network is the two-channel variant of Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}, wrapped in the EDM preconditioning (\texttt{model.EDMPrecond}). Here $x$ (analogous to $x_t$ in flow matching) is the noisy high-pass residual whose clean target is $x_1 = \mathrm{HP}(\delta_{high}-\delta_{low})$, and $\delta_{low}$ is the low-resolution conditioning map (used at all scales) that steers the denoiser.
\begin{equation}
  D_\theta(x, \sigma, \mathrm{cond}) = c_{\mathrm{skip}}(\sigma)\,x
    + c_{\mathrm{out}}(\sigma)\, F_\theta\!\big(\,[\,c_{\mathrm{in}}(\sigma)\,x \,\oplus\, \delta_\mathrm{low}\,],\ c_{\mathrm{noise}}(\sigma),\ \mathbf{c}\big),
  \label{eq:edm_precond}
\end{equation}
with the standard coefficients
\begin{equation}
  c_{\mathrm{skip}} = \frac{\sigma_d^2}{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{\sigma\,\sigma_d}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}}, \quad
  c_{\mathrm{noise}} = \tfrac14 \ln \sigma,
  \label{eq:edm_coeffs}
\end{equation}
\cite{karras_elucidating_2022}. Each coefficient has a distinct job, and together they let \emph{one} network operate across five orders of magnitude in noise level (Figure~\ref{fig:edm_sampling}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item $c_{\mathrm{in}}$ normalises the input: $x = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$ has variance $\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2$, so $c_{\mathrm{in}}\,x$ has unit variance at \emph{every} noise level -- the raw network $F_\theta$ never has to adapt its operating range to $\sigma$.
  \item $c_{\mathrm{skip}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}}$ blend an identity path with the network output. At small $\sigma$, $c_{\mathrm{skip}} \to 1$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}} \to 0$: the noisy input is already almost the answer, and the network only supplies a small correction. At large $\sigma$, $c_{\mathrm{skip}} \to 0$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}} \to \sigma_d$: the input is essentially pure noise, and the network must synthesise the answer outright. The denoiser thus interpolates continuously between ``pass through'' and ``generate'', instead of one set of weights having to learn both regimes unaided.
  \item $c_{\mathrm{noise}}$ log-compresses $\sigma \in [0.002, 80]$ into an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ scalar, which is what the sinusoidal embedding of Figure~\ref{fig:film_unet}(a) receives -- the diffusion counterpart of the flow time $t$.
\end{itemize}
The conditioning channel ($\delta_{low}$) is neither noised nor rescaled by $c_{\mathrm{in}}$ -- it is not part of the diffusion process, it only steers it. One EDM default is deliberately not adopted: the data scale $\sigma_d$, tuned to $0.5$ for natural images in \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}, is instead measured as the standard deviation of the actual training target -- the high-pass residual, whose variance differs materially from a natural image's -- over a few real batches at start-up (\texttt{estimate\_sigma\_data}); using the wrong $\sigma_d$ would mis-scale all four coefficients of Eq.~\eqref{eq:edm_coeffs} at once.

\paragraph{Training.} Per example, a noise level is drawn as $\ln\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(-1.2, 1.2^2)$, the target is noised to $x_t = x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon$, and the denoising error is weighted per noise level and normalised per patch:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, \lambda(\sigma)\,
      \frac{\big\lVert D_\theta(x_1 + \sigma\varepsilon,\ \sigma,\ \delta_{\mathrm{low}}) - x_1 \big\rVert^2}
           {\max\!\big(\mathrm{Var}(x_1)/\overline{\mathrm{Var}},\ 0.1\big)} \right],
  \qquad
  \lambda(\sigma) = \frac{\sigma^2 + \sigma_d^2}{(\sigma\,\sigma_d)^2},
  \label{eq:edm_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda(\sigma)$ is EDM's weighting that equalises gradient magnitude across noise levels \cite{karras_elucidating_2022}, and the bounded per-patch normalisation is the same device -- and the same measured motivation -- as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_loss}. The bound is essential here: an unbounded $1/\mathrm{Var}$ divisor compounds multiplicatively with $\lambda(\sigma)$'s own dynamic range, and the combination was observed to produce loss spikes that prevented convergence entirely. The optimiser setup mirrors the flow pipeline's.

\paragraph{Sampling.} Inference follows EDM's deterministic Algorithm 1 (\texttt{model.sample\_heun}): the Karras noise schedule
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_i = \Big(\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho} + \tfrac{i}{N-1}\big(\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho} - \sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho}\big)\Big)^{\rho},
  \qquad \rho = 7,\ \ \sigma \in [0.002,\ 80],
  \label{eq:karras_schedule}
\end{equation}
integrated with Heun's second-order method over $N = 32$ steps from $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\max}^2 I)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:edm_sampling}). The step count is not a tuning failure but the point of this pipeline: a trajectory from pure noise is genuinely curved (Sec.~\ref{sssec:diffusion-theory}), and with up to two denoiser calls per Heun step, this is the most expensive of the three corrections per shell. The corrected patch is composed exactly as in the flow pipeline, $\mathrm{low} + \mathrm{HP}(\mathrm{sample})$, and full skies are reconstructed with the shared global noise field and $p=32$ blending of Sec.~\ref{subsec:pipe-patches} -- the sampler is deterministic given (noise, conditioning), which is precisely what makes overlapping tiles consistent.

\centering

In summary, the three pipelines answer the same question at three levels of ambition: transfer restores the power spectrum by construction but cannot restore structure; unet-flow transports each patch along a short, nearly straight bridge and restores stochastic small-scale power at moderate cost; diffusion generates the missing band from noise through a full multi-step sampler, at the highest cost. All three inherit the same guarantees from the shared formulation -- large scales pinned to Disco-DJ, corrections confined to the scales where the deficit lives, and evaluation on held-out cosmologies. Running them at production scale is the subject of the next section.

\subsection{Local Development}
\label{sec:local_dev}

Both codes were first set up on a local machine to establish a minimal working configuration with an interactive debugging loop: a bug in the shell-builder logic of Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder} or the IC hand-off of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation} is caught in seconds, rather than after a queued cluster job has run to completion. Tests at $16^3 = 4096$ particles complete in under a minute on a single core; such a run is not physically meaningful, it exercises the full pipeline -- initial conditions, integration, lightcone accumulation -- end to end before committing to a production configuration.

The binding constraint at production size is memory. Storing only the raw phase-space state -- six single-precision components per particle -- for a CosmoGridV1-sized run gives
\begin{equation}
    N_p = 832^3 \approx 5.8\times10^{8}, \qquad
    N_p \times 24\ \text{bytes} \approx 14\ \text{GB},
    \label{eq:phasespace_mem}
\end{equation}
already beyond a typical workstation's RAM once any working headroom is included -- and this floor is exceeded differently by the two codes. \textsc{PkdGrav3} carries, on top of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phasespace_mem}, its per-particle tree, per-particle rung state, and domain-decomposition buffers (Sec.~\ref{sec:pkdgrav}), so its footprint grows with tree depth and rung occupancy. Disco-DJ is instead bounded by its force mesh: the Poisson solve is one global FFT (Sec.~\ref{sec:discodj}), so the whole PM grid -- density, its Fourier transform, and force components -- must be resident simultaneously, and the demand scales with the chosen mesh resolution rather than the particle count. Production runs of either code therefore require a cluster.

\subsection{Euler (ETH Zurich)}

On Euler, each code gets its own virtual environment: Disco-DJ is built on JAX and needs a matched GPU software stack, while \textsc{PkdGrav3} is a compiled tree code with its own toolchain -- a per-code split that avoids the version conflicts a shared environment invites. Euler provides the memory and GPU partitions for the full $832^3$ resolution; the GPU acceleration specifically benefits Disco-DJ, whose entire force solve is GPU-native, whereas the \textsc{PkdGrav3} build used here is CPU-bound. Long-running jobs are distributed across multiple nodes where the code supports it.

\subsection{CSCS (Alps)}

The largest runs use the CSCS Alps system, whose NVIDIA GH200 nodes provide 120~GB of on-package memory each. This directly addresses Disco-DJ's binding constraint: since its memory demand tracks the PM mesh (Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev}), a larger memory pool translates into a finer force mesh -- and hence better small-scale force resolution -- per node. Production runs use \texttt{RES\_PM} $= 1664 = 2\times832$ mesh cells per dimension at the CosmoGridV1 box and particle count (Appendix~\ref{app:slurm}). The same nodes also host the correction-pipeline training of Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}, where the memory allows large per-GPU batches of $256^2$ patches, and DDP training spans the four GPUs of a node.

\section{Software Engineering}
\subsection{Code and Data Layout}

Executables and virtual environments live in the cluster's backed-up but quota-limited \texttt{HOME}; simulation snapshots, shell maps, per-job logs, and diagnostic plots -- bulk, regenerable output -- go to \texttt{SCRATCH}, which trades persistence for effectively unbounded (periodically purged) capacity. Keeping every submission's full log proved valuable for debugging and cross-run comparison alike: a run's log is often the only remaining record of exactly which configuration produced a given output. The CosmoGridV1 reference dataset ($\sim110$~TB) was transferred to CSCS with a managed Globus transfer job, which runs unattended and recovers from dropped connections -- an interactive single-stream copy at this scale would be both too slow and too fragile.

\subsection{Job Scheduling and Submission}

Every Python driver is paired with a Slurm batch script: the driver runs unchanged from the local tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:local_dev} up to a production allocation, while only the surrounding batch script changes. Jobs are launched interactively (\texttt{bash}) for fast-turnaround debugging or queued (\texttt{sbatch}) for long unattended runs. Each batch script follows the same two-part layout (Appendix~\ref{app:slurm}): a header of \texttt{\#SBATCH} directives declaring \emph{resources} (nodes, GPUs, task layout, exclusivity, wall-clock limit), \emph{accounting} (account, partition), and \emph{bookkeeping} (job name, working directory, \texttt{\%j}-tagged log paths so concurrent runs never overwrite each other) -- followed by a short execution body that activates the code-specific environment, fixes input paths (e.g.\ the \texttt{CosmoML.tipsy} IC of Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and sets the physical parameters as shell variables (\texttt{RES=832}, \texttt{RES\_PM=1664}, \texttt{BOXSIZE=900.0}).

The resource header also fixes the job's cost against the allocation,
\begin{equation}
    C = n_{\mathrm{nodes}} \times t \quad [\text{node-hours}],
    \label{eq:node_hours}
\end{equation}
billed regardless of how much of $t$ is spent computing -- which is exactly why short single-node interactive sessions are used for debugging and exclusive multi-node allocations are reserved for production. One detail keeps the scripts scalable: the header reserves one task per node, and the execution body computes the real degree of parallelism as one process per GPU, \texttt{NTASKS=\$((SLURM\_NNODES * 4))}, passed explicitly to \texttt{srun}. Scaling a job up therefore means editing a single \texttt{--nodes} line; the process-per-GPU launch follows automatically.

\subsection{Version Control}

The whole project -- the analysis and ML pipelines together with the modified Disco-DJ containing the shell builder -- is kept in a dedicated Git repository. Beyond protection against machine failure, this gives a reliable way back to a known-good state when an experimental change breaks the pipeline, and keeps the many configuration variants organised.

\paragraph{Statistics and resolutions.} Every pipeline is scored with the same shared diagnostics (\texttt{ml/analysis/}): per-shell full-sky angular power spectra pooled into three redshift bins, example patches with per-patch 2D power ratios, one-point moments (variance, skewness, excess kurtosis) as a function of shell depth, and weak-lensing convergence maps. The $\kappa$ maps are built with the \textsc{UFalcon} lightcone integration \cite{sgier_fast_2018} (\texttt{ml/analysis/weak\_lensing.py}): all corrected shells within a source bin's redshift range are combined into one convergence map per cosmology at the same $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, $\ell \le 1500$ as the shells they integrate -- building $\kappa$ at a finer resolution than its input shells would only upsample, producing spectra above the shells' own band limit that are pixel-window artefacts rather than signal -- using the DES-Y3 \textsc{metacal} source redshift distributions of tomographic bins 1 ($z \lesssim 1.05$) and 4 ($z \lesssim 1.85$) -- the lowest- and highest-redshift bins, which weight the nearby (hardest) and distant (easiest) shells respectively. \paragraph{A common evaluation footing.} All three pipelines are scored at one and the same resolution: shells at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, with the correction band capped at $\ell_{\max}=1500$. This is a deliberate choice rather than a technical limit. Scoring each method at the best resolution it happens to support would confound two different things -- how good the correction is, and how much input power its representation was given -- and would flatter the transfer function on exactly the small scales where the comparison is interesting.

\new{\paragraph{Choosing where the correction starts.} The onset scale \mbox{$L$} of}
\new{Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min} is the pipeline's one free geometric parameter, and it was}
\new{initially set to \mbox{$5\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} on the reasoning that the deficit originates near}
\new{the particle-mesh cell.} \new{Measurement does not support that.} \new{Taking the multipole at which}
\new{each shell's uncorrected ratio first falls below \mbox{$0.99$} and converting it to a comoving}
\new{scale gives \mbox{$2\pi\chi(s)/\ell_{\rm onset}(s) = 17.0 \pm 1.1\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$}, constant to}
\new{\mbox{$4\%$} over shells spanning a factor \mbox{$22$} in comoving distance.} \new{The deficit does set in at}
\new{a fixed comoving scale, as Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation} argued -- but that scale is}
\new{\mbox{$17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$}, some thirty particle-mesh cells, not one.} \new{An onset at}
\new{\mbox{$5\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} therefore opens the correction a factor \mbox{$3.4$} too late on every}
\new{shell, leaving the first part of the deficit untouched.}

\new{Re-running the pipeline with \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} and nothing else changed confirms}
\new{it.} \new{In the nearest redshift bin the corrected shell spectra move from \mbox{$0.977$} to \mbox{$1.003$} over \mbox{$\ell \in [400, 900]$} and from \mbox{$0.972$} to \mbox{$1.001$} over \mbox{$[900, 1300]$}: the residual undercorrection was the correction opening too late, not a limitation of the method.} \new{The middle bin, previously switched off entirely at this band limit, is now corrected and also lands on unity (\mbox{$0.985 \to 1.002$}, \mbox{$0.973 \to 1.003$}).} \new{Critically, the far bin does not move -- it sits at \mbox{$0.998$}--\mbox{$1.004$} under both settings -- so the earlier onset does not begin perturbing shells that were already correct, which is the failure mode that would have ruled it out.}

\new{A second change was made at the same time and for the same reason. The hand-over above \mbox{$\ell_{\min}$} was originally a raised-cosine ramp of width \mbox{$0.10\,\ell_{\max}$}, introduced against ringing in real space. Measured against what it cost, that trade was a poor one: the ramp held the correction below full strength for some \mbox{$150$} multipoles past each shell's onset, which is precisely the band the convergence spectra were most deficient in. Since \mbox{$\ell_{\min}$} now tracks the shell, there is nothing left for a ramp to protect -- below it the shell is already correct, above it the correction is wanted in full -- so the hand-over is a hard step. All numbers below use \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} with no ramp.}

\new{\paragraph{Full-sky \mbox{$C_\ell$}.} With that onset, Figure~\ref{fig:transfer_cl} shows the}
\new{correction working across the lightcone rather than in one bin.} \new{In the nearest bin it}
\new{lifts the ratio from \mbox{$0.953$} to \mbox{$1.003$} at \mbox{$\ell \in [400,900]$}, from \mbox{$0.854$} to \mbox{$1.001$}}
\new{at \mbox{$[900,1300]$}, and from \mbox{$0.768$} to \mbox{$1.003$} at \mbox{$[1300,1500]$} -- a repair worth up to}
\new{\mbox{$23$} percentage points, landing within \mbox{$0.3\%$} of the truth.} \new{The middle bin, whose}
\new{uncorrected deficit reaches \mbox{$2.7\%$} at the band edge, is likewise brought to \mbox{$1.003$}.} \new{The}
\new{most distant bin is left alone, unchanged at \mbox{$0.998$}--\mbox{$1.004$}: its \mbox{$\ell_{\min}$} exceeds}
\new{\mbox{$\ell_{\max}$} and, as Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_validation} showed, it has no deficit to}
\new{repair.} \new{That the pipeline corrects two bins to a few parts in a thousand while leaving}
\new{the third untouched is the behaviour Eq.~\eqref{eq:ell_min} was designed to produce.}

\section{U-Net Flow-Matching Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-flow}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The flow was trained for 200 epochs on the $10^5$-patch dataset (Table~\ref{tab:training}), distributed over 16 DDP ranks (4 Alps nodes $\times$ 4 GH200), with the base learning rate $3\times10^{-5}$ scaled linearly to $4.8\times10^{-4}$ and cosine-annealed; \new{the high-pass residual used the same per-shell onset \mbox{$L = 17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} as the harmonic pipeline, so the model is asked to correct exactly the band that is actually deficient on each shell.} Figure~\ref{fig:flow_loss} shows the variance-normalised flow-matching loss of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_loss}: \new{validation improves until epoch \mbox{$\approx 109$} (best \mbox{$0.145$} at epoch 109 -- the checkpoint used for everything below)} and then drifts slowly upward while the training loss continues to fall, i.e.\ mild but genuine overfitting past that point. The residual plateau is expected rather than alarming: at intermediate $t$ the target velocity is not uniquely determined by the network's input, so the regression loss is bounded below by this irreducible conditional variance.

\section{Diffusion Pipeline}
\label{sec:res-diffusion}

\paragraph{Configuration and training.} The diffusion model shares the flow's data, architecture width, schedule length, and high-pass formulation (Table~\ref{tab:training}); \new{it differs in the objective (Eq.~\ref{eq:edm_loss}, with the measured \mbox{$\sigma_d = 1.149$} -- larger than before, because the per-shell onset admits a wider band into the residual the model must synthesise), the unscaled learning rate of \mbox{$3\times10^{-5}$}, and the 32-step Heun sampler with shared sphere noise and \mbox{$p=32$} blending at inference.} Its loss curve (Figure~\ref{fig:diff_loss}) looks qualitatively different from the flow's: the EDM-weighted denoising loss collapses from $\approx 5.9$ to $\approx 0.6$ within the first $\sim 20$ epochs and is then almost flat, \new{with training and validation coinciding to the end (best validation \mbox{$0.512$} at epoch 165) -- no overfitting is observed.} This is characteristic of the objective: every example is presented at a freshly drawn noise level and noise realisation, an implicit augmentation that makes memorisation far harder than for the flow's fixed low$\to$high pairs.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:res-synthesis}

Figure~\ref{fig:method_comparison} reduces all three pipelines to a single view: for each held-out cosmology the ratio of a $\kappa$-map moment to its reference value, averaged over the 30 cosmologies, with the cosmology-to-cosmology scatter as an errorbar. Because every method is scored at the same $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, $\ell_{\max}=1500$ footing, the vertical ordering is a statement about the corrections themselves rather than about the resolutions they were given.

\new{Three consistent messages emerge.} \new{First, every pipeline repairs the two-point statistics on the scales it acts on, and at a small fraction of the \mbox{$N$}-body cost: the uncorrected bin~1 variance ratio of \mbox{$0.803 \pm 0.018$} is lifted to \mbox{$0.973 \pm 0.008$} by the flow, \mbox{$0.967 \pm 0.006$} by the transfer function and \mbox{$0.948 \pm 0.010$} by the diffusion model.} \new{The three are within three percentage points of one another, which is the substantive point: on a two-point statistic the cheapest correction is competitive with the most expensive, and the transfer function achieves its result at essentially zero inference cost and with the tightest scatter.}

\new{Second, the higher moments separate the deterministic from the generative route cleanly, and the separation is structural rather than a matter of tuning.} \new{A pure amplitude rescale preserves phases, so it cannot manufacture the phase correlations that skew a distribution; the transfer function accordingly moves skewness only from \mbox{$0.733 \pm 0.020$} to \mbox{$0.793 \pm 0.027$} and excess kurtosis from \mbox{$0.537 \pm 0.042$} to \mbox{$0.638 \pm 0.058$}. Moving its correction onset from \mbox{$5$} to \mbox{$17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-transfer}) improved its variance and its whole \mbox{$C_\ell$} recovery substantially while leaving those two essentially untouched -- which is the cleanest possible demonstration that the limitation is structural.} \new{The generative models go far beyond it: the flow reaches \mbox{$0.989 \pm 0.011$} in skewness and \mbox{$0.979 \pm 0.037$} in excess kurtosis, the diffusion \mbox{$0.896 \pm 0.013$} and \mbox{$0.772 \pm 0.036$}.}

\new{Third, the flow is the strongest correction overall.} \new{It leads on every one of the three moments in both source bins, and it does so with the whole non-Gaussian signal essentially recovered rather than merely improved -- an excess kurtosis within \mbox{$2\%$} of the reference where the uncorrected lightcone retains barely half of it.} \new{The residual across all methods remains one-sided -- every correction undershoots, none overshoots -- so what is left is a small systematic deficit rather than added noise.}

\new{The same picture holds in bin~4 with uniformly smaller gaps (flow \mbox{$0.992/0.992/0.983$}, diffusion \mbox{$0.986/0.963/0.906$}, transfer \mbox{$0.979/0.892/0.784$}), as expected: the distant shells are the ones Disco-DJ already gets nearly right, so less has to be repaired.} \new{Taken together the results suggest a division of labour rather than an outright winner.} \new{Where an analysis consumes two-point statistics, the transfer function delivers them to within a few percent for free, and is the pragmatic choice.} \new{Where it consumes the field -- which is the entire premise of map-level inference -- only a generative correction supplies what is missing, and on this evidence the flow-matching model supplies almost all of it.}

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis set out to answer a practical question at the heart of map-level, simulation-based cosmological inference: can the small-scale accuracy of an expensive reference $N$-body lightcone be restored onto a cheap particle-mesh lightcone, well enough for the statistics such analyses actually consume? The answer, on held-out cosmologies spanning the full CosmoGridV1 $w$CDM grid, is yes -- with the qualification that \emph{which} correction suffices depends on \emph{which} \jozef{why is 'which' highlighted? I would just remove all of these underlined words } \fixed{cause found: \texttt{ulem} was loaded without \texttt{normalem}, which silently redefined \texttt{\textbackslash emph} to underline. Fixed at the package line, so all 48 underlines went away at once.}statistic must be right.

The foundation was infrastructural. By reconstructing each CosmoGridV1 cosmology's exact linear input -- transfer function, $\sigma_8$, and radiation density, massive neutrinos included -- and letting \textsc{PkdGrav3} generate one backscaled initial condition consumed byte-identically by both solvers (Sec.~\ref{sec:ic_generation}), and by embedding a shell builder into Disco-DJ's time integration that reproduces \textsc{PkdGrav3}'s moving-lightcone crossing condition on CosmoGridV1's radial shell grid within constant memory (Sec.~\ref{sec:shell_builder}), the learning problem was reduced to its irreducible core: paired full-sky lightcones differing only through the force solver. Every result in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} rests on this controlled setup.

On these pairs, three corrections were built (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction-pipelines}), sharing one measured insight -- Disco-DJ's small-scale deficit is primarily one of \emph{amplitude}, its phases remaining correlated with the truth deep into the nonlinear regime -- and one shared guarantee, the high-pass residual formulation that pins the already-correct large scales to the fast simulation exactly. \new{The deterministic transfer-function pipeline holds shell and weak-lensing convergence spectra to within half a percent of the reference across the band it acts on, at essentially zero inference cost and with the tightest cosmology-to-cosmology scatter of the three; where an analysis consumes two-point statistics it remains the pragmatic choice, being competitive on convergence variance (\mbox{$0.967$} against the flow's \mbox{$0.973$}) at a small fraction of the cost.} \new{Getting there required correcting one of its own assumptions: the deficit sets in at a fixed comoving scale, as designed for, but that scale is \mbox{$17\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} rather than the \mbox{$5\,\mathrm{Mpc}/h$} originally assumed, and the difference is worth up to \mbox{$23$} percentage points of repaired power (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-transfer}).} \new{The same measurement applies to the two generative pipelines, which now high-pass at that per-shell scale rather than at a single angular cutoff -- a change that lifted the flow's recovered excess kurtosis from \mbox{$0.92$} to \mbox{$0.98$} of the reference.} What it cannot do -- by construction, not by failure -- is generate the one-point non-Gaussianity of the missing structure. The two generative pipelines close exactly that gap: both the flow-matching U-Net and the EDM diffusion model restore skewness and excess kurtosis at shell and convergence level on cosmologies they never saw, \new{the flow most completely -- recovering the convergence excess kurtosis to \mbox{$0.979 \pm 0.037$} of the reference, against \mbox{$0.537$} uncorrected -- and with the tightest cosmology-to-cosmology scatter, the diffusion consistently further short on every moment. Their convergence spectra differ over most of the band, the flow leading by three to five percentage points through the intermediate multipoles, the two converging near \mbox{$0.90$} only at the \mbox{$\ell = 1500$} band limit where both are resolution-bound rather than correction-bound.} That a 32-step stochastic sampler, evaluated on thousands of overlapping tiles sharing a single global noise field, reassembles into consistent full-sky maps also validates the overlap-tile machinery itself as a general route for applying patch-based generative models to the sphere.

Several limitations bound these conclusions and set the agenda. All three pipelines were evaluated at $N_{\mathrm{side}}=512$, with the correction band capped at the matching $\ell_{\max}=1500$ \jozef{I saw 1024 in the plot titles and also lmax of 3000 is too high for nside=512} \fixed{both correct. All three pipelines are now scored on one footing, $N_{\rm side}=512$ with $\ell_{\max}=1500=3N_{\rm side}-1$; the \mbox{$\kappa$} maps are now built at the same \mbox{$N_{\rm side}=512$}, \mbox{$\ell\le1500$} as the shells that feed them -- building them finer, as an earlier version did at \mbox{$N_{\rm side}=1024$} and \mbox{$\ell\le2048$}, only upsampled the input and produced a spurious collapse past the correction band.} -- a common footing chosen so that the comparison measures the corrections rather than the resolutions they were handed (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-protocol}). This bounds the results from both sides. The learned models have never been asked to correct the $\ell = 1500$--$3000$ band at all, so nothing here shows they generalise to it; and the transfer function was scored with its correction band closed on 13 of the 69 shells -- those with no measurable deficit -- so the band limit costs it little, though the native-resolution behaviour out to $\ell = 3000$ remains untested here. Lifting both to the native resolution -- retraining the patch models on $N_{\mathrm{side}}=2048$ tiles, which the patch machinery already supports -- is the most direct extension, and is what would turn the present ordering into a statement about the methods at full resolution. The corrected fields are continuous overdensities, not integer count maps, which downstream weak-lensing statistics tolerate but a consumer requiring per-pixel count validity would not. The corrections were validated on matter shells and derived convergence maps at fixed total neutrino mass, without baryonic feedback -- which operates on precisely the scales corrected here and would need its own treatment \cite{schneider_matter_2015, fluri_full_2022}. And each cosmology entered with a single realisation, so realisation-to-realisation generalisation at fixed cosmology remains to be quantified.

The direction, however, is clear. A Disco-DJ lightcone costs minutes where the reference costs node-hours, and the corrections add almost nothing to that -- so corrected fast lightcones can populate the simulation demand of deep-learning analyses of current surveys \cite{thomsen_dark_2026, fluri_full_2022} at a small fraction of the reference suite's cost, reserving full $N$-body runs for validation rather than production. The longer-term prospect is tighter still: Disco-DJ is differentiable end-to-end \cite{list_disco-dj_2026}, and a differentiable corrected forward model -- from cosmological parameters through the corrected lightcone to the map-level statistic -- would put gradient-based inference within reach of the same pipeline.
